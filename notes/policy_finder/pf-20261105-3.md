## Explorations

1. **What do the two reference managers score, and how do the artificial humans react to a punishment?** Looked at `plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/per_round.parquet` and `aggregates.csv` with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-3/ref_response.py. `zero` scores a mean valid payoff of 24.55 per round (about 589 over 24 rounds), `humanlike` only 22.94 (about 551): humanlike spends a mean punishment of ~2.4 per player-round, which costs the mean payoff 2 x 2.4 = 4.8 per round, and buys contributions of ~13 against zero's decaying 11.6 -> 5.5 (a gain of about 0.6 x 5 = 3). Under zero, contributions fall ~0.4 per round and the change is strongly mean-reverting on the own level (c=0: +1.45 next round; c=20: -1.72). Under humanlike, next-round change rises with punishment for low contributors: c in (4,8] goes from -0.06 (p=0) to +1.8 (p 4-7) and +2.6 (p 7-10), then saturates (~3 at p>10); c in (0,4] up to +4. For contributors above 12, punishment goes with a fall (-1 to -2.9), but that may be confounded (humanlike punishes high contributors after drops). OLS dc ~ p + c + gmean: 0.07 per punishment point, own c -0.29, group mean +0.26. So a punishment point buys ~0.3 contribution next round from a low contributor, with diminishing returns beyond ~8; a point costs the group 2 payoff, a contributed point returns 0.6, so a punishment must raise the sum of future contributions by > 3.3 per point to pay. It can only pay if the effect persists and spreads; next I want the persistence and the spillover to the others, and the pilots' random rules as a cleaner (exogenous) look.
2. **Do the pilot humans respond to punishment, is it lasting, and does it spread?** Looked at the pilot CSV (contribution, punishment, player_no_input, by experiment_name) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-3/pilot_response.py. Under the random rules (`random_1`, 16 rounds, punishment more exogenous) contributions rise 10.6 -> 15.3 by round 11 while punishment falls 7.0 -> 1.8, and dip at the last rounds (14.6 at round 15). Regressing c at t+k on own p_t, others' mean p_t, c_t, group mean and rounds to the end: own punishment +0.13 (k=1, se 0.025), +0.22 (k=2), +0.28 (k=5) -- the effect grows rather than fades; others' punishment +0.12 (k=1) to +0.34 (k=5), so punishing one player also lifts the other three. Under human managers the own-punishment coefficient is ~0 (they punish reactively, confounded). The cell table is sharp: under random rules, c<=4 gains +0.15 at p=0, +2.4 at p 3-6, +4.0 at p 6-10, +7.1 at p>10; c 9-14 gains ~+0.2-1 whatever p; under human managers a full contributor (c=20) who is punished drops by 5-14 points. So: punish only low contributors, never the high ones; one punishment point seems to buy ~0.3 own + ~0.3 spread contribution per later round (about 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.36 payoff per remaining round against a cost of 2), so it pays only with ~6+ rounds left -- punishing late is waste. Next: the same persistence on the sim's artificial humans, and the end-game there.
3. **Is the response as lasting on the artificial humans, and what does the zero sim's decay look like?** Looked at the reference parquet (humanlike run) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-3/ref_persistence.py. On the clones the linear effects are weaker than in the pilots but they do not fade: own punishment +0.054 at k=1, +0.062 at k=8; others' mean punishment +0.067 (k=1) rising to +0.158 (k=8). Per punishment point that is about 0.06 own + 0.05..0.16/3*3 spread = 0.12-0.22 contribution in every later round. For c<=8, own +0.08-0.09; for c>=14 own -0.06 at k=1 (punishing high contributors backfires a bit). Under zero, the share of zero contributors grows 5% -> 28% and the share >=15 falls 36% -> 13%: the decline is free riders spreading; player means are very spread (10th pct 1.9, 90th 15.7). Timeouts: 5.6% in round 0, ~1.8% later. So an early point of punishment, worth ~0.2 contribution x 0.6 x ~15-20 remaining rounds = ~2 payoff, roughly breaks even against its cost of 2 on average -- the gain must come from where it is most efficient, and it shrinks linearly with the rounds left. Next: is there a nonlinearity (small punishments of moderate contributors) that makes some punishments much cheaper per unit of effect?
4. **Which punishment sizes are efficient for which contributors?** Looked at the humanlike clones with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-3/ref_small_p.py: per contribution bin, dummies for the punishment level against p=0, controlling c, previous c, group mean, others' punishment and round. Moderate contributors (c 7-15) answer a small punishment: p=2-3 gives +0.3..+0.6 next round and +0.25..+0.6 four rounds later (~0.2 per point, lasting); the lowest (c<=3) ignore 1-3 points and need big ones (p 9-12: +1.3, p>=13: +3.1, still +3.0 at k=4, ~0.2 per point); c 16-20 barely respond and large punishments (13+) cost them -0.7..-0.95. So effect per point is roughly ~0.2 for anyone below ~15 if the dose fits the shortfall, and none or negative above ~15. That points to punishment proportional to the shortfall below a threshold in the low-to-mid teens, capped, front-loaded, and stopping well before the end because the payback needs many remaining rounds.
5. **Drafted the rule.** `p_i = min(cap, slope * max(0, thr - c_i)) * w(t)`, with `w(t) = ((H - t)/H)^taper` for t < H and 0 from round H on. thr: the contribution below which a player is punished; slope: punishment per point of shortfall; cap: largest punishment (no more spent on hopeless free riders and avoid the backlash of huge punishments); H: the round from which the manager stops punishing (the payback of a punishment scales with the rounds left, so late punishment is pure cost); taper: how quickly punishment strength fades toward H (0 flat; 1 proportional to the rounds left of the horizon). Five parameters, the maximum. The rule can reach the zero manager (small slope floors to 0), so the sweep can tell if punishing pays at all on these clones.
6. **Validated and checked the rule.** `validate-rule` passes (256 distinct design points). scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-3/check_rule.py runs the code on a hand-made history (c = 0, 5, 12, 20; player 2 timed out) with thr 14, slope 1, cap 8, horizon 16, taper 1: round 0 gives [8, 0, 2, 0], round 8 [4, 0, 1, 0], round 15 onward nothing, as intended (the timed-out player gets 0, and the gate `t < horizon` keeps taper 0 from punishing past the horizon, since 0**0 = 1). An open question I cannot settle here: whether the clones' gain per point (~0.2 per later round) beats its cost of 2 at all; the sweep's low-slope / short-horizon corner is close to `zero`, so it can tell.

## Key findings

1. Never punishing beats the human-like manager on the clones: mean valid payoff 24.55 vs 22.94 per round; humanlike spends ~2.4 punishment per player-round (4.8 payoff per round) to keep contributions at ~13 rather than zero's 11.6 -> 5.5 decay (exploration 1).
2. A punishment point costs the group 2 payoff points and a contributed point returns 0.6, so a point must buy more than 3.3 contribution points over the rest of the game (exploration 1).
3. The effect lasts: on the clones own punishment adds +0.054 at k=1 and +0.062 at k=8 rounds later, others' punishment +0.067 rising to +0.158 (spillover to the group); in the random-rule pilots own +0.13 -> +0.28 and others' +0.12 -> +0.34 (explorations 2, 3). That makes ~0.2 contribution per point per later round, which pays only with many rounds left.
4. Who to punish: contributors below ~15 respond about 0.2 per point if the dose fits (c 7-15 respond to 2-3 points, c<=3 only to 9+); c 16-20 do not respond and big punishments cut them by up to ~1 point on the clones and by 5-14 points in the human-managed pilots (explorations 2, 4).
5. Under zero the decline is free riding spreading: zero contributors go from 5% to 28%, high contributors (>=15) from 36% to 13% (exploration 3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager spends punishment only where and when it pays back: on players below a contribution standard, in proportion to their shortfall, capped, mostly early in the game, and not at all in the last rounds, when the rounds left are too few to repay the double cost. Punishing high contributors, punishing beyond what changes behaviour, and late punishment are pure loss. The rule: `p_i = min(cap, slope * max(0, thr - c_i)) * ((horizon - t)/horizon)^taper` for t < horizon, else 0.
- `thr` (int, [6, 20]): the contribution standard; I expect 10-15, where findings 4 put the edge of responsiveness.
- `slope` (float, [0.15, 4], log): punishment per point of shortfall; I expect 0.4-1.5 (2-3 points for a moderate shortfall); the low end lets the sweep approach the zero manager.
- `cap` (float, [2, 30], log): the largest punishment; I expect 5-12 so free riders get a dose that works (finding 4) without the large tail humanlike spends.
- `horizon` (int, [6, 23]): the round from which punishment stops; I expect 12-20 (finding 3: payback needs ~10+ rounds).
- `taper` (float, [0, 2]): how fast strength fades toward the horizon; I expect 0.5-1.5, matching a value per point that is proportional to rounds left.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 2 say indiscriminate or heavy punishment loses, so the rule punishes only shortfalls (thr) and caps the dose (cap); finding 4 sets the standard below the non-responsive high contributors and grounds a shortfall-proportional dose (slope), since low contributors need bigger doses than moderate ones. Finding 3 (lasting ~0.2 per point per later round) makes the value of a point proportional to the rounds left, hence horizon and taper; finding 5 says the loss under zero comes from free riding spreading, which early punishment of the low contributors targets directly, with the spillover to the others as a bonus. It would be contradicted if the sweep's best points sat at the low-slope / short-horizon corner (punishment never pays on these clones) or at thr 20 (high contributors also respond). I did not model reactions to a player's own trend (forgiving improvers), relative-to-group standards, or timeout history: the data did not show a clear lever there and the parameter budget (5) is better spent on the dose and the timing; the clones' behaviour past round 16 (beyond their training) is also unknown, which is one more reason to let the horizon be swept.
