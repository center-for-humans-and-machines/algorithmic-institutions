# pf-20261112-6

## Explorations

1. **What does the baseline look like on the score?** Looked at the reference sims (per_round.parquet) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-6/ref_score.py, scoring with `pool_scores.against_anchor` (group sum of
   1.6c - p per round, averaged over rounds). Found `zero` (never punishes) leads `ah` by 13.97 (se 1.44):
   own pool 60.5 vs 46.5, mostly because its group grows to 4.57 members vs 3.43 (ref_sims.py: by round 4
   the zero group already holds 4.9 of 8). Per-member contributions are actually lower under zero (8.1 vs 9.0)
   and ah punishes ~1.8/player-round. So the score is a *total* pool, and group size is the dominant lever;
   next I want to know what the switching model reacts to and how contributions respond to punishment.
2. **How do the simulated players respond to punishment?** Looked at the reference sims with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-6/sim_response.py (next-round contribution change of players who stay,
   by own contribution bin x punishment bin; switching at decision rounds by punishment bin). Found that in
   ah-run groups a 0-contributor punished 10-30 raises next round by +3.1 vs +1.7 unpunished (+1.4), a 0-5
   contributor +2.6 vs +0.7; but high contributors (15-20) punished drop by -2.2 to -3.7 vs -1.4. Switching:
   at a decision round P(leave) is 0.26 unpunished, 0.33 at 1-2, 0.42 at 3-5, 0.51 at 6-10, 0.66 at 10+.
   Zero groups lose 0.24 per decision; ah groups lose 0.46 at round 3 (vs 0.31 for zero).
   So punishment buys at most ~1.4 contribution points (=2.2 pool points/round) from a free rider at a price
   of ~15 pool points that round plus a large exodus risk. That looks bad before the last switch and only
   possibly worth it afterwards. Next: check what the switching responds to over the block (lags), and the
   human data.
3. **What do the switches respond to, and what do the linear human models say?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-6/switch_lags.py on the reference sims (linear probability model of
   leaving at rounds 7-19 in ah groups) and linear_coefs.py on the linear baselines fitted to the human data
   (artifacts/baselines/contribution_ridge_best, does_switch_multinomial_best; the GNN weights of the
   simulated stack are not in this container, so I cannot simulate). Sims: leaving rises 0.008/pt of own
   punishment in the decision round and the one before, 0.0055 two rounds before, only 0.0023 three rounds
   before (the first round of the 4-round block); and it falls steeply with the own-minus-other group payoff
   gap (0.48 when 5+ points behind, 0.14 when 5+ ahead), the same under zero and ah. Human switch model:
   +0.245 logit per point of the group's mean punishment over the stay, -0.08 per point of own payoff.
   Human contribution model: +0.34 next-round contribution per point of group-mean punishment, but -0.146
   via the payoff gap to the other group and -0.135 via the per-capita pool, so a net of only ~+0.1 per point
   that costs a full pool point per member. So punishment is roughly a wash on contributions and a clear loss
   on membership; any punishment worth keeping is (a) small, (b) aimed at free riders, (c) placed where the
   switch decision forgets it most: the first round of a block or after the last switch (rounds 20-22).
   Round 23 punishment can buy nothing. Next: human data, to see whether low contributors respond.
4. **Do humans tell the same story?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-6/human.py on the 50 games (one copy
   each). Low contributors do respond: a 0-contributor punished 8-30 raises next round by +3.9 vs +1.0
   unpunished, a 1-5 contributor +3.1 vs +0.1; high contributors (10-20) punished 8-30 drop by -5.7 vs -1.5.
   But at the block level, each point of the group's mean punishment over the 4-round block (relative to
   the other group) costs 0.37 members at the decision, while each point of mean contribution lead gains only
   0.09: punishment would have to buy ~4 contribution points per punishment point to break even on members.
   Human managers punished most in rounds 0-3 (3.1-3.7/player) and ~1.0-1.3 late. So the hypothesis firms
   up: never punish cooperators; punish free riders only in proportion to how far they fall short, only
   where the exodus price is low (after the last switch, rounds 20-22, and perhaps the first round(s) of a
   block), and never in round 23. Since zero itself leads ah by ~14, the rule must be able to collapse to
   zero so the sweep can find out whether any punishment pays.
5. **Drafted the rule.** Four parameters: `thr` (free-riding threshold), `k` (points per point of
   shortfall below thr), `t_start` (first round punishment is allowed), `w` (before round 20, punish only in
   the first w rounds of each 4-round block, far from the next switch decision). Round 23 is hard-wired to
   zero (nothing left to buy) and timed-out players are skipped. Zero is nested: t_start = 23, or k*thr < 1
   (floored away). validate-rule passed. With k log-uniform on [0.05, 5] the design check
   (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-6/design_check.py) showed 84 of 256 points never punish, which is a
   third of the budget spent on one policy; I raised k's floor to 0.15, which leaves 41 zero-equivalent points
   (still a clear reference plateau) and 28 points that punish only in rounds 20-22. Revalidated: 256
   distinct points.

## Key findings

1. The score is a total pool, so membership dominates: `zero` leads `ah` by 13.97 (se 1.44), with 4.57 vs
   3.43 members, although its members contribute less (8.1 vs 9.0) (exploration 1).
2. In the sims, leaving at a decision rises from 0.26 unpunished to 0.66 when punished 10+, and falls from
   0.48 to 0.14 as the group's payoff goes from 5+ behind to 5+ ahead of the other group; punishment in the
   first round of a block weighs about a third as much as in the last two rounds (0.0023 vs 0.008 per point)
   (explorations 2, 3).
3. In humans, a point of block-mean punishment lead costs 0.37 members at the decision, while a point of
   contribution lead gains 0.09 (exploration 4).
4. Free riders do respond to punishment (sims: a 0-contributor +3.1 vs +1.7 next round when punished 10+;
   humans +3.9 vs +1.0), but punished high contributors cut back (sims -3.7 vs -1.4; humans -5.7 vs -1.5)
   (explorations 2, 4).
5. The human-fitted linear contribution model gives +0.34 per point of group-mean punishment, offset by
   -0.146 (payoff gap) and -0.135 (per-capita pool), so a net of ~+0.1 contribution per pool point spent
   (exploration 3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here is mostly a non-punisher: punishment lowers its group's payoff relative to the other
group, which drives members out and toward `ah`'s group, and every member that moves counts twice in the
lead. The only punishment that can pay is small, aimed solely at free riders (where it raises contributions
and where losing the player costs little pool), proportional to how far they fall short, and placed where
the switching decisions see it least: after the last switch (rounds 20-22) and possibly in the first
round(s) of a block. Never in round 23. The rule: punishment = k * max(thr - c, 0) for valid members, in
rounds t_start <= t < 23, and before round 20 only when t mod 4 < w.
- `thr`, free-riding threshold in contribution points, uniform [0.5, 12]; I expect 1-5 (only near-zero
  contributors; up to 12 to let the sweep test punishing below-average contributors).
- `k`, punishment per point of shortfall, log-uniform [0.15, 5]; I expect small values (k*thr of 1-6
  points for a zero contributor), or the zero-equivalent corner k*thr < 1.
- `t_start`, int [0, 23]; I expect 16-21 (late, mostly after the last switch); 23 is exactly `zero`.
- `w`, int [1, 4]; I expect 1 (first round of a block only) if early punishment is used at all.

### Justification

Findings 1-3 say membership is the main lever and punishment is its main enemy, so the rule never touches
cooperators and can collapse to zero (t_start = 23, or k*thr < 1), which is the known 14-point baseline the
sweep can compare against. Finding 4 justifies the threshold and the shortfall scaling: free riders are the
only players whose response is positive, and cooperators punished respond negatively. Finding 2's lag
pattern justifies `w` (first-of-block rounds are weighted least by the switch decision) and finding 3 plus
the absence of any switch after round 19 justify `t_start` (after round 20 punishment cannot cost members).
Finding 5 is why I expect k small: even with no exodus, punishment roughly breaks even on contributions,
so only a few rounds of a free rider's improved contribution can repay it. Contradicting evidence would be
a sweep peak at large k with early t_start and w = 4 (punishment pays despite exodus), or a flat landscape
in which the zero-equivalent points score as well as any other (punishment never pays, and the rule
reduces to zero). Not modelled: targeting by a player's history or tenure (recent joiners), thresholds
relative to the group mean, and reacting to the other group's state; the observational evidence for these
is weak and confounded, and with no access to the simulated stack I preferred few, interpretable
parameters.
