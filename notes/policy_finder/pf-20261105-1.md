# pf-20261105-1

## Explorations

1. **What the game and the reference sims say at a glance.** Read docs/reports/basics_exp1.md and
   plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/aggregates.csv. Under `zero` the mean contribution decays from
   11.6 (round 0) to 5.5 (round 23) while the payoff falls from 26.6 to 23.0; under `humanlike`
   contributions rise to ~13.2 and stay flat, punishment ~2-3 per player per round, and the payoff is
   21.1 rising to ~23.8. So `zero` is far ahead early and humanlike only catches up after round ~13.
   Arithmetic: one point of punishment costs the group 2 points (player + pool), one point of
   contribution earns the group 0.6. A punishment point must buy >= 3.3 contribution-points over the
   rest of the game to pay. No exp1 artificial-human weights are in artifacts/, so I cannot
   simulate locally; I will rely on the data. Next: how the pilots respond to punishment.

2. **How pilot humans respond to punishment.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-1/explore_pilots.py
   and response_pilots.py on experiments/pilot_random1_player_round_slim.csv (valid rows, next-round
   change dc). Low contributors (c 0-4) change by +0.9 unpunished, +2.0 at p 3-5, +3.8 at p 6-10,
   +6.4 at p 11-30. Mid (5-9): +0.1 / +0.8 / +2.2 / +4.1. Contributors at 10-14 barely react (+0.5),
   and full contributors (20) who get punished *drop* by 4-12 (small n, but consistent). Unpunished
   contributors at 10-19 drift down ~1.1-1.3 per round. OLS of dc on p and min(p,5): slope 0.18 per
   point beyond 5, ~0.05 per point below 5 -> the first-round response is well under 1 contribution
   point per punishment point. Whether punishment pays thus hinges on how long the gain persists
   (and spillovers to others). Next: the reference sims, per player, same table, and persistence.
3. **Scoring the references and the sims' response to punishment.** response_sims.py on the
   reference per_round.parquet. Score (sum over rounds of the valid players' mean payoff):
   zero 589.3 (se 2.1), humanlike 550.6 (se 3.4). Timeouts ~1.9% under both. Humanlike punishes by
   contribution: 6.1 at c 0-4, 4.6 at 5-9, 2.9 at 10-14, 1.1 at 15-19, 0.3 even at c = 20. The
   sims echo the pilots: punished low contributors rise (c 0-4, p 11-30: +4.8 next round vs +0.8),
   punished full contributors fall (c 20, p 11-30: -2.7 vs -0.75), and the binned gains *grow* to
   +5 rounds rather than fade. Accounting: score = sum_t (0.6*mean c - 2*mean p) + const. Humanlike's
   contribution lead over zero sums to ~127 player-rounds (gain ~76) while its punishment costs
   ~2*55 = ~110, net ~-35, which matches the -39 gap. So humanlike loses because it pays too much
   per contribution point, not because punishment is useless. Next: is it the amount or the mere
   fact of being punished that moves players?

4. **Regressions and the size of a punishment.** regress.py (c_{t+k} on c, others' mean, p, 1[p>0],
   round, split c<10 / c>=10) and small_p.py (dc by exact p). In the humanlike sim, for c<10:
   +1 round: 0.29*1[p>0] + 0.084*p; +5 rounds: 0.44 + 0.084*p. In random_1 pilots (random rule
   managers, less selection): 0.62 + 0.30p at +1, 1.58 + 0.50p at +5 for c<10, and ~0.8 + 0.01p for
   c>=10. The others' mean enters at 0.18-0.38 (conformity). By exact p in bins, p=1 already gives
   ~70% of the gain of p=5 for c 5-14 -- a step at p>0 then a shallow slope. But the binned table
   mixes in selection (the punished are the lower ones within a bin) and the punished get punished
   again (future 5-round punishment 11.8 if p=0 vs 17-24 if p=1-3). Next: condition on exact c and
   on the others' level.

5. **Exact contribution and the group's level.** exact_c.py: next-round change at c in
   {0,5,10,15,18,20} by p, split by others' mean (0-7, 8-13, 14-20). The conformity effect is
   big: at c = 10 the change is -1.8 if others average <=7, -0.5 if 8-13, +0.5 if 14-20. Within a
   cell the punishment effect is smaller than the binned tables said: c=10 / others 8-13: +0.6 at
   p=1, +1.0 at p=2, +1.3 at p=3, then flat to p=8; c=5: about +0.4 at p 2-3; c=0: hardly anything
   below p=5, +1.5 at p=8; c=15: +0.3-0.7 at p 1-3 when others are high, negative when they are mid;
   c>=18: punishment only hurts (-0.1 to -1.4). So: mid contributors (5-14) respond to a small
   nudge, zero contributors need a larger punishment, near-full contributors must not be touched.
   Persistence via conformity (own autoregression 0.77-0.84 plus 0.2-0.3 from others) suggests a
   gain lasts many rounds, which is what can make a 2-3 point nudge pay (needs >= 3.3 contribution
   points summed over the future per punishment point). The value of punishing falls with the rounds
   left, and a final-round punishment is pure loss. Next: draft the rule.
6. **Drafted the rule.** configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261105-1.yml, four params: punish a player
   only if this round's contribution is below an absolute `target` (int), by a flat `base` (the
   step response of entry 4) plus `slope` per point of shortfall (zero contributors need more, entry
   5), and only up to round `last_round` (the endgame argument of entry 5). Players at or above the
   target -- and so always full contributors -- are never touched (entry 5: punishing c >= 18
   backfires). I kept the target absolute rather than relative to the group: a uniformly low group
   is exactly the decaying group under `zero` that needs pushing, and conformity (entry 5) carries a
   raised player's gain to the others anyway. First ranges: target [4, 20], base [0, 6], slope
   [0, 1], last_round [12, 23]. validate-rule passes (256 distinct points).

7. **A crude toy check of the ranges.** toy_sim.py: a linear stand-in (next c on own c, others'
   mean, 1[p>0] by c band, p, round; resid sd 3.5) fitted to both reference runs, playing the rule
   on a grid. It does *not* reproduce the decay under `zero` (scores zero at 610 vs the real 589:
   own 0.77 + others 0.21 leaves almost no drift), so it misses the very mechanism punishment works
   on, and in it no punishment pays: score falls steeply with base and slope (e.g. target 12, base 2,
   slope 0: -14 to -29; target 16, base 0, slope 0.5: -44 to -74), and more gently with an early
   last_round. I do not trust its sign, but it does say the cost grows fast with the amount, so the
   good region, if any, sits at small amounts, and the sweep needs dense coverage there and a way to
   approach `zero` (target low or base and slope near 0). Narrowed base to [0, 5] and slope to
   [0, 0.6]: at target 16 a zero contributor then gets at most 5 + 9.6 ~ 15, still above the p ~ 8
   where entry 5 saw zero contributors respond. Kept target and last_round as they were.
8. **Final validation.** Re-ran validate-rule with the narrowed ranges: passes, 256 distinct design
   points.

## Key findings

1. Punishment costs the score 2 points per point dealt, and a contribution point earns 0.6, so a punishment point has to bring at least 3.3 extra contribution-points over the rest of the game to pay (exploration 1).
2. Never punishing (`zero`) scores 589.3; the human-like manager scores 550.6. Its contributions hold at ~13 while zero's decay from 11.6 to 5.5, but at ~2-3 points per player-round it pays ~110 for a gain of ~76 (exploration 3).
3. The human-like manager also punishes full contributors (0.3 per round at c = 20, 1.1 at c 15-19), and punishing players at c >= 18 only lowers their next contribution, by 0.1-1.4 more than leaving them alone (explorations 3 and 5).
4. The response is a step followed by a shallow slope. Being punished at all is worth about 0.3-0.6 next-round points (sim), plus ~0.08 per point. Holding exact c and the others' level fixed, mid contributors gain +0.6 to +1.3 at p = 1-3 and little more up to p = 8 (explorations 4 and 5).
5. Zero contributors hardly move below p ~ 5, but they rise by ~1.5 at p = 8 (exploration 5).
6. Conformity is strong. At c = 10 the next-round change is -1.8, -0.5 or +0.5 as the others average <= 7, 8-13 or 14-20, and the others' mean has a weight of 0.2-0.4 in every regression. A gain on one player spreads and persists, which is the only way a punishment can pay (explorations 4 and 5).
7. A crude linear stand-in that cannot reproduce zero's decay finds no punishment that pays, with costs rising steeply with the amount. If punishment pays at all in the real sims, it pays at small amounts (exploration 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager is cheap and targeted. It deals small punishments only to players below a contribution standard and leaves those at or above it alone, so full contributors are never hit. The flat part of the punishment buys the step response of being punished at all (finding 4). A small extra per missing point reaches the zero contributors, who respond only to larger punishments (finding 5). The aim is to stop the group's decay early, because conformity then holds contributions up at little ongoing cost (finding 6). It stops punishing near the end, when too few rounds remain for a gain to repay the cost (finding 1). Rule: `punishment = [t <= last_round] * [c < target] * (base + slope * (target - c))`.
- `target` (int, [4, 20]): the standard. A player below it is punished. I expect 10-16: above the level where zero's groups settle, below the full contributors who react badly.
- `base` (float, [0, 5]): the flat nudge for being below the standard. I expect 1-3, where most of the step response is already bought (finding 4).
- `slope` (float, [0, 0.6]): the extra points per point of shortfall. I expect 0.1-0.4. A zero contributor then gets about 5-8 points, where finding 5 sees a response.
- `last_round` (int, [12, 23]): the last round with punishment. I expect 18-22: the final round surely does not pay (no future), and the rounds just before it barely do.

### Justification

- **Shape of the rule.** Findings 1 and 2 show that punishment only pays when it is cheap. Humanlike lost about 39 points by paying too much per contribution point.
- **`target`.** Finding 3 is why players at or above the standard are never punished. Its upper bound of 20 is a standard that full contributors always meet. Its lower bound of 4 lets the sweep approach `zero`, which finding 7 says may be the honest optimum.
- **`base` and `slope`.** Findings 4 and 5 give the step-plus-slope form and both parameters. Both ranges include 0, so the sweep can drop either one.
- **`last_round`.** It follows from finding 1 applied to the rounds left.
- **What would contradict the hypothesis.** The sweep could peak at base 0, slope 0 or target 4, meaning no punishment pays against these artificial humans. Or it could peak at the top of `base` or `slope`, meaning large punishments, which would go against findings 4 and 7. Either result is informative.
- **What I did not model, and why.**
  - Targets relative to the group, because finding 6 suggests conformity already spreads the effect.
  - Punishing drops in a player's own contribution, because each player has a long and noisy history.
  - Timeouts, because a timed-out player cannot be punished and the rate is ~2%.
  - Punishment history (e.g. escalating for repeat offenders), because with at most 5 parameters I kept the four with the clearest grounding.
- **Caveat.** The response estimates are correlational. Selection by the human-like manager, and repeated punishment of the same players, inflate them.
