## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other in the reference sims, on the score's own metric?** Looked at `plots/simulation/25_LEVIN_run1_ah_zero_pairings_batched/per_round.parquet` with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/ref_scores.py (the repo's `against_anchor`, total pool 1.6*sum(c)-sum(p) per group). Found `zero` (never punishes) leads `ah` by +13.97 pool points per round (60.5 vs 46.5), with mean group size 4.57 vs 3.43. Most of the lead comes from membership: after the first switch at round 4, the zero group jumps to ~4.9 members and a 65 pool, while the ah group drops to ~3.1. Per member, the ah group contributes more (9.3 vs 8.3 mean c), and in ah_vs_ah the pool grows from 40 to 63 over the game, while in zero_vs_zero it falls from 56 to 48. So punishment does build contributions over time, but it costs members and pays for itself in the pool. `common_good` in the parquet is per capita; the score uses the total. Next: what drives the switch decision in the artificial humans, and how do contributions respond to punishment?

2. **What makes artificial humans switch groups?** Looked at the reference sims per player and 4-round block with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/switch_sims.py (switch = group differs in the next block). Found a 27.5% switch rate per decision. It climbs steeply with the punishment a player received in the block: 23.6% at 0 mean punishment, 34% at 1-3, 46% at 3-6, 57% at 6-10 and 68% above 10. A logit puts +0.10 log-odds on each point of own mean punishment. The other group's mean punishment repels (-0.19 per point), and the other group's mean contribution attracts (+0.15 per point). Players in the group with the lower mean payoff leave at 46-59%, against 15% in the better-paying group. So punishment drives away the very players it hits, and visible punishment also keeps the other group's players from coming over. Next: does punishment buy contributions that could pay for this?

3. **Does punishment raise contributions in the sims?** Looked at next-round contribution against this round's punishment for players who stay, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/contrib_response_sims.py. Found an OLS coefficient of +0.059 contribution per point of punishment (controlling for own c, group mean, round), which persists at about +0.06 two and three rounds on. Peer following is strong: +0.34 on the group mean. Among zero contributors, a punishment of 5-10 gives +3.0 next round against +1.6 unpunished. So one point of punishment costs 1 pool point now and returns about 1.6*0.06 = 0.1 pool points per later round, plus some herding spillover. It breaks even only over roughly 10 or more remaining rounds and ignores the switching cost. That fits `zero` beating `ah` by 14. Next: does it matter when within a block the punishment falls (for switching), and does punishing in the last block, where no switch follows, make sense?

4. **Does it matter when in a block the punishment falls?** Looked at the switch decision against the punishment received in each of the block's four rounds (ah-managed games) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/switch_timing.py. Found log-odds per point of +0.016-0.020 for the block's first round, rising to +0.037-0.042 for its last round, the one just before the decision. Punishment from the previous block has no positive carry-over (-0.03, likely selection). So recency matters: punishment right before a switch costs about twice as many members as punishment just after one. The last block (rounds 20-23) has no switch after it, so its punishment costs no members, and punishment in round 23 buys nothing at all. Next: check that the human data tells the same story.

5. **Human data: same picture?** Looked at the 50 deduplicated human games with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/human.py. Found next-round contribution +0.047 per point of punishment (sims +0.059) and peer following of +0.33. The switch rate rises with mean punishment received, from 19% at 0 to 45% at 3-6 and 67% above 10, just like the sims. Across games, each point of mean punishment a manager deals more than the rival manager goes with a -4.8 pool lead (corr -0.27). Human managers punish mostly low contributors (3.3 at c=0, 0.4 at c>15) and punish most in rounds 0-3 (3.4-3.7), the worst time given the first switch. So the artificial humans faithfully carry the "punishment drives people out" effect. My hypothesis: the best manager is close to `zero`. Any punishment should (a) hit only clear free riders, (b) avoid the rounds just before a switch decision, and (c) be concentrated in the final block, which has no exit, but not in its last round(s), where the contribution gain has no time to pay back. Next: draft a rule whose parameter space contains exact zero punishment, so the sweep can find it if nothing beats it.

6. **Drafted the rule and checked its design.** Rule: punishment = k * max(thr - c, 0), applied (a) in rounds 0-19 only in the first 4 - quiet rounds of each 4-round block, keeping the `quiet` rounds before each switch decision punishment-free, and (b) in the final block only in rounds that leave at least `min_left` rounds after them. It validates (256 distinct points). With scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-12/design_check.py I drew the Sobol design and applied each point to the contributions of the zero_vs_zero reference games. The median point spends 0.31 punishment per player-round, against ~1.8 for `ah`. 24% of points spend under 0.05, i.e. are effectively `zero` (thr < ~1, or quiet = 4 with min_left = 4). Each (quiet, min_left) cell holds 12-14 points. So the sweep covers the "do nothing" baseline I expect to be near-optimal and the light, well-timed punishment variants that might beat it, and the heavy end (up to 13.6) is there to confirm the trend. This is my final version; I am not changing it further.

## Key findings

1. In the reference sims, `zero` (never punishes) leads `ah` by +13.97 pool points per round (60.5 vs 46.5). Most of the lead comes from membership: mean group size is 4.57 vs 3.43, with the zero group jumping to ~4.9 after the first switch (exploration 1).
2. Punishment builds contributions only slowly. It is worth +0.059 next-round contribution per point in the sims and +0.047 in humans, so one point (cost 1 pool point) returns ~0.1 pool point per later round. Yet when both managers punish (ah_vs_ah), the pool climbs from 40 to 63 over the game, while it falls from 56 to 48 when neither does (zero_vs_zero) (explorations 1, 3, 5).
3. Punishment drives its target out of the group. The switch rate rises from 24% at 0 mean punishment to 46% at 3-6 and 68% above 10, at +0.10 log-odds per point in the sims, with the same gradient in humans (19% to 67%). The other group's punishment also deters newcomers (-0.19 log-odds per point) (explorations 2, 5).
4. Recency matters: punishment in the round just before a switch decision weighs about twice as much on switching as punishment in the block's first round (+0.04 vs +0.02 log-odds per point) (exploration 4).
5. In the human games, each point of mean punishment dealt above the rival manager goes with -4.8 pool lead. Human managers punish most in rounds 0-3 (3.4-3.7 per player), right before the first switch (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A manager maximises its lead mainly by keeping and attracting members. Every member adds 1.6*c to the pool, and a member who moves over also takes the same amount away from the rival, while `ah` punishes and so pushes its own players towards us. Punishment costs the pool directly, costs members, and repels the other group's players. Its contribution payback is too slow to cover that in most rounds. So the best manager is close to `zero`. Punishment can only pay where exits are impossible or least sensitive: on clear free riders, in the rounds just after a switch decision, and in the early rounds of the final block, which has no exit after it but still leaves time for a contribution response. It should never be used in the last round.

The rule: punishment = k * max(thr - c, 0), gated by timing.
- `thr` (float, [0, 12]): free-rider line. Members below it are punished in proportion to their shortfall. Near 0 means the `zero` manager. I expect the best values in 0-5, punishing only near-zero contributors.
- `k` (float, [0.2, 4], log): punishment per point of shortfall. I expect 0.5-2, since a zero contributor drawing 5-10 shows the clearest response.
- `quiet` (int, 0-4): the number of rounds before each switch decision (rounds 0-19) in which no one is punished. 4 means no punishment before round 20. I expect 3-4.
- `min_left` (int, 1-4): in the final block, punish in round t only if 23 - t >= min_left. 4 means no punishment in the final block. I expect 2-4.

### Justification

Finding 1, together with findings 3 and 5, makes membership the main lever and punishment the main way to lose it. That is why the design is centred on light punishment and contains exact zero (thr < 1, or quiet = 4 with min_left = 4; 24% of the design points). Finding 2's slow payback (~0.1 pool per later round per point) motivates `min_left`. It also motivates leaving round 23 always unpunished, because in that round punishment buys nothing. Finding 4 motivates `quiet`: if punishment is used at all before round 20, it belongs at the start of a block. Finding 3 suggests targeting only low contributors (`thr`, shortfall-proportional `k`), since punishing cooperators would push out the members who fill the pool. The ranges stop at the natural zero edges (thr = 0, quiet = 4, min_left = 4). A best point at those edges would mean "punish nothing" and would not be a cut-off range. thr goes up to 12 and k up to 4 so the sweep can confirm that heavy punishment loses.

What would contradict the hypothesis: a sweep whose best points have thr above ~6 or quiet of 0-1, i.e. punishment of moderate contributors right before switch decisions beating zero. That would mean the contribution and herding gains in the artificial humans outweigh the exits.

What I did not model:
- Punishing free riders deliberately to push them into `ah`'s group. Under herding (+0.33 on the group mean) this could raise our members' contributions and lower theirs, but it costs the pool directly and risks deterring newcomers. It can partly emerge from high thr with quiet = 0 anyway.
- Thresholds relative to the group mean, and reacting to the rival's punishment. Both would add parameters without evidence that they beat the simple gate.
- Timed-out players. The game charges them nothing anyway.
