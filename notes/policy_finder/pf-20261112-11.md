# pf-20261112-11

## Explorations

1. **How does never punishing (`zero`) do against `ah` on the score?** Looked at the reference sims
   (`per_round.parquet`, columns agent_group, contribution, punishment) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-11/ref_score.py, using the sweep's own scorer
   (`pool_scores.against_anchor`, pool = 1.6*sum(c) - sum(p) per group, not per capita).
   Found `zero` leads `ah` by 13.97 +- 1.44 pool points (own 60.5 vs 46.5), with 4.57 vs 3.43 mean
   members. Per round: in rounds 0-3 (equal sizes) zero's pool is 55 vs 42 for ah, because ah
   punishes ~3.5/player (≈14 off the pool) while getting only ~0.2-0.5 more contribution per
   player; after the first switch zero holds 4.9 vs 3.1 members and the lead is ~+20, settling to
   ~+12 with 4.6 vs 3.4. Contributions in ah's group run ~9.4-9.5 vs ~8.0 in zero's.
   So the score is a total pool, group size is worth ~12-13 pool points per member, and zero is a
   strong baseline; any punishment must pay for itself 1:1 out of the pool and must not cost
   members. Next: what drives switching in the artificial humans and in the human data.

2. **How do the artificial humans respond to punishment, in contribution and in switching?** The
   AH model weights (.pt) are not in the container, so I cannot simulate; I read the responses off
   the reference sims, where `ah`'s sampled punishments give variation, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-11/sim_response.py (columns contribution, punishment,
   agent_group, contribution_valid; next round within a block, and the switch at each decision).
   Found: (a) next-round change in contribution after a punishment depends on the player's own
   contribution: a 0-contributor rises +1.6 unpunished, +3.15 if punished >10 (≈+1.5 extra); a 1-5
   contributor +0.56 vs +2.6; a 6-10 contributor 0.0 vs +0.8; but 11-15 and 16-20 contributors
   *fall* more when punished (-1.5 unpunished vs -3.8 for 16-20 punished >10). So punishment only
   helps on low contributors. (b) Switching at a decision: base rate 0.24 unpunished, 0.43 at
   10-20 points of punishment over the block, 0.68 at >40. Logit over a block: own punishment sum
   +0.026/point, own group mean punishment per player-round +0.10, the other group's -0.12; own
   group mean contribution -0.128/point, the other's +0.098; own group size -0.14/member.
   So members flow to the group with the higher mean contribution and the lower mean punishment.
   A punishment of 10 on a free-rider costs 10 pool points now for ≈1.5 contribution (2.4 pool
   points) next round — it only pays if the rise persists for several rounds or lifts the
   group's mean contribution enough to draw members. Next: persistence of the rise over the
   following rounds, and the human data.

3. **Does the rise after a punishment last?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-11/persistence.py,
   reference sims, change in contribution 1-4 rounds after round t, by own c_t and p_t, plus OLS of
   the change on p_t*c_t, c_{t-1} and round. Found the rise holds and even grows: a 0-contributor
   punished >5 is +3.2/+3.9/+4.2/+4.4 after 1/2/3/4 rounds vs +1.85/2.2/2.3/2.45 unpunished; 1-5
   punished 2.8/3.2/3.5/3.7. OLS: +0.094 per punishment point at c=0 next round, +0.12 after 4
   rounds, minus 0.007-0.009 per point of own contribution, so it vanishes near c≈12-13 and turns
   negative above. Per point, small punishments do more: at c=0, 1-5 points buy ≈+0.9-1.3 (≈0.3
   per point), >5 points ≈+1.4-2 (≈0.12-0.15 per point). Caveat: `ah` tends to punish the same
   player again, so part of the persistence may be repeated punishment. Rough economics: 3 points
   on a free-rider for +0.9 contribution held ~10 rounds is ≈14 pool points for 3 spent; in the
   last rounds it cannot pay back. So I now think: small, targeted punishment of low contributors,
   early, none late, could beat zero. Next: the human data.

4. **Do the humans look the same?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-11/human.py on the 50 human
   games (deduplicated on session, episode, round_number, participant_code: 9600 rows). Human
   managers punish by contribution: mean 4.4 (43% of the time) at c=0, falling to 0.33 (7%) at
   16-20; punishment is heavy in rounds 0-3 (≈3.5-3.9) and ≈1-1.8 afterwards. Human next-round
   responses match the AH: c=0 +0.97 unpunished, +1.7 at 1-5, +4.0 at >5; 4-8 contributors
   -0.27 / +0.92 / +2.1; 12-20 contributors -1.4 / -1.3 / -4.5 (punishing high contributors
   backfires badly). Human switch rate rises with block punishment just like the AH's (0.19 at 0,
   0.44 at 10-20, 0.67 at >40). Per game, pool lead correlates 0.83 with the size difference and
   0.44 with the punishment difference — but that one is confounded with size (bigger groups have
   more to punish), so I don't read it as causal.
   So the picture holds in both: the profitable target is low contributors (below ≈8-10), the
   cost is members lost through the received-punishment and group-punishment switching terms, and
   late punishment cannot pay back. Next: draft the rule.

5. **Drafted the rule.** Punishment = min(cap, k * max(0, T - c_t)) for each member, only while
   t <= last_round, nothing afterwards. Four params: T (contribution below which a member is
   punished: from entries 2-4 the effect is positive below ≈8-12 and negative above), k (points
   per point of shortfall), cap (most any member gets in a round: entry 3 says small punishments
   buy more contribution per point, entry 2 that large ones drive the receiver out), last_round
   (entry 3: a punishment needs ≈6+ rounds of the raised contribution to pay back 1:1, so late
   ones only cost). k and cap together cover graded (small k, high cap) and flat (high k, low
   cap) forms; zero punishment stays reachable (k*(T-c) < 1 floors to 0), so the sweep can fall
   back to the `zero` baseline (+14 over ah) if punishment never pays.

6. **Does the design span the right punishment levels?** Validated the rule (256 distinct points)
   and, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-11/design_cost.py, applied every design point
   statically to the contributions of the zero-managed group in the reference sims (ignores the
   players' responses). Mean punishment per member-round over the design: median 0.26, quartiles
   0.09-0.84, max 8.9; 49% of points below 0.25, against `ah`'s 1.87. So the sweep samples densely
   near zero (where I expect the best values, given `zero` is +14 over ah) while still reaching
   ah-like and heavier regimes; I keep the ranges.

## Key findings

1. The score is the total pool, so group size dominates: `zero` leads `ah` by +13.97 +- 1.44 pool
   points with 4.57 vs 3.43 members, and its lead in rounds 0-3 (55 vs 42, equal sizes) is ah's
   ≈14 points of punishment barely bought back in contribution (entry 1).
2. Punishment raises next-round contribution only for low contributors (c=0: +1.5 extra for >10
   points; 6-10: +0.8) and lowers it for high ones (16-20: -2.3 extra), in the AH and in humans
   alike (humans 12-20 punished >5: -4.5 vs -1.4) (entries 2, 4).
3. The rise lasts: +0.094 per point at c=0 next round, +0.12 after 4 rounds, fading to zero near
   c≈12; small punishments buy more per point (≈0.3 contribution per point at 1-5 points vs ≈0.13
   above 5) (entry 3).
4. Switching away rises with punishment received (0.24 unpunished to 0.43 at 10-20 block points and
   0.68 above 40; humans 0.19 to 0.67) and with the group's mean punishment (+0.10 logit per point),
   and falls with the group's mean contribution (-0.128 logit per point, +0.098 for the other
   group's) (entries 2, 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here is almost a non-punisher: it keeps its group the cheaper and more attractive
one, so it holds the extra members that make `zero` +14 over `ah`, and spends only small
punishments on members who contribute little, early in the game, where each point of punishment
buys a lasting rise in contribution (and a higher group mean that draws members) worth more than
the point it removes from the pool. It never punishes high contributors and stops punishing once
too few rounds are left for the rise to pay back. The rule: each member gets
min(cap, slope * max(0, threshold - c_t)) while t <= last_round, nothing after.
- `threshold` (float, [1, 14] uniform): contribution below which a member is punished; I expect
  4-9.
- `slope` (float, [0.1, 5] log): punishment points per point of shortfall; I expect 0.3-2.
- `cap` (float, [1, 20] log): most punishment per member and round; I expect 2-6.
- `last_round` (int, 3..22): last round with any punishment; I expect 10-19.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the bar and the cost: every punishment point is a pool point, and members are worth
≈12-13 pool points each, so the rule must be light and must not push members out; zero
punishment is inside the design (slope*shortfall < 1 floors to 0), so the sweep can fall back to
`zero`. Finding 2 gives the target and `threshold`: only low contributors respond upward, the
response crosses zero around 8-12, so the range [1, 14] covers "free-riders only" to "anyone below
the typical contribution", with the best expected at 4-9. Finding 3 motivates `cap` and `slope`:
small punishments buy the most per point, so a cap of a few points with a moderate slope should
win; the log ranges let the sweep test both the graded form (low slope, high cap) and the flat
form (high slope, low cap). Finding 3's persistence is the reason for `last_round`: at ≈0.1-0.3
contribution per point per round (0.16-0.5 pool points), a punishment needs a handful of rounds
left to pay back, so 3..22 brackets it with the expected best in 10-19; round 23 is excluded since
it can never pay. Finding 4 is why the punishments should stay small: each point received and
each point of group mean punishment pushes members out, while a higher group mean contribution
pulls them in.
What would contradict it: the sweep's best point sitting at the low corner (tiny slope or cap,
i.e. zero) would mean the contribution response does not survive the switching cost or the
persistence in entry 3 was `ah` re-punishing the same player; a best at threshold 14 or cap 20
would mean heavier punishment pays, against findings 2-4.
Not modelled: the other group's state (its mean contribution and punishment drive switching, but
I cannot act on it), a player's punishment history (repeat offenders), relative-to-group
thresholds (the response in finding 2 is tied to the absolute contribution level), and the
position within a switching block — I lack the AH switch model to estimate recency, and each
would cost a parameter in a 256-point design.
