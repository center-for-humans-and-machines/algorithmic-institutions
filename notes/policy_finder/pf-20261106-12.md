# pf-20261106-12

## Explorations

1. **What does the human data look like, and which managers had big pools?** Looked at
   contributions, punishments, pools and group sizes (one copy per game, 50 games,
   100 group-episodes) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/a1_basic.py. Mean
   contribution is flat at ~9/20 over rounds; mean punishment falls from ~3.5 in
   rounds 0-3 to ~1.1 late. Group size at the end is all over the place (1 to 8
   members). Per group-episode, pool correlates 0.66 with mean members and 0.77
   with mean contribution, but -0.32 with mean punishment. Binned by mean
   punishment per member-round: 0-0.3 -> pool 41, 0.3-1 -> 57, 1-2 -> 67,
   2-4 -> 45, >4 -> 22 (members 4.1, 4.9, 4.2, 3.6, 2.7). So mild punishment
   beats none, heavy punishment empties the group. Next: how contributions
   respond to punishment at the individual level, and what drives switching.

2. **How do contributions respond to punishment?** Looked at next-round change in
   contribution (same group, no switch in between) by own contribution and
   punishment received, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/a2_response.py.
   Low contributors (0-2) rise +0.7 unpunished, +1.2 at p 1-2, +1.6 at p 3-5,
   +2.7 at p 6-10, +4.0 at p >10: about +0.2-0.3 contribution per punishment
   point above the unpunished baseline, roughly linear. Mid contributors (7-10)
   gain ~+1 at p 1-5 vs -0.5 unpunished. High contributors (>14) who are punished
   drop hard (-3 to -15), so punishing them is pure loss. By deviation from the
   group mean the pattern is the same: below-mean players rise with punishment,
   above-mean players fall. Each punishment point costs the pool 1 now; +0.25
   contribution is worth 0.4 pool next round, and contributions are persistent
   (own-c coefficient 0.62 in a linear fit, group mean 0.35), so a point of
   punishment on a free-rider pays back roughly 1-1.5 pool over the following
   rounds -- break-even-ish, positive only if the player stays. So punishment
   should be targeted (only well below the norm), moderate, and must not drive
   members out. Next: what drives switching.

3. **What drives switching?** Looked at the switch decision after rounds 3, 7, ...,
   19 against punishment, per-capita pools of both groups, contribution and group
   size with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/a3_switch.py and a4_timing.py.
   Switch rate 29% overall (44% at the first decision, ~24% later). Punishment
   at the decision round itself: 0 -> 22% switch, 3-5 -> 39%, 6-10 -> 58%,
   >10 -> 69%. In a logit with lags, the decision-round punishment weighs 0.062
   per point, the three rounds before 0.045, 0.034, 0.032: recent punishment
   counts most, older still counts. Own per-capita pool -0.93 per 10 points,
   other group's +0.49 per 10. Above-norm players punished at the decision round
   leave at 58-84%; below-norm players punished hard (>8) at 55%. Higher
   contributors are somewhat more likely to move. The simulator's switch model
   uses exactly per-capita pool, punishment and group (with an RNN), so in
   simulation the same channels should be there. Since the score is total pool,
   members are worth a lot (a 4-player group at c=10 makes 64, 8 players 128):
   keeping people and attracting the other group's is the big lever, and that
   means low punishment near decisions and a high per-capita pool. Next: is the
   contribution gain from punishment persistent enough to pay for itself?

4. **Does the punishment effect persist, and is it concave?** Regressed c at t+k
   (k = 1..4, same group throughout) on c_t, the leave-one-out group mean, and
   punishment split into below-norm (with a separate term for the first 5
   points) and above-norm, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/a5_persist.py.
   For below-norm players each of the first 5 points adds ~0.16 contribution at
   k=1, ~0.14 at k=2, ~0.18 at k=3, ~0.21 at k=4 -- it does not fade within a
   block; points beyond 5 add only ~0.06 each. Punishing an above-norm player
   costs ~0.08 contribution per point. So a small punishment (<= ~5) on a
   free-rider returns ~0.25-0.3 pool per round per point for the rest of the
   game: it pays for its cost of 1 if roughly 4+ rounds remain, so punishing
   in the last ~3 rounds is a loss, and in round 23 it is pure loss (no future).
   Large punishments are inefficient and drive exits (entry 3).
   This is the hypothesis taking shape: punish only players below the group
   norm, in proportion to the shortfall, with a low cap, soften at decision
   rounds, stop near the end. Next: draft the rule.

5. **Does the other manager's harshness matter for who leaves?** Added each
   group's mean punishment over the 4-round block before a decision, own and
   other, to the switch logit (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/a6_opp.py).
   Own group's block mean punishment +0.243 per point, the other group's -0.185,
   on top of the player's own punishment (+0.064) and the per-capita pools
   (-0.73 / +0.36 per 10). So a harsh regime pushes out everyone, not only those
   punished, and what counts is roughly our harshness relative to the other
   manager's. Human managers did not soften at decision rounds (1.72 vs 1.78
   mean). This is the one thing the rule can learn about the other manager from
   `p`, so I want it in: let the group's mean punishment rise to the other
   manager's recent level plus an allowance, never far beyond it. Against a
   non-punishing opponent this keeps us mild; against a harsh one it frees us to
   discipline free-riders, since players will not flee to the harsher group.

6. **Drafted the rule.** Five params: per player, punishment = slope x shortfall
   below norm_frac x (leave-one-out mean contribution of the other valid members
   this round), capped at pmax; then the whole group's punishments are scaled
   down so their mean per valid member is at most allow + the other manager's
   mean punishment per valid member over the last 4 rounds; nothing from round
   t_stop on. I dropped a separate decision-round multiplier (entry 3's 0.062 vs
   0.032 lag weights) to stay within 5 params: the group budget and pmax already
   bound exit risk every round, and I judged opponent-relative harshness (entry 5)
   and the end-game cut (entry 4) to be bigger levers.

7. **A trap in the rule sandbox: some torch calls fail when run cold.** Replaying
   the human histories through the draft (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-12/t1_replay.py,
   a fresh process) failed with KeyError '__import__', although validate-rule
   had passed. Testing single ops in fresh processes with no builtins showed that
   `x.sum(-1, keepdim=True)`, `th.clamp(x, max=...)`, `x.clamp(max=...)` and
   `x.clamp(min=<float>)` trigger a lazy torch import on their first call, which
   the builtins-free exec cannot do; validate-rule only passed because something
   had warmed them up. I rewrote the code with `.sum(-1).unsqueeze(-1)`,
   `th.minimum(.., th.ones_like(..))`, integer `clamp(min=)` and `+ 1e-6`; it now
   runs cold and validate-rule passes on all 256 points. On the human histories
   at (norm_frac 0.8, slope 1, pmax 6, allow 1, t_stop 21) the rule deals ~0.6-1.0
   points per member-round; at (1.0, 0.5, 4, 0.3, 20) ~0.4-0.6 -- below the
   human managers' ~1.7, i.e. in the mild band that did best in entry 1.

## Key findings

1. Group-episodes whose manager punished 1-2 points per member-round had the
   largest pools (67), above no punishment (41) and far above heavy punishment
   (>4: 22, with 2.7 members instead of ~4.5); pool tracks group size (r = 0.66)
   (entry 1).
2. Punishment raises the next contributions of below-norm players (~+0.16 per
   point for the first 5 points, ~+0.06 per point beyond) and the gain persists
   at least 4 rounds; punishing above-norm players lowers their contribution
   (entries 2, 4).
3. Since each point costs the pool 1 and returns ~0.25-0.3 per remaining round,
   punishment only pays with ~4+ rounds left; in round 23 it is pure loss (entry 4).
4. Punishment drives exits: at the decision round, 0 -> 22% switch, >10 -> 69%;
   recent rounds weigh most (0.062 per point vs ~0.03 three rounds earlier)
   (entry 3).
5. Exits depend on the whole regime relative to the other group: own group's
   block mean punishment +0.243 logit per point, the other group's -0.185, own
   per-capita pool -0.73 per 10, other's +0.36 per 10 (entry 5).
6. In the rule sandbox, keyword clamps with float bounds and `keepdim=True` fail
   on a cold torch; the code avoids them (entry 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager keeps its group large and attractive and spends punishment only
where it buys contribution: small punishments for players below the group's
norm, in proportion to how far below, never for those at or above it, with a low
cap per player; it is never much harsher than the other manager, since a harsh
regime relative to the other group sends everyone over; and it stops punishing
in the end game, where the future gain no longer pays the immediate cost to the
pool. Against a lenient opponent this rule stays mild; against a harsh one it
may discipline free-riders more, because nobody escapes to the harsher group.

The rule (5 params):
- `norm_frac` (float, [0.4, 1.2]): the norm is this fraction of the
  leave-one-out mean contribution of the other valid members this round.
  Expected best 0.8-1.0.
- `slope` (float, [0.2, 4], log): punishment points per contribution point
  below the norm. Expected best 0.5-1.5.
- `pmax` (int, 1-15): per-player, per-round cap. Expected best 3-6.
- `allow` (float, [0.05, 8], log): the group's mean punishment per valid member
  may be at most `allow` + the other manager's mean punishment per valid member
  over the last 4 rounds; above that every punishment is scaled down together.
  Expected best 0.3-1.5; 8 is effectively no budget.
- `t_stop` (int, 14-23): no punishment from this round on. Expected best 20-21.

### Justification

Finding 2 is why punishment targets only below-norm players and grows with the
shortfall (`norm_frac`, `slope`); its concavity (most of the effect in the first
~5 points) and finding 4 (large punishments drive exits) are why there is a cap
`pmax` with an expected best of 3-6, the range reaching 15 in case the
simulator's response is less concave. Findings 1 and 5 motivate the group
budget `allow` tied to the other manager's observed punishment: mild punishment
beat none in the data, but our harshness relative to theirs moves everyone's
switch odds, so the budget follows the opponent; the log range from 0.05
(almost no punishment unless the opponent punishes) to 8 (no budget) lets the
sweep pick the leniency, with the human best band (1-2 per member-round in
total) inside it. Finding 3 grounds `t_stop`: break-even at ~3-4 rounds left
puts it near 20-21; the range starts at 14 in case the simulated effect is
weaker, and ends at 23 (only the last round free). The norm is relative to the
group so it calibrates itself to groups of any level and size.

What would contradict it: a sweep peak at `allow` = 8 with large `pmax`
(harsh punishment pays in the simulator, so exits are weaker than in the data),
or at the low edge of `slope`/`allow` (any punishment is a net loss, the best
manager does nothing). Not modelled: a separate softening at decision rounds
(finding 4's lag weights; left out to stay within 5 params, partly covered by
the cap and the budget), attracting switchers by per-capita pool beyond what
mild punishment gives, punishing on longer histories (repeat free-riders), and
group-size dependent behaviour; each would add a parameter whose value the data
can barely pin down. Finding 6 is a code constraint, not a modelling choice.
