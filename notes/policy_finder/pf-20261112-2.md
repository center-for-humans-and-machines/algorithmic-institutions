# pf-20261112-2

## Explorations

1. **What does the score actually measure, and how do `ah` and `zero` compare?** Looked at the reference sims (`per_round.parquet`, `common_good`, `contribution`, `punishment`) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-2/ref_sims.py, and at `environment.py::compute_common_good_per_group`. Found that in the simulation `common_good` is *per capita*: (1.6*sum c - sum p) / number of valid contributors, so the score is a per-capita pool lead, and group size only matters in that an empty group scores 0. `zero` (never punishes) beats `ah` by +1.17 +- 0.23 (zero_vs_ah) and loses by -1.27 when seated as group 1 (ah_vs_zero, sign flipped): `ah` raises mean contributions by about 1 point (9.0 vs 8.1, i.e. +1.6 pool per capita) but spends about 1.8 per capita on punishment, so it nets a loss. `zero` also draws members (4.9 vs 3.9), which is irrelevant to the score. Under `zero`, contributions decay slowly, 8.6 -> 8.1. So the bar is: any punishment must raise 1.6*mean contribution by more than it costs. Next: how the artificial human contribution model reacts to punishment (size, target, lag).

2. **How do the artificial humans' contributions respond to a punishment?** Looked at `ah`-managed groups in the reference sims (next-round change in `contribution` by this round's contribution bin x `punishment` bin, plus lagged regressions) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-2/sim_response.py, and at the contribution model's config (graph net + RNN over everyone's previous contribution and punishment, with a "stimulus skip"). Found, for players who gave 0: next-round change +1.68 unpunished, +2.32 at p in 1-2, +2.6 at p 3-6, +3.1 at p 6-30, so the effect is concave in p and the first 1-2 points buy the most (~+0.6 contribution for ~1.5 points). For givers of 4-8: +0.29 / +0.54 / +0.68 / +0.82 / +1.29. Punishing high givers (16-20) backfires: -1.4 unpunished vs -2.2 to -4.4 punished. Lagged OLS (c_{t+k} ~ c_t, p_t, group mean) gives about +0.05-0.06 per punishment point at lags 1, 2 and 4, i.e. the effect persists, and a herding coefficient of 0.33-0.5 on the group mean. These are confounded (ah picks whom to punish), but the shape is clear: small punishments of low givers early pay; punishing cooperators hurts. The model only sees numbers, but the switch model reads punishment and common_good, so punishment may also push people out. Next: is the persistent effect large enough to cover the cost, and what does punishment do to switching, in sims and in humans.

3. **Does the punishment effect persist, and does punishment drive players out?** Looked at `ah`-managed sim groups with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-2/sim_event.py: future contribution at lags 1-4 (same group) residualised on c_t, c_{t-1}, group mean and round among unpunished players, by c_t bin x p_t bin; and the switch rate at decision rounds by the block's mean contribution and mean punishment. Found the effect does not fade over 4 rounds: a zero-giver punished 1-2 gives +0.25 to +0.3 more at every lag (0.70/0.64/0.72/0.84 vs 0.39/0.50/0.50/0.52); givers of 1-9 punished 5-10 give +0.5 to +0.7 more at every lag; givers of 15-20 punished >10 give 1.2 less. Switching rises with punishment received in the block: low givers (<=4) leave 31% unpunished, 40% at 1-2 avg, 45% at 2-4, 54% at 4-8, 66% above; high givers react less (18% -> 27% at 2-4). Switchers out of a group give less than stayers (7.95 vs 10.36 in ah groups). So punishing free-riders works twice for the per-capita score: they give more if they stay, and they are the ones pushed toward the other group, where they lower its per-capita pool. A rough budget: a 1.5-point fine on a zero-giver buys ~0.3 contribution per round over the remaining rounds, x1.6 = ~0.5/round, so it pays back within ~3 rounds; it does not pay in the last 2-3 rounds. Next: the human data, as a check on the model, and what human managers did.

4. **Do the humans and their managers tell the same story?** Looked at the human CSV (one copy per game: 50 games) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-2/human.py: next-round change by contribution x punishment bin, managers' punishment by contribution and round, per-capita pool (1.6*mean c - mean p) by the manager's mean punishment (quartiles over the 100 group-games), and switching by block punishment. Found the same shape as in the sims, stronger: zero-givers +0.99 unpunished, +1.63 at p 1-2, +3.5 at p 6-10; givers of 4-8 -0.16 / +0.65 / +1.19; givers of 16-20 punished 6+ collapse (-7 to -15). Human managers punish like `ah` (4.4 on zero-givers, 0.34 on 16-20 givers) and front-load (3.5-3.9 per player in rounds 0-3, ~1.1-1.3 from round 13). Per-capita pool by the manager's mean punishment: 12.2 (p<0.77), 15.2 (0.77-1.46), 14.6 (1.46-2.69), 10.5 (>2.69): an interior optimum around 1 point per capita per round, with heavy punishers worst. Humans also leave more after being punished (low/mid givers 20% unpunished -> 39-43% at 1-4 -> 55% above), high givers barely react up to 4. So I now think a good manager spends about 1 point per capita per round or less, all of it on low givers, front-loaded, and nothing on cooperators or in the last rounds. `ah` spends 1.8 and wastes part on cooperators. Next: draft the rule.

5. **Drafted the rule.** Punish only a valid member of my group whose contribution is below a threshold `thr`: fine = `base + slope*(thr - c)`, only in rounds t <= `t_stop`. `base` is the flat fine that buys the steep first part of the concave response (finding in 2/4: the first 1-2 points buy the most per point); `slope` lets a deeper shortfall cost more (zero-givers respond up to p ~10); `thr` decides who counts as a free-rider (sim response turns from positive to negative between givers of 9-12 and 15-20); `t_stop` ends punishment once too few rounds remain to repay it (the effect persists ~+0.3/round, so a fine pays back over ~3+ rounds, and after round 19 there are no switches left to push free-riders out). I left out a relative (group-mean) threshold and history terms: the response bins in 2-4 are in absolute contribution, and 4 parameters keep 256 Sobol points dense.

6. **Are the first ranges spending sensibly?** Wrote the rule with thr [3, 16], base [0, 4], slope [0, 1.5], t_stop [10, 23] and validated it (passes). Then checked with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-2/cost_check.py what each corner would spend if applied to zero-managed sim contributions (static, ignoring the response). Found the low corner spends 0-0.3 per capita, mid points 0.6-1.8, but the top corner 7-16 per capita, far beyond the ~1 point the human managers' best quartile spent (entry 4) and the 1.8 with which `ah` already loses (entry 1). So I narrowed to thr [3, 15], base [0, 3], slope [0, 0.8]: the top corner still spends ~4-5 per capita, which keeps a clearly bad region for the fitted surface to bend down in, while more points land near 0.5-2. Revalidated: 256 distinct points, valid.

## Key findings

1. In the simulation the score is a per-capita pool lead, (1.6*sum c - sum p)/valid members; group size counts only when a group is empty. `zero` beats `ah` by +1.17 +- 0.23 because `ah` raises contributions ~1 point (+1.6) but spends ~1.8 per capita on punishment (exploration 1).
2. The artificial humans' response to punishment is concave and depends on what the player gave: a zero-giver fined 1-2 gives ~+0.6 more next round, givers of 4-8 +0.25 to +1.0; givers of 16-20 punished give 0.8-3 less (exploration 2).
3. The effect persists: residualised future contributions stay +0.25-0.3 (fine 1-2 on zero-givers) or +0.5-0.7 (fine 5-10 on givers of 1-9) higher at lags 1-4 (exploration 3).
4. Punishment pushes players out, more so low givers: 31% -> 45% (fine 2-4 per round) -> 66% (fine >8) for givers <=4, 18% -> 27% for givers of 16-20; leavers give ~2.4 less than stayers (exploration 3). Pushing free-riders across raises my per-capita pool and lowers the other group's.
5. Humans show the same pattern, stronger, and the per-capita pool of human-managed groups peaks at a mean punishment of ~0.8-1.5 per player-round (15.2) against 12.2 for near-non-punishers and 10.5 for heavy punishers (exploration 4).
6. Fine-tuned spending matters: the same rule shape spends 0.3 or 15 points per capita depending on its corner (exploration 6).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here is a frugal, targeted punisher. Every point of punishment comes straight off the per-capita pool, so it must buy at least 1/1.6 of a point of contribution. Small fines on low givers do that (concave, persistent response, and they nudge free-riders over to the other group), fines on cooperators backfire, and fines in the last rounds cannot be repaid. So: punish only members giving below a threshold, a small flat fine plus a little per point of shortfall, and stop near the end; total spend around or below 1 per capita per round, well under `ah`'s 1.8.

The rule (`configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261112-2.yml`), for a valid member of my group in round t <= t_stop with contribution c < thr: fine = base + slope*(thr - c); otherwise 0.
- `thr` (float, [3, 15]): the free-rider threshold. Expect 8-12.
- `base` (float, [0, 3]): flat fine for anyone below thr (floored, so <1 means none). Expect 1-2.
- `slope` (float, [0, 0.8]): extra fine per point short of thr. Expect 0.1-0.4.
- `t_stop` (int, [10, 23]): last round in which to punish. Expect 18-21.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the bar (a point of fine must buy >0.625 of contribution) and shows `ah` overspends, so the rule restricts spending to where the response is largest. Findings 2 and 3 support `thr` and `base`: the response is positive for low and middling givers and negative above ~12-15, and the first 1-2 points buy the most, so a flat `base` captures the steep part; `thr` [3, 15] covers both "only near-zero givers" and "anyone below average", with the sign change of finding 2 (between bins 9-12 and 15-20) inside it. `slope` lets deeper shortfalls cost more since zero-givers still respond up to ~10 points (findings 2, 5); its top is cut to 0.8 by finding 6, and 0 is allowed for a flat-fine rule. `t_stop`: with a persistent ~+0.3/round effect (finding 3) a fine repays over ~3+ rounds, and after round 19 there is no switch left for finding 4 to act through, so the best stop is likely 18-21; [10, 23] includes "never stop" and early stopping, as human managers front-load. Finding 5 says the optimum spend is interior, which the ranges straddle (finding 6). It would be contradicted if the sweep's best had base and slope near 0 (no punishment pays against this stack) or thr at 15 with high spending. I did not model a group-relative threshold, repeat offenders, timing around switch rounds or the other group's state: the observed responses are in absolute contribution, the effects of the rest are not measurable from confounded reference data, and four parameters keep the 256-point design dense.
