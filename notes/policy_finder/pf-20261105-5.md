# pf-20261105-5

## Explorations

1. **What do the two reference managers score, and how do the clones respond to punishment?**
   Looked at `plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/per_round.parquet` (contribution, punishment,
   payoff, by manager) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-5/ref_response.py. Found
   `zero` scores 589.3 and `humanlike` 550.6. Mean payoff per round is
   `20 + 0.6*mean(c) - 2*mean(p)`, so the score is 480 + sum over rounds of that surplus.
   Under zero, contributions decay from 11.6 (round 0) to 5.5 (round 23); under humanlike
   they rise to ~13.2 and hold, but at ~2.4 punishment points per player-round (cost 4.8 per
   round), which outweighs the 0.6*5.3 = 3.2 gained. For punishment to pay, each punishment
   point must buy > 3.3 contribution points summed over the rest of the game. One-round
   response (humanlike sims): a low contributor (c 0-5) next round changes by +0.03 unpunished,
   +0.8 at p 1-2, +1.4 at p 3-5, +2.6 at p 6-10, +4.0 at p > 10, so roughly +0.3 per point
   in the next round alone; high contributors (c 16-20) who are punished drop *more* (-1 to
   -2.7 vs -0.8). So punishment only of low contributors, and it must persist ~10 rounds or
   spill over to the others to pay. Next: the pilots with random rule managers, to see the
   persistence and a less confounded dose-response.
2. **Is the effect persistent, and does it spill over? (pilots and clones)** Looked at the
   pilots (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-5/pilot_response.py) and the humanlike sims
   (ref_lags.py), regressing c[t+k]-c[t] on own punishment, own c, others' mean c and others'
   mean punishment. Pilots: the effect grows with lag (dc1 +0.16/pt, dc3 +0.28/pt), and
   punishing a c > 15 player wipes it out (-0.31/pt extra); 8+ points on a 16-20
   contributor drops them by 5-7. Clones: own punishment of a c <= 10 player gives a flat
   +0.08 per point at every lag 1..8 (persistent, does not fade), on a c > 10 player about 0;
   others' punishment (mean over the other 3) gives +0.07 rising to +0.17 by lag 8, and
   others' contributions pull own up with weight 0.2-0.5 (herding). So one point on a low
   contributor buys about 0.08 + 3*0.15/3 = ~0.23 contribution points per later round,
   0.14 payoff per round against a cost of 2: it breaks even only with ~14 rounds left.
   So: punish only low contributors, early, and stop well before the end. Pilots also show
   the random rule managers punished hard in round 0 (7.0) and got contributions rising to
   15 by round 11. Next: whether light punishment does nearly as well as heavy, by episode.
3. **Does light punishment do nearly as well as heavy, game by game?** Looked at the
   reference sims by episode (ref_episodes.py): games binned by round-0 mean contribution
   and, under humanlike, by mean punishment in rounds 0-7. Found that within every c0 bin
   the lightest-punishing quarter (0.6-0.9 pts per player-round) or the next (~2) scores
   best, and the heaviest (5.5-6) worst: e.g. c0 12-16: 656 (light) vs 507 (heavy) vs 621
   under zero; c0 16-20: 695 vs 525 vs 658. Light punishment holds late contributions at
   16-17 where zero lets them decay to 8-10. In weak groups (c0 <= 8) zero is better (534 vs
   <= 489): there punishment is heavy and contributions only reach 8-12. Selection is
   possible (light punishment means few low contributors), but the zero games with the same
   c0 do decay, so a little punishment seems to stop the collective slide. One-round table
   (entry 1) also shows a concave dose: the first 1-2 points buy +0.7 for a 0-5 contributor
   and +0.7 for a 10-15 one, more per point than larger doses. So I want: a small fixed
   amount on anyone below a standard, a modest extra per point of shortfall, and no
   punishment in the last rounds where it cannot pay back.
4. **Drafted the rule.** Four parameters: `standard` (int; a valid player contributing less
   than it this round is punished), `base` (fixed punishment for being below), `slope` (extra
   per point of shortfall) and `stop_round` (no punishment from this round on). Punishment
   = (t < stop_round) * (c < standard) * (base + slope * (standard - c)). The base term is
   there because the first points buy the most (entries 1 and 3); the slope lets the sweep
   decide whether bigger shortfalls deserve more; the standard is absolute rather than
   relative to the group, because the decay under zero is collective (everyone slides
   together, entry 1), which a group-relative standard would never punish. Validated.
5. **Are the ranges sane in cost?** Applied the rule, without feedback, to the contributions
   of the reference sims (cost_check.py). Under zero-like contributions (the worst case, no
   response) standard 12 / base 2 / slope 0.3 / stop 18 costs 2.0 pts per player-round, close
   to humanlike's 2.4; with humanlike-like contributions about 1.0. Standard 20 / base 3 /
   slope 1 costs 9-15 per player-round, hopeless. So I narrowed slope from [0, 1.5] to
   [0, 1] and base from [0, 8] to [0, 6], so fewer design points go to plainly ruinous
   corners, while keeping the corners near zero (base = slope = 0 is the zero manager) in.

## Key findings

1. Mean payoff per round is 20 + 0.6*mean(c) - 2*mean(p); zero scores 589.3 and humanlike
   550.6 because humanlike's 2.4 pts per player-round cost 4.8 per round and buy only +5.3 in
   contributions (+3.2 payoff) (entry 1).
2. Punishment helps only low contributors: in the clones a 0-5 contributor rises +0.8 next
   round at 1-2 points and +4 at > 10 points (concave), while punished 16-20 contributors drop
   by 1-2.7 (entry 1); the pilots show the same, with 8+ points on a 16-20 contributor
   costing 5-7 contribution points (entry 2).
3. The effect persists: in the clones +0.08 per point on own later contributions at lags
   1-8 without fading, plus +0.07-0.17 per point of others' mean punishment and herding
   on others' contributions (weight 0.2-0.5); a point breaks even only with ~14 rounds left
   (entry 2).
4. Light punishment beats heavy in every group type: in humanlike games with round-0 mean
   12-16, the lightest-punishing quarter scores 656 vs 507 for the heaviest and 621 under
   zero, holding late contributions at 16 instead of zero's decay to 8 (entry 3).
5. Without punishment the clones' contributions slide collectively, 11.6 to 5.5 over 24
   rounds (entry 1).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes little, early and only those below a contribution standard: a
small sure punishment for falling short stops the collective slide (findings 4, 5) and
nudges free riders up, and the effect lasts and spreads to the rest of the group
(finding 3), while punishing high contributors or punishing late wastes points (findings
2, 3). The rule: punishment = (t < stop_round) * (c < standard) * (base + slope *
(standard - c)).
- `standard` (int, [4, 20]): the contribution below which a player is punished; I expect
  10-16.
- `base` (float, [0, 6]): the fixed punishment for falling short; I expect 1-3.
- `slope` (float, [0, 1]): extra punishment per point of shortfall; I expect 0-0.4.
- `stop_round` (int, [8, 24]): the first round with no punishment; I expect 14-20.

### Justification

Finding 2 says punish low contributors only, hence a standard and no punishment above it;
the response for 10-15 contributors (+0.7 at 1-2 points) is why the standard range reaches
up to 20 rather than stopping at 10. Finding 2's concave dose and finding 4 support a fixed
`base` with a modest `slope`; the slope range starts at 0 so a flat rule is possible.
Finding 3 (break-even with ~14 rounds left, but persistent and spreading, so possibly
sooner) gives `stop_round`, ranged 8-24 so the sweep can also choose never to stop.
Finding 5 is why the standard is absolute, not relative to the group. Contradicting
evidence would be a sweep best at base = slope = 0 (punishment never pays against these
clones) or at standard 20 / stop 24 (the clones respond far more than the regressions
suggest). Not modelled: timeouts (a timed-out player cannot be punished and the clones
time out ~2% of the time, so it is not worth a parameter), the player's history (repeat
offenders, recovery after punishment), and a group-relative standard; each would add a
parameter on evidence I could not separate from the confounding in the reference sims.
