# pf-20261104-2

## Explorations

1. **How does the score work, and how do `ah` and `zero` do on it?** Read
   docs/reports/basics.md and src/aimanager/simulation/pool_scores.py, then
   summarised the reference sims (per_round.parquet, 1000 episodes per
   pairing) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/ref_sims.py. The score is
   the group's *sum* of 1.6*c - p per round (not per member; the parquet's
   `common_good` column is per member), so group size enters linearly. Against
   `ah`, `zero` scores 60.5 (se 0.95, mean 4.57 members over the game, 4.9 after
   the first switch) and `ah` against itself 56.0 (4.0 members). `zero`'s
   members contribute less (8.1 vs 9.0 under `ah`) and its contributions decay
   slowly (8.3 -> 8.2 by block), but it pulls about one extra player from `ah`
   at the first switch and keeps them. `ah` punishes ~3.2/player early, ~1 late.
   So membership beats contribution discipline here: a never-punisher already
   beats `ah` by 4.5 points. Next: what drives switching in the models, and
   does punishment raise contributions at all in the contribution model?
2. **What do the artificial humans see?** Read the training configs of the
   stack (configs/training/artificial_humans/...). The models are not in this
   container (only copula json), so I cannot simulate; I must reason from the
   human data and the reference sims. The contribution model sees each
   player's previous contribution and previous punishment plus groupmates
   (edge model, group vnode, RNN). The switch model sees `common_good` (the
   per-member share, norm 20), the player's own punishment, group and round,
   with an edge model across players. So leaving is driven by own punishment
   and the share the group gives, relative to the other group.
3. **Does punishment raise next contribution in the human data?** Looked at
   contribution, punishment and next contribution (one copy per game, 50
   games, 9600 rows) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/human_response.py.
   Within a contribution level, more punishment means a bigger rise next
   round for low contributors: at c 0-4 the change is +0.9 unpunished, +2.5 at
   p 5-10, +4.0 at p>10; for players below their groupmates' mean, a
   regression gives ~0.13 contribution points per punishment point, about the
   same for small and large deficits. Punishing high contributors backfires
   badly: at c=20, p 2-5 gives -9.4 next round vs -1.7 unpunished; the
   regression term p*(c>=15) is -0.41. So punishment only ever belongs on
   players below the group, and the one-round return is small (0.13*1.6 = 0.2
   pool per point spent, against 1 point lost now); it pays only if it
   persists for several rounds and does not drive players out. Next: the
   persistence, and switching vs punishment.
4. **What drives switching, and does the effect of punishment last?** Looked
   at the five switch decisions (rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19) in the human data
   with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/human_switch.py. (Checked
   generic/data.py: the models' `common_good` is the per-member share, the CSV's
   is the group total.) Leaving rises steeply with the punishment received in
   the decision round: 22% unpunished, 29% at p 1-3, 49% at p 4-8, 67% at p>8;
   and with the last 4 rounds' total (19% at 0, 55% above 20). It falls with
   the own-minus-other per-member share (49% when 8+ below the other group,
   11% when 8+ above). Unpunished bystanders leave more only when groupmates
   average >3 punishment (37% vs 21%). Big groups hold players (14% leave at
   8 members, 41% at 4). The first decision is the big one: 44% switch after
   round 3, ~24% later. The punishment response does persist: 0.12, 0.12, 0.10,
   0.11 contribution points per punishment point at 1-4 rounds later (players
   below their groupmates). So a point spent on a shirker early could return
   ~0.11*1.6*remaining rounds, if he stays; but it raises his exit risk by
   several percentage points at a decision round. Next: check that the sim
   reproduces this (switching vs own punishment in ah vs zero).
5. **Does the sim reproduce it?** Same questions on the reference sims with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/sim_switch.py. Yes, closely: in
   ah_vs_ah leaving is 24% unpunished at the decision round, 43% at p 4-8, 61%
   at p>8; punishment in the 3 rounds before the decision also counts but
   less (unpunished-at-decision players: 21% -> 35% when they got >8 earlier).
   A linear fit: +0.87 pp per punishment point at the decision round, +0.57 pp
   per point in the 3 rounds before, -0.87 pp per point of own-minus-other
   share, -2.3 pp per member of the own group. In zero_vs_zero the share
   difference alone moves leaving from 55% to 13%, and big groups hold
   (15% leave at 8, 33% at 4). The sim's contribution response is weaker than
   the humans': 0.07 points next round per punishment point for players below
   their groupmates (humans 0.13), and punishing c>=15 players by >5 costs
   them 1.1 points more than not punishing. Back-of-envelope for one
   punishment point on a shirker at round 4: gain ~0.07*1.6 = 0.11 pool per
   round it persists (~1 over 10 rounds), cost 1 now plus ~0.6 pp exit risk
   at the next decision times ~13 pool/round over ~16 rounds (~1.2) plus the
   share drop on everyone. Net negative, and worse at decision rounds and late
   in the game. That explains why zero beats ah. Next: what did the human
   managers whose groups did well do?
6. **What did successful human managers do?** Per game and group (100 groups)
   with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/human_managers.py, binned by the
   manager's mean punishment per member-round. The second quartile (0.75-1.4)
   had the largest pool, 59.4, with contributions 10.1 on 4.1 members; the
   near-zero quartile (<0.75) 53.9, with contributions 7.5 but 4.6 members; the
   top quartile (>2.5) only 38.3 on 3.5 members. Confounded (managers react to
   their groups), but it says light punishment can lift contributions by far
   more than it costs, while heavy punishment empties the group. Human
   managers punish by deficit (6.8 at deficit >10, 1.7 at 0-3) but also hit
   above-mean players (0.76 at deficit < -3), which I found backfires (3), and
   they do not spare decision rounds (1.7-1.8 at every block position). `ah`
   imitates them, so a manager that keeps the deficit targeting, drops the
   punishment of good contributors and goes quiet at decision rounds and late
   could keep part of the contribution gain at a fraction of the exit cost.
   That is my hypothesis; the sweep must be free to find that zero is best.
7. **Drafted the rule.** Punishment = min(cap, k * max(0, group mean - c - tol)),
   group mean over this round's valid members of my group; multiplied by
   w_dec at the decision rounds (3, 7, 11, 15, 19); zero from round t_stop on.
   Five params, k's range starts at 0 so the sweep can recover `zero`. Noted
   the manager floors to integers, so k*(deficit - tol) must reach 1 to bite.
8. **A trap in the rule sandbox.** Tested the rule by hand with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-2/test_rule.py (run_rule on a made-up
   round). My first draft passed validate-rule but raised KeyError
   '__import__' when run in a fresh process: on this torch (2.5.1) some calls
   lazily import inside the builtins-free scope (`th.remainder(t, 4)`,
   `.sum(dim=-1, keepdim=True)`, `th.sum(x, -1, True)`); validate-rule did not
   catch it because its process had already done the import (a throwaway rule
   with th.remainder also "passes"). I rewrote those as `t - 4*floor(t/4)` and
   `.sum(dim=-1).unsqueeze(-1)`; the rule now runs in a fresh process and
   still validates. The hand check gives the expected numbers (mean of valid
   members only, cap, half weight at round 7, zero after t_stop).
9. **How much does the design punish?** Same script, applying the formula to
   the zero-managed group of zero_vs_ah (the contributions an unpunished group
   makes). k=0.3, tol=2, cap=5 punishes 7% of member-rounds, 0.09 points per
   member-round; k=0.5/tol=2/cap=5 0.22; k=1/tol=3/cap=8 0.36; the corner
   k=1.5, tol=0, cap=20 1.9 (about `ah`'s 1.8). So the box spans from `zero` to
   `ah`'s volume, with the human-best band (0.75-1.4 per member-round,
   exploration 6) inside. I expect the best points at the light end.

## Key findings

1. The score is the group's sum of 1.6c - p per round, so membership counts
   linearly; `zero` beats `ah` (60.5 vs 56.0) by taking ~1 player from it at the
   first switch, despite lower contributions (8.1 vs 9.0) (exploration 1).
2. Punishing a below-mean player raises his contribution by ~0.12 points per
   punishment point in humans (0.07 in the sim) and the rise lasts at least 4
   rounds; punishing players at c>=15 cuts their next contribution (humans:
   -0.41 per point; c=20 with p 2-5 drops 9.4) (explorations 3, 4, 5).
3. Leaving rises with punishment received, most in the decision round itself:
   sim +0.87 pp per point at the decision round, +0.57 pp per point in the 3
   rounds before; 61% leave at p>8 vs 24% unpunished; a lower share than the
   other group and a small group also push players out (explorations 4, 5).
4. Among human managers, light punishers (0.75-1.4 per member-round) had the
   best pools (59.4, contributions 10.1) against near-zero (53.9, c 7.5) and
   heavy punishers (38.3); humans and `ah` also punish above-mean players and
   ignore the switch calendar (exploration 6).
5. A back-of-envelope count makes a punishment point a net loss whenever an
   exit can follow soon, and late in the game, when the response has no
   rounds left to pay back (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here protects membership first and buys contributions only
cheaply. It punishes only members who fall clearly below their group's mean
contribution this round, in proportion to the shortfall and lightly, with a
cap so no one gets the large punishments that empty groups; it never touches
average or good contributors; it softens or skips punishment at the
switch-decision rounds, where a point costs the most exits; and it stops
punishing near the end, when there is no time left for the response to repay
the points. If even this is not worth it, the rule degenerates to `zero`
(k = 0, or tol / t_stop that never bite), which the sweep can find.

Rule: punishment = min(cap, k * max(0, mean_c - c - tol)) * (w_dec at rounds
3, 7, 11, 15, 19) * (t < t_stop), over the valid members of my group, where
mean_c is the mean contribution of this round's valid members.

- `k` (float, [0, 1.5] uniform): punishment points per point of shortfall.
  I expect 0.2-0.8; 0 is `zero`, 1.5 with tol 0 is about `ah`'s volume.
- `tol` (float, [0, 8] uniform): shortfall left unpunished. Expect 1-4, so
  only clear free riders are hit; 8 almost never punishes.
- `cap` (float, [1, 20] log): most punishment per member per round. Expect
  3-8: leaving jumps above p 4-8; log because the small values are where the
  difference between "a signal" and "a fine" lies.
- `w_dec` (float, [0, 1] uniform): factor at decision rounds. Expect near 0.
- `t_stop` (int, 8..24): first round with no punishment. Expect 16-21;
  24 never stops, 8 punishes only in the first two blocks.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 3 say members matter more than their contributions and that
punishment is what makes them leave, so the rule must keep its volume low
(`k`, `cap`) and avoid the decision rounds (`w_dec`). Finding 2 says the
only punishment that buys contributions is that on below-mean players and
that punishing good contributors backfires, hence the shortfall-to-mean
targeting with a tolerance (`tol`); the mean is relative because humans and
the contribution model react to their groupmates (herding). Finding 4 says a
light punisher can lift contributions well above an unpunished group's,
which is why the rule does not simply fix k = 0. Finding 5 and the
persistence in 2 give `t_stop`: after the last switch there is no exit cost,
but also only a few rounds to repay each point. The hypothesis is
contradicted if the sweep's best points sit at k near 0 or tol near 8 (then
`zero` is the answer here, and the sim's weak 0.07 response is why), or if
w_dec near 1 is best (the decision-round timing would not matter in the
model). Not modelled: the other group's state (its share or size, which the
switch model compares against; I cannot change `ah`'s group and kept the
rule to five params), the players' punishment history (a repeat-offender
escalation; the response to a point looked the same for small and large
deficits, so a flat slope seemed enough), newcomers (treated like everyone)
and timed-out players (their punishment is charged 0 anyway; they are left
out of the group mean).
