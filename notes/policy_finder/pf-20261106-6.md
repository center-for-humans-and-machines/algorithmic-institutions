# pf-20261106-6

## Explorations

1. **What is in the data, and what does the score reward?** Looked at
   experiments/2group_8agent_50ep.csv (deduplicated: 50 games, 9600 rows) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/a1_overview.py. Mean contribution sits at
   8-10 of 20 all game (8.75 in round 0, 9.2 in round 23), barely moving.
   Human managers punished hard early (mean 3.7 in round 0, 29% of all
   player-rounds punished) and eased to ~1.0-1.3 after round 12. Group size
   after the first switch spreads from 1 to 8; the pool of a group is
   roughly 1.6 * sum(c) - sum(p), so it scales with members. Since an empty
   group scores 0 and the score is our group's pool, holding members looks as
   important as raising contributions. No trained artificial humans exist in
   this container (only copula json), so I cannot simulate; I must reason
   from the data. Next: what makes players switch groups.

2. **What drives switching?** Looked at the switch decisions (rounds 3, 7,
   11, 15, 19; next round's group_id) against the player's last-4-round
   punishment, contribution and both groups' means, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/a2_switch.py. Switch rate is 29%
   (43% at the first decision, ~24% later). Leaving rises steeply with own
   punishment: 19% at mean p=0, 24% at 0-1, 36% at 1-3, 45% at 3-6, 58% above
   6. Relative to the other group it is sharper: groups punishing >3 more than
   the other lose 62% per decision, groups punishing >3 less lose 12%. In a
   logit, dp (own group mean p minus other group's) is +0.24/pt, own p
   +0.12/pt, dc (contribution gap) -0.10/pt, group size -0.16/member. Payoff
   gap is the cleanest summary: players worse off than the other group by >6
   leave 57%, better off by >2.6 leave 11%. So punishment is a direct
   membership cost, measured relative to what the other manager does; next:
   does punishment buy contributions at all.

3. **Does punishment buy contributions, and from whom?** Looked at next-round
   contribution change of players who stayed, by their contribution and
   punishment, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/a3_response.py.
   Low contributors respond: at c=0, dc is +1.0 unpunished vs +1.7 at p=2-5,
   +3.4 at p=5-10, +4.0 at p>10; at c in 1-4, +0.24 vs +1.6 (p=2-5) and +3.4
   (p>10). The gain persists 2-3 rounds later (c<=8: +0.7 unpunished vs +1.4
   at p 2-5, +2.4 at 5-10, +3.4-3.6 at >10). Punishing contributors at 12+ is
   harmful (c=20 punished 2-5 drops 9.4 vs 1.8 unpunished). In a pooled OLS
   the indicator of any punishment (+0.53) matters more than its size
   (+0.09/pt, n.s.): a small signal does most of the work. Human managers
   punished 33-44% of contributors at c<=8, mean 2-3. So: punish only low
   contributors, mildly; never touch high ones. But each point punished
   costs the pool 1 now and raises leaving (entry 2); the gain of ~1 point
   contribution is worth 1.6 a round while the player stays. Next: whole-
   game outcomes by manager style.

4. **Whole-game outcome by manager style.** Per game and group (empty
   rounds as 0) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/a4_managers.py.
   Mean pool by quintile of the manager's punishment per player-round:
   57.8 (p<=0.56, punishing 9% of player-rounds), 58.9 (0.56-1.29, 27%),
   63.9 (1.29-1.78, 30%), 50.8 (1.78-3.1, 37%), 28.1 (>3.1, 59%). So
   an interior optimum: light, selective punishment beats both none (members
   4.7 but contribution only 7.6/round) and heavy (members 3.0). Relative
   to the other manager: groups punishing >1/player-round less than the
   other average 5.1 members and pool 62.8; >1 more, 2.9 members and 38.6.
   Pool correlates 0.66 with size and 0.78 with per-capita contribution.
   So the rule should punish a little, selectively, and stay at or below
   the other manager's level. Next: when within a 4-round block punishment
   drives leaving, and endgame.

5. **When in a block does punishment drive leaving, and who leaves?** Logit
   of the switch on own and group-mean punishment in each of the 4 rounds
   before a decision, and the other group's, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/a5_timing.py. Own punishment counts
   only in the decision round itself (+0.069/pt; earlier rounds ~0), but
   the group's mean punishment counts in every round of the block
   (+0.03 to +0.09 per point per round): players leave a punishing group even
   when they are not the ones punished. The other group's punishment holds
   them (-0.08 in the decision round, -0.02 to -0.04 earlier). Below a group
   mean of 1/member a round, leaving is no higher than at 0 (21.7% vs 21.7%
   for low contributors); above 3 it is 58%, and even high contributors
   (c>=12) leave 38%. Players who just joined leave more (34% vs 27%).
   So what matters for membership is the group's punishment budget per
   member, compared with the other group's: a cap on that budget, tied
   to what the other manager does, is the lever for robustness across
   opponents.

6. **Drafted the rule.** Shortfall punishment k*(c_thr - c) for members
   below an absolute threshold (entry 3: only low contributors respond, high
   ones are hurt); then scaled down so the group's mean punishment per member
   this round is at most m0 + rho * (the other group's mean punishment per
   member over the last 4 rounds) (entries 2, 4, 5: the budget per member,
   relative to the other group, sets leaving); no punishment in the last
   n_end rounds (no future rounds to buy contributions, only pool cost).
   Five parameters, the maximum. I left out a special case for decision
   rounds and for newcomers to stay within five; the budget cap already
   limits the decision-round cost.

7. **Validated and sanity-checked the draft.** validate-rule passes (256
   distinct points). With scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-6/t1_sanity.py on
   a toy group contributing 0, 4, 10, 20 (c_thr=8, k=1, m0=1, rho=1,
   n_end=2): when the other manager punishes 0 the budget of 4 points is
   spread as 2 and 1 (the floor to integers eats the rest); when it punishes
   3 a member the full 8 and 4 go through; the 10 and 20 contributors are
   never touched; rounds 22-23 are silent. One thing I noticed: because the
   manager floors, a small budget spread over several low contributors can
   round to nothing, so a small m0 behaves like "do not punish" against a
   non-punishing opponent. I keep it: "none" is a legitimate end of the
   range (entry 4 has non-punishers at 57.8, close to the best).

## Key findings

1. Punishment drives players out: switch rate per decision is 19% at no
   punishment and 58% at a mean above 6 over the block; a group punishing >3
   per member more than the other group loses 62% vs 12% when it punishes >3
   less (exploration 2).
2. What drives leaving is the group's mean punishment in every round of the
   block (+0.03 to +0.09 logit per point per round), not just one's own; the
   other group's punishment holds players (-0.08 in the decision round), and
   below a group mean of 1 per member leaving is no higher than at 0 (entry 5).
3. Only low contributors respond to punishment: at c=0 the next-round change
   is +1.0 unpunished vs +3.4 at p 5-10, persisting 2-3 rounds; at c=20 a
   punishment of 2-5 drops contribution by 9.4 (entry 3).
4. Whole games have an interior optimum: pool 63.9 when the manager punishes
   1.3-1.8 per player-round (30% of player-rounds), 57.8 when it barely
   punishes, 28.1 above 3.1 (entry 4).
5. Pool is size times contribution: it correlates 0.66 with group size and
   0.78 with per-capita contribution, and punishing less than the other
   manager is worth about 2 members (5.1 vs 2.9) (entry 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes only players clearly below the cooperative level,
in proportion to how far below they are, which buys 1-3 points of their
contribution for a few rounds, while keeping the group's total punishment per
member small, and at most a little above what the other manager does, because
any punishment in the group pushes members toward the other group and a
lost member costs its whole contribution. It stops punishing at the end,
when no later rounds can repay the cost.

The rule: `punishment = k * max(0, c_thr - c)` for each member who
contributed, scaled down so the group's mean per member is at most
`m0 + rho * p_other`, with `p_other` the other group's mean punishment per
member over the last 4 rounds; zero in the last `n_end` rounds.
- `c_thr` (int, [2, 16]): contribution below which a member is punished.
  I expect 6-10, around the human mean of 9.
- `k` (float, [0.2, 6], log): points of punishment per point of shortfall.
  I expect 0.5-2, giving 3-10 to a free rider.
- `m0` (float, [0.1, 5], log): budget per member when the other manager
  punishes nothing. I expect 0.7-2.
- `rho` (float, [0, 1.5]): share of the other manager's punishment added to
  the budget. I expect 0.3-1.
- `n_end` (int, [1, 10]): final rounds without punishment. I expect 1-4.

### Justification

Finding 3 sets the target and the shape: low contributors respond more the
larger the punishment, high ones are hurt, so punishment grows with the
shortfall (`k`) and stops at a threshold (`c_thr`). The range for `c_thr`
runs from 2 (only free riders) to 16 (everyone below near-full), since I
cannot tell whether the artificial humans' herding makes punishing middling
contributors pay; 12+ is where finding 3 turns negative, so the upper part
of the range is where I expect it to fall off. `k` is log-wide because its
best value depends on whether the cap binds: with a binding cap `k` only sets
how the budget is shared. Findings 1, 2 and 5 say membership hangs on the
group's punishment per member relative to the other group, so the cap is per
member (`m0`) and partly follows the other manager (`rho`); this is what makes
the rule robust to unknown opponents: against a harsh one it may punish more
and still look mild, against a lenient one it stays near its base budget.
Finding 4 puts the best whole-game level near 1.3-1.8 per player-round,
hence `m0` centred on about 1 with a log range down to near-zero (the
"do not punish" corner) and up to 5 (beyond the harsh human managers).
`rho` up to 1.5 lets the sweep test matching or exceeding the other
manager. `n_end` >= 1 because a punishment in round 23 has no future at all;
up to 10 lets the sweep find that punishment never pays (if it does not, it
will push `n_end` and `m0` to their low-punishment ends, which would
contradict the hypothesis that some punishment pays). Also contradicting it:
a best `c_thr` at 16, or the best `rho` at 0 with high `m0` (opponent level
not mattering). Not modelled: sparing the decision rounds (own punishment
counts only there, finding 2's +0.069) and newcomers (34% vs 27% leaving),
both to stay within five parameters, and a threshold relative to the group
mean, which risks punishing high contributors in cooperative groups.
