Under the hood that point punishes ~9 per member in round 0 (the relative cap is off in round 0), ~2-4 in rounds 1-3 (capped by the other manager's level), and nothing from round 6. Contributions in my group jump from 8.8 in round 0 to ~10.1-10.3 in round 1 and stay there or climb (to 11.8 / 10.0 / 12.7 / 9.2 by round 23). Group size hardly moves (3.7-4.6). The opponents, read from their punishments: opponent 1 punishes 10.8 in round 0, decaying, with spikes in the first round of each block (3.8, 1.6, 0.7); opponent 2 is steady and mild (3.8 falling to 0.9); opponent 3 punishes hard in the first two rounds of each block (10.8, 9.3, then 6.7/5.7, 3.3/2.9, 1.7/1.5) and nothing else; opponent 4 never punishes. Against opponent 4 my group still holds 4 members while contributing 0.5-1.4 more for the whole game. So the artificial humans seem to answer one early heavy punishment with a lasting rise, and do not flee it much; v1's horizon/fade idea and the cap mattered less than the round-0 shock. Next: measure the artificial humans' responses directly from v1's 131k simulated games (contribution response to punishment and its persistence, switching response), since they, not the humans, are the test.
2. **How do the artificial humans' contributions answer punishment?** Converted v1's sweep (131k simulated games, 256 points x 4 opponents x both seats) to arrays with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-7/v2_arrays.py and ran v2_ah_contrib.py on a 40k-game sample. Next-round change for 0-4 contributors: +1.0 unpunished, +1.6 at 1-2 points, +2.1 at 3-5, +2.7 at 6-9, +3.0 at 10-14, +4.8 at 15-30; for 5-9 contributors +0.1 to +3.2; for 10-14 -0.25 to +1.4; for 15-20 punishment backfires (-1.0 to -3.3). It persists: players at 0-9 in round 0 punished 15-30 contribute 10.4 by round 5 and 10.2 at round 23, vs 6.9 / 7.4 for unpunished ones. OLS of c(t+k) on own c, group mean and p gives +0.11 / +0.11 / +0.13 per point at k = 1 / 3 / 6, with the group mean's weight growing (0.33 -> 0.59), so a lift spreads through the group. So the simulated players respond more strongly and more lastingly than the humans did in my v1 analysis (+0.10 at t+3). Next: what makes them switch.
3. **What makes the artificial humans switch?** v2_ah_switch.py, logit at the 5 decisions on 60k games. Switch rate 0.27 (0.39 at the first decision). Per point: own block-mean punishment +0.04, own punishment in the decision round itself (the block's last round) +0.059 on top, own group's mean punishment minus the other's +0.12, own group's mean contribution minus the other's -0.10, own group size -0.15 per member. By own block-mean punishment: 0.23 unpunished, 0.39 at 2.5-5, 0.47 at 5-10, 0.59 above 10. So the group's cooperation gap holds people about as strongly as the punishment gap pushes them out, and a punishment dealt just before a decision costs about twice as much in exits as the same punishment early in the block. Next: which phases of punishment paid in v1's sweep.
4. **Which punishment phases paid in v1's sweep?** v2_point_level.py: per design point and opponent, my group's mean punishment per member in round 0, rounds 1-3, 4-11, 12-23, regressed with the pool. Round 0 pays strongly against every opponent: +1.6 / +1.5 / +2.0 / +0.7 pool per unit of round-0 mean punishment (a unit costs only 4/24 = 0.17 pool), concave (-0.06 to -0.09 per unit squared, optimum near 9-14 per member). Rounds 1-3 return about what they cost (-0.3 vs direct cost 0.5), rounds 4-11 a little (-0.4 vs 1.3), rounds 12-23 nothing (-2.2 vs 2.0). Group size falls only 0.02 per unit of round-0 punishment but 0.06-0.16 per unit later. Caution: later phases were capped by v1's relative cap, so they are confounded with the opponent's punishment. So the early shock is the core of a good rule and late punishment is a cost. Next: is that about round 0, or about a player's first punishment?
5. **Round 0 or first punishment?** v2_ah_first.py: c(t+4) for players at c <= 12 who stay, on own c, group mean, p, sqrt(p), p x first-ever-punishment, p x round 0, p x block start, log(1 + punishment received before). Coefficients: sqrt(p) +0.35, p +0.013, p x first-ever +0.082, p x round 0 -0.007, p x block start 0.002, log(1 + earlier punishment) -0.13. In the raw table, a first-ever punishment of 7-12 lifts c(t+4) by 2.3-5.5 over unpunished peers in any round, while a repeated one lifts it by 1.7-2.0. So round 0 is not special in itself: it is where every punishment is a first one and every free rider can be reached. The response is concave (sqrt) and saturates with punishment already received. With a sqrt response a*sqrt(p) lasting R rounds, the best single punishment is p* = (0.8 a R)^2: about 30 with 20 rounds left, ~8 with 10 left, ~2 with 5 left, so the right size falls with the square of the remaining rounds. This suggests: punish each free rider hard once, early; repeat less and less; fade quadratically; avoid the decision rounds. v1's relative cap shut punishment off next to the non-punishing opponent 4 (its pool there was the lowest, 57) and its slack had the smallest effect (0.75), so I drop it.
6. **Drafted the v2 rule.** punishment = k * max(0, c0 - c) * fade * saturate * timing for my group's valid members, with fade = max(0, 1 - t / horizon)^2 (the best punishment size falls with the square of the rounds left, exploration 5), saturate = budget / (budget + punishment the member has received so far from either manager) (first punishments lift most; repeated ones less, exploration 5), and timing = dec in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, else 1 (a punishment just before a decision drives members out about twice as much, exploration 3). I dropped v1's relative cap (exploration 5). Round 0 needs no special case: there fade = 1 and no one has been punished yet, so the rule gives the large first shock v1's best point gave. A cold-process replay first crashed again on `th.remainder(t + 1, 4)` (the lazy import of exploration 8 in v1), so I compute the remainder as t + 1 - 4 * floor((t + 1) / 4).
7. **What levels does the draft produce on human histories?** v2_replay.py, mean punishment per member of group 0 per round, the rule's own punishments fed back into `p`. (c0 11, k 3, horizon 20, budget 20, dec 0.3): 10.2 in round 0, 5.7 and 4.4 in rounds 1-2, 0.9 in the decision round 3, ~2-3 in the next block, ~1 in the third, zero from round 15. That is close to v1's best (9 in round 0, then 2-4) but with the saturation and decision-round dips. (c0 10, k 1, horizon 12, budget 5, dec 1): 2.8 then fading to 0 by round 6, a mild punisher. (c0 14, k 8, horizon 40, budget 100, dec 0): ~21 in each non-decision round early, 4-8 late, the harshest corner. So the ranges span from nearly no punishment to far harsher than any human style, with the region I expect to be best inside. validate-rule passes on all 256 points.

## Key findings

1. In v1's sweep the best point (final pool 65.5; 68.7 / 66.2 / 69.9 / 57.2 against opponents 1-4) dealt a heavy round-0 punishment (about 9 per member) and almost nothing after round 5. Its lowest pool was next to the opponent that never punishes, where the relative cap shut its punishment off (exploration 1).
2. The artificial humans answer punishment strongly and lastingly. Players at 0-9 punished 15-30 in round 0 contribute 10.2-10.4 for the rest of the game, against 6.9-7.4 for unpunished ones. The effect is about +0.11-0.13 per point up to six rounds later, and it spreads through the group mean (explorations 2, 5).
3. The response is concave (sqrt(p) +0.35). It is strongest for a first punishment (+0.082 per point on top) and weaker after earlier punishment (log(1 + received) -0.13). Round 0 itself adds nothing (-0.007). With R rounds left, the best single punishment is about (0.8 a R)^2: ~30 with 20 rounds left, ~8 with 10 left (exploration 5).
4. Switching responds per point to the group punishment gap (+0.12 logit) and to the group contribution gap (-0.10), and twice as much to punishment in the decision round itself (+0.059 on top of +0.04 for the block mean). Switch rates go from 0.23 unpunished to 0.59 above 10 per round (exploration 3).
5. Across v1's design points, round-0 punishment gained 0.7-2.0 pool per unit against every opponent, at a cost of 0.17. The optimum was near 9-14 per member. Rounds 1-11 returned roughly their cost and rounds 12-23 nothing. Later punishment also cost members 3-8 times more per unit (exploration 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager gives every free rider one hard punishment early, while there are many rounds left for the lift to pay back. It punishes repeat offenders less and less, because the simulated players' response saturates, and fades punishment roughly with the square of the rounds left. It holds back in the round just before a switch decision, where a punishment costs members. It does not tie itself to the other manager. A good group keeps and attracts members through its higher contributions, which weigh about as much as the punishment gap. Capping punishment by the other manager's level only disarms the rule next to a lenient manager. The rule reads the other manager only through the punishment its members have already received, which counts towards their saturation.

The rule: punishment = k * max(0, c0 - c) * max(0, 1 - t / horizon)^2 * budget / (budget + received) * (dec in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, else 1), for valid members of my group.
- `c0` (int, [5, 18]): the expected contribution; members below it are punished in proportion to their shortfall. I expect 9-13. v1's surface peaked at 11, punishment works on contributors up to 14 and backfires above 15.
- `k` (float, [0.5, 12], log): points per point of shortfall for an unpunished member at the start. I expect 2-6, which makes round-0 punishment ~10 per member and up to 30 for zero contributors. v1's peak sat at its upper edge of 3, so the range now goes up to 12.
- `horizon` (float, [4, 60], log): the round by which the quadratic fade reaches zero. I expect 15-35: the payback argument says punishment is worthless in the last ~5 rounds. 4 punishes only the first block, as v1's peak (horizon 6, linear) did; 60 hardly fades.
- `budget` (float, [2, 200], log): the received punishment at which a member's punishment is halved. I expect 10-40, about one heavy first punishment. 200 is practically no saturation, and 2 means one punishment each.
- `dec` (float, [0, 1]): the factor in the decision rounds. I expect 0-0.5. 1 means no special treatment.

### Justification

Findings 1, 2 and 5 support the early, heavy, shortfall-based punishment: the lift is large and lasting, and spreads through the group. Round 0 paid against all four opponents. So c0 and k set whom to punish and how hard, and k's range is extended past v1's edge. Finding 3 gives `budget` (saturation with punishment received) and `horizon` with a quadratic fade (optimal size ∝ remaining rounds squared). It also shows that round 0 matters because every punishment there is a first one, so the rule needs no special round-0 case. Finding 4 gives `dec`. It also argues against v1's relative cap: the punishment gap matters, but the contribution gap offsets it. Finding 1 shows the cap cost most next to the non-punishing opponent, and the slack had the smallest effect of v1's parameters.

What would contradict the hypothesis: a best budget at 200, which would mean repeated punishment keeps paying, or a best horizon at 60, which would mean late punishment pays. A best k at 0.5 would contradict finding 5. A best dec near 1 would mean timing within the block does not matter. A pool next to opponent 4 still well below the others would mean the other group's punishment matters in a way I have not modelled.

What I chose not to model:
- A relative cap or any explicit response to the other manager's level. v1 showed it was weak and harmful next to a lenient manager.
- A threshold relative to the group mean. The absolute c0 already tracks the region where punishment helps (below 15).
- Separate treatment of newcomers. The saturation already treats a newcomer the other manager never punished as unpunished.
- Rewards, which are impossible.
The AH analyses are observational on v1's simulated games, so the per-point numbers carry confounding with the design. The ranges are kept wide where that matters (k, horizon, budget).
