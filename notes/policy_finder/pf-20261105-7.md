# pf-20261105-7

## Explorations

1. **What do the reference sims score, and where does the payoff go?** Read
   plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/aggregates.csv. Under `zero` the mean
   contribution decays from 11.6 (round 0) to 5.5 (round 23); payoff per player
   26.6 -> 23.0, roughly 585 over 24 rounds. Under `humanlike` contributions
   rise 11.6 -> 13.2 and hold, but punishment is ~2-3 per player-round, which
   costs twice (player + pool), so payoff 21.1 -> 23.8, roughly 555 in total:
   below `zero`. Mean payoff per player = 20 + 0.6*mean(c) - 2*mean(p), so one
   point of punishment must buy 3.3 points of contribution to break even.
   So punishment must be efficient: rarely executed, targeted, and producing
   lasting contribution gains. Next: in the pilots, how does a player's next
   contribution respond to being punished, by how much and how it depends on
   the punishment size.
2. **What did the pilot managers do, and how did contributions move?** Looked
   at experiments/pilot_random1_player_round_slim.csv (contribution,
   punishment, payoff by experiment_name and round_number) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/01_pilot_overview.py and 02_punish_shape.py.
   Human managers (trail_rounds_2, 8 rounds) punish ~2.5/player-round, mostly
   free riders (c=0: 10.4 mean) but also 43% of the 14-19 contributors get
   something, and contributions sit flat at ~12.6. The random_1 rules punish
   the deficit to 20 steeply (c=0 -> 10.7, c in 5-9 -> 8.2, c=20 -> 0, never
   punish full contributors) and contributions rise from 10.6 to ~15.2 by
   round 11; their punishment falls from 7.2 (round 0) to ~2 later. So a
   "full contribution is safe, deficit is charged" schedule moves people up,
   but costs a lot early. Timeouts are rare (1-3%). Next: the per-player
   response, next contribution change vs this round's punishment and own
   contribution, in the pilots.
3. **How does the next contribution respond to punishment?** With
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/03_response.py: mean change c(t+1)-c(t)
   by own c(t) bin and punishment bin, pilots and reference sims. The
   response depends strongly on who is punished. Low contributors (c<=4) go
   +0.8 unpunished, +4.8 (sim) / +6.7 (pilots) after 12-30 points; c in 5-9:
   -0.0 -> +2.9 (sim). c in 10-14 gains only ~+1, c in 15-19 nothing or less,
   and punishing a full contributor backfires (-2.7 sim, -5 to -11 pilots).
   The linear fit on the humanlike sim: c(t+1) = 0.35 + 0.79 c + 0.18 mean(c
   others) + 0.055 p + 0.001 p^2, so a group-level shift persists at ~0.97
   per round. Rough accounting: punishing one free rider 15 points costs the
   group mean payoff 7.5 (2*15/4); a +4-5 jump for that player is +1.1 in the
   group mean, which persisting at 0.97 over ~20 rounds is ~15
   contribution-rounds = +9 payoff. Barely worth it early, not worth it late.
   So: punish only low contributors, steeply, early; never punish high
   contributors; stop toward the end. Next: local projections, the effect of
   p(t) on c(t+k) for k=1..6 and on the other players, to see persistence.
4. **Does the effect persist, and does it spill over?** Local projections in
   the humanlike sim (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/04_projection.py) and
   exact-contribution tables (05_exact.py). With linear controls for c, its lag
   and the others' mean, the own effect per point is near zero (0.004-0.03),
   others' punishment adds ~0.04 per point to each player: small. But the
   linear controls are crude; at exact c the picture is clearer: a c=5
   player unpunished goes to 4.6 next round (4.6 three rounds on), punished
   9-15 goes to 8.9 (9.3), with the others' mean 6.4 higher, worth ~1.2 by the
   peer coefficient 0.18; so ~+3 from ~12 points, ~0.25 per point, and it
   holds. c=0 players move ~0.1 per point (1.4 -> 5.5 at 15-30 points, half of
   it peers). c=15 players do not move; c=20 players drop 1-2 when punished.
   Reference scores (mean payoff summed over rounds): zero 589.3 +- 2.1,
   humanlike 550.6 +- 3.4. Humanlike wastes much of its punishment on c>=15
   (about a third of c=15 rows get punished). Break-even: one point costs 0.5
   of the round's mean payoff; at 0.25 points of contribution per point, worth
   0.0375 per later round, it needs ~13 effective rounds of persistence. So
   punishment pays only early, only on mid/low contributors, and the zero rule
   is a strong baseline. Next: a crude surrogate of the clones fitted on the
   reference sims, to rough-size the ranges of a deficit rule.
5. **Rough sizing on a surrogate.** Fitted a gradient-boosted 21-class model
   of c(t+1) on c, its lag, the others' mean (and lag), own and others'
   punishment (and lag), the round and own running mean, on both reference
   sims (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/06_surrogate.py), and played
   deficit rules p = s*max(0, thr-c) for rounds t < T against it
   (07_surrogate_sim.py, 1500 games, no timeouts). Zero scores 595 (real 589,
   close enough). Punishing all game long is a disaster (thr 20, s 2, T 23:
   419; thr 15, s 1, T 23: 523), though it lifts contributions to 14-17.
   Short early campaigns are about break-even or slightly better: thr 20,
   s 0.5, T 6: 598 +- 2; thr 15, s 1, T 6: 597; and their contribution gain
   persists (round 23 mean 9.2-9.4 vs 5.9 under zero) after the punishing
   stops. So the shape that can beat zero is: punish the deficit for a short
   opening window, softly, then stop and let the raised level coast. The
   surrogate's punishment effect is confounded (it learnt from the
   humanlike manager's choices), so this sizes the ranges, it does not pick
   values. Next: variants on the surrogate, punishing drops instead of
   levels, a relative threshold, a slope fading over the window.
6. **Variants and very short windows.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/08_variants.py
   and 09_short.py on the surrogate (3000 games, se ~1.3). Zero 596.6. The
   best shapes are the shortest, softest campaigns: thr 20, s 0.5 for the
   first 2-4 rounds scores 604-605 (+8); thr 15, s 1, T 2-3: 601-602. A
   slope of 1 or more, or a window of 6+, falls below zero again. Punishing
   drops (c(t-1)-c(t)) is worse than punishing levels (594 at best);
   a threshold relative to the others' mean (others + margin) is worse
   (590); a slope fading linearly to 0 over T is about as good as a hard
   window of half the length (fade 20/1/8: 599). A flat fine for anyone
   below 15 in the first 4 rounds: 597-601. So the gain lives in the
   opening: a cheap, deficit-proportional nudge while contributions are
   still high, before the decay sets in; afterwards the clones coast on
   the raised level (round 23 mean 8.2 vs 6.0). Since the surrogate's
   response may be weaker than the real clones' (pilot humans moved twice
   as much, entry 3), the window range must allow much longer campaigns.
   Next: whether capping the fine (free riders respond little per point)
   adds anything.
7. **Caps and a late phase.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/10_cap.py.
   Capping the opening fine (4-6 points) does not help (600-602 vs 603 for
   the uncapped thr 20, s 0.5, T 3). Adding a late phase that keeps fining
   whoever sits below 5 or 10 after the window is ruinous (543-561): the
   persistent free riders do not convert, they just keep paying. So no
   cap and no maintenance phase: the rule stops completely after its window.
8. **Drafted the rule.** Four parameters: `target` (int, the contribution
   below which a player is fined, the fine growing with the shortfall),
   `slope` (points per point of shortfall), `window` (int, number of opening
   rounds with fines) and `taper` (0 = full fine through the window, 1 =
   fine fading linearly to 0 by its end). punishment = slope *
   max(0, target - c) * (t < window) * max(0, 1 - taper*t/window). Ranges:
   target 10-20 (surrogate best 15-20, below 10 the fines miss the mid
   contributors who respond most); slope 0.1-3 log (0.1 is all but zero
   after flooring, the safe fallback; surrogate best ~0.5; pilots suggest
   real responses up to twice as strong, which could favour 1-2); window
   1-16 (surrogate best 2-4, but a stronger real response would move it
   later; past 16 every variant lost); taper 0-1 uniform (the surrogate was
   indifferent between a hard stop and a fade).
9. **Validated.** `validate-rule` passes: 256 distinct design points, the
   code runs at rounds 0, 11 and 23 on all of them. Left as drafted in 8.

## Key findings

1. Punishment costs the round's mean payoff twice its mean (mean payoff =
   20 + 0.6 mean(c) - 2 mean(p)), so a point of punishment must buy ~3.3
   contribution-rounds; the humanlike manager does not manage it (550.6 vs
   589.3 for zero) even though it holds contributions at ~13 (exploration 1, 4).
2. The response depends on who is punished: mid contributors (c ~5-10) gain
   ~0.25 points per point of punishment and keep it; free riders ~0.1;
   c=15 nothing; full contributors drop 1-2 when punished (exploration 3, 4).
3. Under zero, contributions decay steadily, 11.6 -> 5.5 over 24 rounds; a
   lift given early persists after punishing stops (surrogate: round-23
   mean 8.2 vs 6.0 after a 3-round campaign) (exploration 1, 5).
4. On a surrogate of the clones, punishing all game long loses heavily
   (419-536), while a short soft opening campaign, 0.5 points per point of
   shortfall below 20 for 2-4 rounds, beats zero by ~8 (605 vs 597)
   (exploration 5, 6).
5. Fining persistent free riders later in the game is ruinous (543-561 on the
   surrogate); caps, drop-based and peer-relative thresholds did not beat the
   plain deficit rule (exploration 6, 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager in this game spends its punishment only where and when it
can be repaid: at the opening, while the group still contributes ~11 and the
whole game lies ahead to collect the returns, it fines each player in
proportion to how far they fall short of a high target, never touching full
contributors, then stops for good and lets the raised level coast through
the clones' persistent, peer-following dynamics. The rule:
punishment = slope * max(0, target - c) * [t < window] * max(0, 1 - taper*t/window).
- `target` (int, 10-20): contribution below which a player is fined.
- `slope` (float, 0.1-3, log): fine per point of shortfall.
- `window` (int, 1-16): opening rounds with fines; none afterwards.
- `taper` (float, 0-1): fade of the fine across the window.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 4 say sustained punishment cannot pay, hence a finite
`window`; finding 3 says an early lift outlives the punishing, hence opening
rather than later. Finding 2 says the fine should follow the shortfall and
spare full contributors, hence `slope * (target - c)` with the fine zero at or
above `target`; finding 5 rules out a maintenance phase and a cap. I expect
`target` near 15-20, `slope` near 0.3-1 and `window` near 2-5 (finding 4); the
ranges extend well past these because the surrogate's punishment effect is
confounded by the humanlike manager's choices and the pilot humans responded
about twice as strongly (exploration 3), which would favour steeper and
longer campaigns; slope 0.1 floors to nearly no punishment, so the sweep can
also fall back towards zero. `taper` is a free shape choice the surrogate
could not decide. The hypothesis is contradicted if the sweep's best points
sit at slope 0.1 (punishment never pays against the real clones) or at
window 16 (punishment pays durably). Not modelled: timeouts (1-3%, the
manager cannot punish them anyway), the others' punishment as a deterrent
(spillover ~0.04 per point, too small), and any end-game effect, since the
rule is already silent long before the end.
