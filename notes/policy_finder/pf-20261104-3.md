# pf-20261104-3

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other in the reference sims?**
   Looked at per_round.parquet (group pool = 1.6*sum valid contributions -
   sum punishments, empty group = 0; group 0 is the first-named manager) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-3/ref_sims.py. Found that never punishing
   beats `ah` clearly: zero's group averages a pool of 59.1 against ah's 44.9
   (zero_vs_ah), and 61.8 vs 48.1 the other way round. Two channels: ah's group
   loses members at the first switch (4.0 -> 3.1 in rounds 4-7, ~3.3 after,
   zero's 4.9 -> ~4.6), and ah charges ~7 points of punishment per round, which
   comes straight off the pool. ah does raise contributions (~8.9-9.4 per
   member vs ~8.0 under zero), but 4 members * 1 point * 1.6 = ~6 pool points
   barely offsets the 7 it spends. So the bar is set by not punishing; any
   punishment has to buy more than it costs, and must not scare members away.
   Next: how the artificial humans' contributions respond to being punished,
   and what drives switching, to see if any punishment pays.
2. **Does punishment move the artificial humans' contributions, and does it
   drive them out?** Looked at ah-managed groups in the reference sims (next
   round's contribution by own contribution and punishment, same group,
   valid both rounds; leave rate at switches by punishment received in the
   4-round block) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-3/sim_response.py.
   Found a small, asymmetric response: a 0-contributor goes on to 1.7 when
   unpunished, 2.3 after 1-2 points, 2.6 after 3-5, ~3.0 after 6-30; a 1-4
   contributor 3.2 -> 3.6 / 3.9 / 4.4 / 5.0. High contributors go *down* when
   punished (20 -> 18.6 unpunished, 15.6 after >10). Leaving rises steeply
   with punishment received in the block: 22% unpunished, 25% (1-3 pts), 32%
   (4-8), 39% (9-15), 50% (16-30), 64% (>30); zero-managed players leave at
   24%. Observational (ah picks whom to punish) but the shape is clear: the
   response is concave, most of the gain comes from the first couple of
   points, and big punishments mostly cost pool and members. A 1-2 point
   punishment of a free-rider buys ~+0.6 next round (~1 pool point) for ~1.5
   points of pool, which only pays if the gain persists over several rounds.
   Next: the human data, for whom humans leave, and whether light/targeted
   managers did better than heavy ones.
3. **Which human managers got the biggest pools?** Looked at the human data
   (one copy per game, 50 games, 100 manager-games; pool per group and round,
   empty = 0) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-3/human.py. Found that by
   quartile of punishment per member per round, the pool is 55.2 (<=0.8 pts),
   **67.0 (0.8-1.5 pts)**, 47.3 (1.5-2.8), 38.1 (>2.8). The lightest punishers
   kept members (4.65) but contributions sagged (7.4); the light-but-real
   punishers held contributions at 10.4 and still kept 4.4 members; heavy
   punishers lost members (3.3) without higher contributions. Within a game
   the less-punishing group wins 56% of games, pool 59.7 vs 44.1. Human
   managers punished by relative standing: 10.6 pts for being >8 below the
   group mean, 4.6 for 4-8 below, 2.2 for 1-4 below, ~0.7 around or above.
   So my working hypothesis: a little punishment, aimed at whoever is clearly
   below the group, beats both none and a lot. 25 groups per quartile is
   thin, so the sim response matters more. Next: who humans leave, and
   whether the sims' contribution gain from punishment persists.
4. **Does the sims' gain from punishment persist, and who leaves?** Looked at
   ah-managed players punished in rounds 0/4/8/... who stay in the group for
   the next 3 rounds (contribution at t+1..t+3), and leave rates by block
   deviation from the group mean x block punishment, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-3/sim_persist.py. Found the gain
   persists and even grows: a 0-contributor goes 1.7/2.0/2.1 unpunished,
   2.3/2.6/2.8 after 1-2 pts, 2.8/3.1/3.5 after 3-5, 3.2/3.8/3.9 after >5; a
   1-4 contributor 3.2/3.4/3.6 vs 3.9/4.2/4.4 after 3-5. So 3 rounds of a 3-5
   point punishment's effect sum to ~3.4 contribution points (~5.4 pool) for
   ~4 points spent, 1-2 points buy ~2 points (~3.2 pool) for ~1.5 spent: the
   first points pay, the rest mostly don't. A contributor of 15-20 who is
   punished at all drops from 16.7 to ~15 for three rounds: never punish the
   top. Leaving: below-average players leave *less* (17% at >5 below vs 23%
   around the mean, unpunished), and with punishment everyone's leave rate
   climbs (to ~26-37% at 5-12 pts in the block, ~50% above 12). Under zero
   the low contributors leave more (29% at <=2 vs 18% at 15-20). So light,
   capped punishment of clearly-below-group members, nothing for the rest.
   The last round's punishment buys nothing (no future rounds), and late
   punishments buy little.
   Next: draft the rule and check what it would spend on the zero-managed
   histories, to set the ranges.
5. **Drafted the rule; what would it spend?** Rule: bar = ref_frac x mean
   contribution of the *other* valid members of my group this round;
   punishment = round(min(pmax, k x shortfall below the bar)), none from round
   t_stop on, none when alone. A relative bar because humans (and so the
   models trained on them) punished by standing in the group (exploration 3)
   and because a bar tied to the group never hits a whole low group at once,
   which would empty it. Rounding to nearest (not floor) so small k still
   gives 1-point taps. Replayed it on 300 zero-managed episodes (zero_vs_ah,
   group 0) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-3/rule_cost.py. Found the
   design spans the useful spectrum: (0.5, 0.15, 2, 20) spends 0.16 pts per
   group-round and touches 3% of members; (0.6, 0.3, 3, 21) 0.67 / 12%,
   almost all on 0-4 contributors; (0.8, 0.5, 5, 21) 2.1 / 24%; (1.0, 1.0,
   10, 23) 7.6 / 43%, ah's level, reaching 9-14 contributors too. ah spends
   ~7 per group-round. So the ranges include zero-like, light-targeted and
   ah-like points, and the sweep can tell them apart. Side note: in a bare
   python process torch raises KeyError '__import__' on the first
   keyword/dim reduction inside builtin-free exec (lazy import); validate-rule
   and the sim warm torch up first, so the script just warms up too.
6. **Hardened the code; validated.** Rewrote the code with elementwise ops,
   th.outer/th.matmul for the sums over players and th.maximum/th.minimum/
   th.relu in place of keyword clamps and dim-reductions, so it runs even in a
   cold torch (a hand-made case: others' mean 11.7, bar 9.3 at ref_frac 0.8,
   k 0.5, pmax 4 -> 4 points for the 0-contributor, 2 for the 5-contributor,
   0 for 10 and 20). rule_cost.py gives identical spending; validate-rule
   passes on all 256 design points.

## Key findings

1. Not punishing beats `ah` in the reference sims, pool 59.1 vs 44.9 (and
   61.8 vs 48.1 mirrored): ah spends ~7 pts per group-round for ~1 point more
   contribution per member and loses ~1.2 members to the other group (1).
2. Human managers who punished lightly (0.8-1.5 pts per member-round) had the
   largest pools, 67.0, against 55.2 for the lightest and 38.1 for the
   heaviest quartile; the lightest let contributions sag to 7.4 (3).
3. In the sims, punishing low contributors raises their contribution, with a
   concave dose response that persists for at least 3 rounds: a
   0-contributor's t+1..t+3 mean goes from ~1.9 unpunished to ~2.6 after 1-2
   pts and ~3.1 after 3-5, little more after >5 (2, 4).
4. Punishing high contributors backfires: 15-20 contributors drop from 16.7
   to ~15 for three rounds after any punishment (2, 4).
5. Leaving climbs with punishment received in the block, from 22% unpunished
   to 25% (1-3 pts), 32% (4-8), 50% (16-30) and 64% (>30), for all
   contributor types (2, 4); below-average players leave less when unpunished
   (17% vs 23%) (4).
6. Human managers punished by standing in the group: 10.6 pts at >8 below the
   group mean, 4.6 at 4-8 below, 2.2 at 1-4 below, <1 otherwise (3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here is a light, targeted punisher. It leaves everyone at or
near the group's level alone, so the group stays as attractive as a
never-punishing one and its good contributors are not discouraged, and taps
the clear free-riders with a few points, enough to pull their contribution
up for the following rounds but well below what would push them (and the
pool's budget) out. It stops punishing near the end, where a punishment can
no longer pay for itself in later contributions.

The rule: bar = `ref_frac` x the mean contribution of the other valid
members of the group this round; punishment = min(`pmax`, `k` x shortfall
below the bar), rounded to the nearest point, and nothing from round
`t_stop` on (nor for a member alone in the group).

- `ref_frac` (float, [0.3, 1.2]): how far below the others a member must be
  to be punished. I expect 0.5-0.9; above 1 it punishes average members,
  which findings 3-4 say does not pay, and the top of the range is there to
  let the sweep show that.
- `k` (float, [0.05, 2.0], log): punishment points per point of shortfall.
  I expect 0.2-0.6, giving a free-rider 1-4 points. At the low end (below
  ~0.05-0.1) almost nothing is ever punished, so the design contains the
  never-punish baseline; at 2 it is harsher than ah.
- `pmax` (int, [1, 12]): the cap per member and round. I expect 2-5, where
  the dose response flattens; up to 12 so the sweep can see the cost of more.
- `t_stop` (int, [12, 23]): the first round with no punishment. I expect
  19-22: a punishment needs a few later rounds to pay back; round 23 is
  never worth punishing, so 23 is the logical top.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the bar: the default to beat is near zero punishment, and
anything spent must come back as contributions or members. Finding 2
suggests a little punishment does better than none in human groups, and
finding 3 gives the mechanism in the sims: the first 1-5 points raise a
free-rider's contribution by ~0.7-1.2 per round for several rounds, worth
more in pool (x1.6) than they cost, while larger doses add little (hence
`pmax` and a modest `k`). Findings 4 and 6 give the relative bar and
`ref_frac` < 1: only those clearly below the group are worth punishing, and
that is the cue humans (on whom the models are trained) associated with
punishment. Finding 5 says each point also costs members, which again
argues for small doses; it is why the cap and a low `k` matter more than
being thorough. `t_stop` follows from finding 3's lag: the payback comes in
later rounds. It would contradict the hypothesis if the sweep's best points
sit at k near its floor (never punishing is best: the contribution gains of
3 are selection by ah rather than a response), or at high `ref_frac`/`pmax`
(the model rewards heavy deterrence). Not modelled: the switch rounds
specifically (leaving tracks the whole block's punishment, not the last
round, so a pre-switch amnesty seemed a weaker lever than the cap), a
player's history (newcomers, repeat offenders; the current round's standing
already carries most of it and 256 points are better spent on 4
parameters), and the other group's state (the rule cannot act on it, and
ah's group is what it is).
