# pf-20261104-7

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` (never punish) score on the stack I will face?** Looked at the reference
   sims' per_round.parquet (pool = 1.6*c - p summed per `agent_group`, the scoring of
   `pool_scores.against_anchor`) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/ref_sims.py. Found that
   `zero` beats `ah` against `ah`: pool 60.5 (se 0.95) vs 56.0 (se 0.99), and `zero` holds 4.57
   members on average against `ah`'s 4.00. Under `ah` the members contribute more (9.0 -> ~10.4 per
   player by the last block, vs 8.3 -> 8.2 under `zero`), but `ah` punishes ~3.2 per member early,
   ~1 late, and that cost plus the lost members outweighs the higher contributions. So punishment
   buys contribution but costs pool and members; the bar is "punish less than ah, and smarter".
   Next: the human data, how contributions respond to punishment and what makes players switch.

2. **How did human managers punish, and how did contributions respond?** Looked at the human data
   (deduplicated: 50 games, 9600 rows; columns contribution, punishment, player_no_input, group_id)
   with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/human.py. Found: mean contribution 9.2, mean punishment
   1.7 (29% of rows punished), falling from 3.4 in rounds 0-3 to 1.1 in rounds 20-23. Managers
   punished by level and by shortfall to the others: 4.4 mean for a 0 contribution vs 0.26 for 20;
   9.1 when 10+ below the others' mean, ~0.8 when at or above it. Next-round change (same group, no
   switch in between) by punishment received: a 0-contributor rises +0.97 unpunished, +4.4 after
   7-12 points; a 5-9 contributor -0.39 unpunished vs +2.1 after 7-12. Punishing high contributors
   backfires hard: a 20-contributor drops -1.8 unpunished but -10 to -20 when punished at all, a
   15-19 contributor -1.5 vs -6.9 after 7-12. A pooled OLS of the change on punishment gives only
   +0.033 per point (se 0.016), with contribution -0.26 (regression to the mean) and others' mean
   +0.21 (conformity). So punishment works only aimed at low contributors, and its one-round return
   (~+3 contribution = +5 pool for ~9 pool spent) does not pay unless it persists for several
   rounds or keeps others from sliding. Next: what makes players switch, since `zero` won on members.

3. **What makes players switch groups?** Looked at the five switch decisions in the human data
   (switch = group_id changes from round 4k+3 to 4k+4) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/switching.py. Found a 28.6% switch rate (43.5% at the first
   decision, ~24% later). It rises with punishment received over the last 4 rounds: 18.9% at 0,
   23% at 1-3, 34% at 4-8, 40% at 9-20, 55% above 20. High contributors (17-20 average) rarely leave
   even when punished (12% at 9-20 points), while low and middle contributors do (43-58%). A logit
   puts the player's own 4-round punishment at +0.046/point (se 0.006), the group's mean punishment
   per member at +0.089 (se 0.024: punishing others pushes everyone out), the other group's mean
   punishment at -0.126 (se 0.024: `ah`'s punishing makes my group attractive), own group's mean
   contribution at -0.147 (stay with cooperators) and the other group's at +0.066. So punishment
   drives free riders out, which costs my pool nothing, but it also scares the middle away; and
   `ah`'s own punishing is a pull I get for free as long as I punish clearly less. Next: is the
   contribution gain from punishment persistent, and does switching react to recent punishment more?

4. **Does the gain from punishing persist, and which rounds' punishment drives switching?** Looked at
   low contributors (0-9, rounds <= 18, staying in the group) k = 1..4 rounds later, and at a logit
   of switching on the switch round's punishment vs the three rounds before, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/persistence.py. Found the gain persists: unpunished low
   contributors move +0.1 at every horizon, those punished 5+ move +2.3 (t+1), +2.5, +2.6, +2.9 (t+4);
   for 0-4 contributors +3.2 vs +0.7 at t+3, for 5-9 +1.8 vs -0.2. (Confounded: punished players are
   often punished again.) Switching weighs the decision round's punishment ~3x the earlier rounds:
   +0.082/point (se 0.014) for round 4k+3 vs +0.028 (se 0.006) per point of the three before. So a
   sharp punishment of a low contributor is worth ~+2.5 contribution (= +4 pool) per round for several
   rounds, which pays back a ~6-9 point cost in 2-3 rounds when the player stays, but not near the end
   of the game, and punishing in a switch round (3, 7, 11, 15, 19) is the costliest for members.
   Next: what the artificial humans actually see, to check these levers exist in the sim.

5. **Do the artificial humans respond to punishment as the humans did?** (The AH model weights are
   not in this container, so the reference sims are my only view of them.) Looked at the `ah` vs
   `zero` runs of the reference sims with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/sim_response.py, and
   at where `ah`'s punishment points go (ah_vs_ah). Found the same direction, weaker: a 0-contributor
   moves +1.6 next round unpunished, +2.7 after 4-7, +2.8 after 7-12, +3.2 after 12-30; a 1-4
   contributor +0.67 unpunished, +1.1 after 1-2, +1.3 after 3-4, +1.7 after 4-7, +2.0 after 7-12;
   a 5-9 contributor +0.03 vs +0.4 / +0.7 / +0.7 / +1.3; at 10-14 nothing (-0.5 vs ~0). So the gain
   per punishment point is largest for small punishments (~0.2-0.3 contribution per point at 1-4
   points, ~0.1 per point above 7): diminishing returns. Punishing 15-20 contributors costs -0.5 to
   -3 more than not. The low-contributor gain persists: +2.4 at t+3 after 5+ points vs +0.6
   unpunished. Switching in the sim: 33.7% out of `ah`'s group vs 22.7% out of `zero`'s; by 4-round
   punishment 22.7% (0), 27% (1-3), 35% (4-8), 46% (9-20), 63% (20+); the switch round's punishment
   weighs +0.056/point vs +0.034 for the three before (a milder recency than the humans' 3x). `ah`
   spends 25% of its punishment points on players giving 10+ (where the gain is nil or negative)
   and 29% on 5-9. So `ah` loses to `zero` partly by wasting punishment on middling and high
   contributors and by punishing all game long. My working hypothesis: punish only clear low
   contributors, lightly (shortfall-proportional, small), stop well before the end since a gain
   needs rounds left to pay back, and go softer in switch-decision rounds; with the option to come
   out at almost no punishment, since `zero` already beats `ah` by 4.5.

6. **Drafted the rule.** punishment = rate * max(threshold - c, 0) for valid members, times
   switch_factor in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, and 0 from round t_stop on. Five parameters:
   threshold (absolute, because the sim's response table is by absolute level and the group mean
   sits at ~8-10 anyway), rate (log range so the sweep can reach ~zero punishment: below
   rate*threshold < 1 everything floors to 0), t_stop (int), switch_factor. That is four; I keep the
   fifth for an escalation on repeat offenders? Thinking about it: the persistence result says a
   punished player stays higher, so a repeat low contributor is one the punishment did not move, and
   punishing them again is less likely to pay; but the humans' managers escalated. Not enough
   evidence either way; I leave it out and stay at four parameters, which also keeps the 256-point
   design denser.

7. **Validated the draft and checked the spending it implies.** `validate-rule` passes (256
   distinct design points). With scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-7/cost_check.py I applied the
   rule statically to the contributions seen under `zero` (no feedback): threshold 8, rate 0.5,
   t_stop 16, switch_factor 0.3 spends 0.56 per member-round and punishes 24% of rows; 6/0.3/16/0
   spends 0.11 (11% punished); 10/1/20/0.5 spends 2.7 (49%), about `ah`'s level; 5/0.2/12/0 spends
   0.03, practically `zero`. So the ranges span from `zero` to beyond `ah`, and the sweep can find
   where the cheap, targeted middle lies. I expect it in the low-spending part (0.2-1 per
   member-round), since `zero` beats `ah` by 4.5 pool points and each punishment point costs one
   pool point for sure but returns only ~0.1-0.3 contribution per round.

## Key findings

1. In the reference sims `zero` beats `ah` against `ah`, 60.5 vs 56.0 pool (se ~1), and holds 4.57
   members to `ah`'s 4.00, though `ah`'s members end up contributing ~1-2 points more (exploration 1).
2. Human managers punished by level and shortfall (4.4 at a 0 contribution, 0.26 at 20); punishing a
   low contributor raised their next contribution (0-contributor +4.4 after 7-12 points vs +1.0
   unpunished), while punishing a 15-20 contributor cut it by 5-20 points (exploration 2).
3. Switching rises with punishment received (19% unpunished to 55% above 20 points over 4 rounds)
   and with the group's mean punishment of others (+0.089 logit per point); the other group's
   punishment keeps players away from it (-0.126) (exploration 3).
4. The gain from punishing a low contributor persists for at least 4 rounds (+2.3 to +2.9 vs ~+0.1
   in humans; +2.4 vs +0.6 at t+3 in the sim), and the switch round's punishment drives switching
   more than earlier rounds' (humans 0.082 vs 0.028 per point, sim 0.056 vs 0.034) (explorations 4, 5).
5. In the sim the response is weaker and has diminishing returns: about 0.2-0.3 contribution per
   punishment point at 1-4 points, ~0.1 per point beyond 7; nothing at 10-14, negative above 14;
   `ah` spends 25% of its points on players giving 10+ (exploration 5).
6. The rule's ranges span spending from ~0.03 (practically `zero`) to ~2.7 per member-round
   (`ah`'s level) (exploration 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here punishes little and only where it pays. Punishment costs the pool one point per
point for sure, drives members (and with them pool) to the other group, and buys back only a
fraction of a contribution point per point; it pays only when aimed at clear low contributors, kept
small (diminishing returns), dealt early enough for the persistent gain to accumulate over the
rounds left, and kept out of the rounds right before switch decisions. Against `ah`, which punishes
broadly, a light-handed manager also attracts the players `ah` drives out.

The rule: punishment = rate * max(threshold - c_t, 0) for valid members of my group, multiplied by
switch_factor in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, and zero from round t_stop on.

- `threshold` (float, [2, 14]): the contribution below which a member is punished. Expected best
  ~5-10, since the sim shows a gain up to 9 and none from 10.
- `rate` (float, [0.05, 3], log): punishment points per point of shortfall. Expected ~0.2-1
  (a 0-contributor gets 1-8 points); the low end reproduces `zero`, the high end exceeds `ah`.
- `t_stop` (int, [4, 23]): first round with no punishment. Expected ~12-20: a gain of ~1.5
  contribution (2.4 pool per round) needs several rounds to repay a punishment of a few points.
- `switch_factor` (float, [0, 1]): multiplier in the rounds before a switch decision. Expected
  low (0-0.5), since that round's punishment weighs 1.6x (sim) to 3x (humans) on switching.

### Justification

Finding 1 shows broad punishment loses to none at all on this stack, which is why the rule's whole
design is about spending less and better than `ah`, and why `rate`'s log range reaches down to
effectively zero. Findings 2 and 5 show the gain exists only below ~10 and turns negative above 14,
which grounds `threshold` and its range [2, 14], and show the diminishing returns that argue for a
small `rate` proportional to the shortfall rather than a flat heavy punishment. Finding 4 (the gain
persists) is why early punishment can pay and why it should stop before the end: `t_stop`. Findings 3
and 4 (punishment, especially in the switch round, pushes players out, and `ah`'s punishing pushes
them to me) ground `switch_factor`. What would contradict it: a sweep peak with large `rate` and
high `threshold` (meaning the AH respond more strongly than the reference sims suggest), or no
pattern in `t_stop`/`switch_factor` (meaning timing does not matter in the sim). Not modelled:
escalation on repeat offenders (the evidence on whether a second punishment pays is confounded),
thresholds relative to the group mean (in this game the mean sits at 8-10, so an absolute threshold
is nearly equivalent and simpler), punishing to steer group composition (the pool counts totals, so a
low contributor leaving costs little either way), and the other group's state, since the
contribution responses I measured do not depend on it in any way I could isolate here.
