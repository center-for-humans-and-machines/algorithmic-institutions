## Explorations

1. **What the score really measures, and how far `zero` already gets.** Read
   `src/aimanager/simulation/pool_scores.py` and scored the reference sims
   (`plots/simulation/25_LEVIN_run1_ah_zero_pairings_batched/per_round.parquet`)
   with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-7/ref_sims2.py. The score is the
   group's *total* pool, sum over members of 1.6c - p (the parquet's
   `common_good` is per capita, a trap). `zero` (never punish) leads `ah` by
   +13.97 (se 1.44): own 60.5 vs 46.5, and members 4.57 vs 3.43. Most of the
   lead is size: at the first switch (after round 3) `zero`'s group jumps to
   4.9 members and keeps ~4.65 to the end, while its per-member contribution
   (8.1-8.3) is *lower* than ah's group (8.8-9.3). So punishment lifts
   contributions a bit but costs members and pool. Every member adds >= 0 to
   the pool unless punished, so I want everyone in my group. Next: which
   inputs the artificial humans' switch and contribution models read, and
   in the human data how punishment moves contributions and switching.

2. **How humans respond to punishment, and what makes them leave.** Looked
   at `experiments/2group_8agent_50ep.csv` (one copy per game, 50 games:
   contribution, punishment, group_id, payoff) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-7/human.py. Next-round change in
   contribution: a player giving 0-5 rises +0.3 unpunished, +1.5 at 3-6
   punishment, +2.8 at 6-10, +3.7 above 10; at 6-10 contribution +0.8
   unpunished vs +0.9-1.2 punished. Punishing players who already give 11+
   is followed by drops of 1-11 points (partly regression to the mean, but
   no sign of a gain). Switching (28.6% of decisions): 19% for players
   unpunished in the block, 36% at 1-3 mean punishment per round, 45% at
   3-6, 58% above 6. A logit over decisions: own block punishment +0.12/pt,
   own-group minus other-group mean punishment +0.24/pt (so even bystanders
   flee a punishing group), own minus other group mean contribution
   -0.10/pt, own minus other group size -0.08/member. Human managers
   punish 3.4-3.7/member in rounds 0-3, then ~1-1.7. So: punishment works
   only on low contributors, and its visibility at group level is what
   costs members. Next: whether the artificial humans in the sims
   reproduce these two responses (that is what my rule faces).

3. **Do the artificial humans behave like the humans?** Same analysis on the
   ah-managed groups of the zero-vs-ah reference sims
   (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-7/sim_response.py). Yes, closely.
   Contribution 0-5: next-round change +0.8 unpunished, +1.3 at 1-3, +1.7 at
   3-6, +2.3 at 6-10, +2.8 above 10; 6-10 contributors gain +0.1 to +0.9;
   punished 11+ contributors drop. Switching: 23% unpunished, 28% at 0-1,
   38% at 1-3, 50% at 3-6, 65% above 6; logit own punishment +0.12/pt,
   group punishment gap +0.10/pt, contribution gap -0.13/pt, size gap
   -0.12/member. Out of a zero-managed group 22.7% switch per decision vs
   33.7% out of ah's; at the first decision 27% vs 50% (ah punishes hardest
   in rounds 0-3). Rough arithmetic: a punishment of 3 on a 0-5 giver costs
   3 pool now and buys ~+0.9 contribution (= +1.5 pool) next round, so it
   pays only if the rise persists ~2+ rounds and it costs no members. Next:
   how persistent the rise is, and whether bystanders react.

4. **How lasting is the rise, net of regression to the mean?** Regressed the
   change in a member's contribution k rounds later on its punishment,
   controlling for its contribution, the others' mean contribution and the
   others' mean punishment, ah-managed groups of the reference sims
   (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-7/persistence.py). The raw tables of
   2-3 overstated it: per punishment point a 0-5 giver rises only +0.064
   (k=1), +0.070 (k=4), +0.056 (k=6), +0.041 (k=12); for givers above 5
   +0.01; others' punishment has no effect on bystanders (op ~0). So one
   punishment point buys ~0.1 pool per remaining round, and pays its own
   cost only with ~10 rounds left, *before* counting members it drives out.
   Conditional cooperation is strong instead: others' mean contribution
   +0.21 (k=1) to +0.50 (k=12) per point. So I now think `zero` is very hard
   to beat, and the only candidate gain is a light, early, narrow
   punishment of persistent near-zero givers (few people punished, so a
   small group-level punishment signal), stopped once too few rounds
   remain to pay it back. The rule must be able to switch itself off so the
   sweep can fall back to `zero`. Next: draft that rule.

5. **Drafted the rule and checked what it would touch.** Four params: `thr`
   (free-riding cut-off), `amount` (flat punishment per round for a flagged
   member; below 1 it floors to 0, so the low end of its log range is the
   `zero` manager, ~20% of the design as a built-in baseline), `persist`
   (consecutive free-riding rounds in my group needed before punishing, so
   one bad round or a newcomer's first round is spared) and `t_stop` (no
   punishment after this round). Tested it on a toy history and on the
   zero-vs-zero sims' histories (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-7/check_rule.py):
   it flags the right players, and the share of members it would flag per
   round is 12% / 7% / 5% at thr 0 (persist 1/2/3), 25% / 17% / 12% at thr
   2, 44% / 34% / 28% at thr 5, 56% / 45% / 38% at thr 8. Because givers
   above 5 hardly respond (+0.01/pt, entry 4), thr 8 would mostly add cost
   and approach ah's own wide punishment, so I narrowed thr from [0, 8] to
   [0, 6]. validate-rule passes (256 distinct points).

## Key findings

1. The score counts the group's total pool, so members matter as much as
   contributions: `zero` leads `ah` by +13.97 (se 1.44), mostly by holding
   4.57 members to ah's 3.43, despite lower per-member contributions
   (exploration 1).
2. Punishment drives people out, both the punished and bystanders: in the
   sims switching rises from 23% (unpunished) to 50% (3-6 per round) and
   65% (above 6), with +0.12 logit per own punishment point and +0.10 per
   point of group-level punishment gap; humans show the same (+0.12,
   +0.24) (explorations 2, 3).
3. Net of regression to the mean, a punishment point raises a 0-5 giver's
   contribution by only ~0.06-0.07 for several rounds (0.04 after 12), and
   givers above 5 by ~0.01; bystanders do not respond to others' punishment
   (exploration 4).
4. Conditional cooperation is strong: others' mean contribution moves a
   member's by +0.2 to +0.5 per point, and a higher group contribution keeps
   members (-0.13 logit per point of gap) (explorations 3, 4).
5. Restricting to persistent near-zero givers keeps the number punished
   small: thr 0-2 with persist 2-3 flags 5-17% of members per round, vs
   ~45% at thr 8 (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here mostly does not punish: members are worth more than the
small contribution gains punishment buys, and every visible punishment pushes
members toward the other group, while `ah`'s own punishing sends members
toward mine. If anything beats pure abstention it is a light, narrow, early
punishment of persistent near-zero givers, the only players whose
contributions respond, stopped once fewer rounds remain than it takes to
repay the cost (~10). The rule: a member of my group who contributed at most
`thr` in each of the last `persist` rounds, all in my group, gets `amount`
this round, as long as the round is at most `t_stop`.

- `thr` (float, [0, 6]): free-riding cut-off; expected best 0-3.
- `amount` (float, [0.5, 16], log): punishment per round to a flagged
  member; below 1 it floors to 0 (= `zero`). Expected best below 1 (off) or
  1-3.
- `persist` (int, [1, 4]): consecutive free-riding rounds needed; expected
  best 2-3.
- `t_stop` (int, [3, 23]): last round with punishment; expected best
  around 8-14 if punishing at all.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the bar (+14 by not punishing) and says members are the main
lever; findings 2 and 4 say punishment and low group contribution both cost
members, and finding 3 says a point of punishment returns ~0.1 pool per
remaining round, so it can only break even with ~10 rounds left: hence
`t_stop` (range over the whole game, expected best mid-game) and `amount`
(log range whose low fifth is the `zero` manager, so the sweep can fall back
to the baseline instead of being forced to punish; its peak at the low end
would mean "never punish" and not a range cut short). Finding 3 (only 0-5
givers respond) bounds `thr` at 6; finding 5 and the bystander cost in
finding 2 motivate `persist`, which narrows punishment to habitual free
riders and spares newcomers. What would contradict it: sweep points with
amount >= 1 and wide thr beating the amount < 1 points, which would mean the
artificial humans' contribution response is bigger in my group than the
ah-managed regressions show. Not modelled: punishment relative to the group
mean (conditional cooperation suggests it, but it widens who is punished,
and finding 3 says only low givers respond), timing within a switch block
(the switching model is recurrent and I could not isolate a recency effect
from the data), and reacting to the other group's punishment or size (my
only lever is my own punishment, and the best response to ah punishing is
still not to punish).
