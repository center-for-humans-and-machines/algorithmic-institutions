# pf-20261104-4

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other in the reference sims?** Looked at
   `per_round.parquet` of the 25_LEVIN reference sims with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-4/ref_sims.py, computing each group's pool
   (1.6*sum c - sum p, empty group = 0) per round and episode. Found that never
   punishing beats `ah`: zero's group pools 59.1 (vs ah 44.9) and 61.8 (vs ah 48.1),
   while ah-vs-ah is 54/58 and zero-vs-zero 50/52. The zero group grows to ~4.6
   members (ah's shrinks to ~3.4) from the first switch on. Per member, ah's group
   contributes more (31.9/3.4 = 9.4 vs 37.0/4.6 = 8.0) but pays ~6 pool points a round
   in punishment. So size is a big lever, and ah's punishing costs it both directly and
   through exits; next I want to know how much punishment actually raises contributions
   and what makes players leave, in the human data.

2. **How do humans respond to punishment, and what drives leaving?** Looked at the
   deduplicated human data (50 games, 9600 rows; `contribution`, `punishment`,
   `group_id`, `payoff`) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-4/human.py. Found:
   (a) next-round contribution change rises with punishment for low contributors
   (c<=5: +0.3 unpunished, +1.3 at p 2-5, +3.7 at p>10) but collapses for high
   contributors who get punished (c>15: -1.8 unpunished, -9.3 at p 5-10); a linear fit
   gives only +0.10 contribution per punishment point. (b) At switch points 29% leave;
   leaving rises with the player's mean punishment over the last 4 rounds (19% at 0, 36%
   at 1-3, 59% above 6) and with the group's mean punishment exceeding the other
   group's (53% when more than 2 above, 18-23% when below or equal), and with a payoff
   deficit to the other group (52% at <-5 vs 15% at >+5). (c) Human managers punish
   mostly low contributors (4.8 at c=0, 0.3 at c=20), and mostly early (3.9 in rounds
   0-3, ~1.2 by the end). So one punishment point buys maybe 0.1-0.3 contribution
   points next round (worth 0.16-0.5 pool) while costing a full pool point and raising
   exits; punishment looks net negative unless very targeted. Next: which features the
   artificial humans' switching and contribution models see, since they are what the
   rule faces.

3. **Do the artificial humans respond to punishment like the humans?** The models
   themselves are not in the container, so I looked at the `ah`-managed groups in the
   reference sims (`per_round.parquet`, ~300k player-rounds) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-4/sim_response.py. Found: a zero contributor rises
   by +1.68 next round unpunished (zero-managed groups: +1.61, so this is mean
   reversion, not selection) but by +2.39 at p 1-2, +2.57 at p 3-5, +3.15 at p>10; three
   rounds later the gap persists (+2.49 vs +3.29 at p 1-2, +4.18 at p>10). For c 1-5
   the same shape (+0.68 vs +1.08 ... +2.62). For c>15 punishment only hurts (-1.4
   unpunished, -3.8 at p>10). So the effect is strongly concave: the first 1-2 points
   buy ~0.7 contribution that lasts several rounds (≈ 1.1 pool/round for ~3+ rounds
   against 1-2 points spent), larger amounts buy little more. Leaving: 26% unpunished
   at the decision round vs 33% at p 1-2, 42% at p 3-5, 65% at p>10 in that round; with
   no punishment in the decision round, the 4-round mean still matters but less (22%
   at 0, 32% at 1-3). Unpunished members do not leave because others were punished
   (21-24% whatever the group's punishment total). So punishing in the decision round
   (rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19) is the most expensive time to punish, and large
   punishments are wasteful. Next: does a punishing group deter joiners?

4. **Do joiners avoid a punishing group?** Looked at players in the zero group at
   decision rounds vs the `ah` group's punishment total with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-4/sim_join.py. Found join rate 26.5% when the other
   group punished 0 in the decision round, flat to 8 points, then 22.7% (8-15) and
   17.7% (>15); over the 4-round block flat up to 30 points, 18% above 60. Joining is
   driven much more by the target group's contribution total (17% at <=20 to 47% at
   >50). So small, targeted punishment barely deters joiners, and a high pool attracts
   them, a virtuous circle. Drafting the rule: punish only contributors below a
   threshold, in proportion to their shortfall, only in the first rounds of each 4-round
   block after a switch (never in the decision round unless the sweep wants it), and
   stop near the end where there is no future to buy.

5. **Drafted the rule and checked it on a toy history.** Wrote
   configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261104-4.yml: a member of my group who contributed
   (valid) and gave less than `thr` this round gets a flat `amount`, but only in the
   first `m` rounds of each 4-round block and only up to round `t_stop`. Flat rather
   than proportional to the shortfall, because entry 3 says the gain is concave in the
   punishment: the first 1-2 points do most of the work, so grading by shortfall would
   mostly spend more on the worst contributors for little extra. A first version used
   `th.remainder(t, 4)`; on my toy run (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-4/test_rule.py)
   it failed with `KeyError: '__import__'` (torch imports lazily inside remainder, and
   the rule has no builtins), although validate-rule had passed, presumably because
   something had already imported it there. Replaced it with `t - 4*floor(t/4)`, which
   is plain arithmetic. The toy run now punishes exactly the valid zero contributor in
   rounds 0, 1, 4, 20 (thr 5, amount 2, m 2, t_stop 20) and nobody in rounds 2, 3, 19,
   21, 23, nor the timed-out player or the one at c = thr. validate-rule passes
   (255 distinct points of 256).

6. **Rough budget.** In the `ah` group about a third of member-rounds are below 5
   (entry 3 counts: ~79k of ~258k non-switch member-rounds at c<=5, 31%). With
   4-5 members, thr 5, amount 2 and m 2 the rule spends ~1.5-2 points per active round,
   ~1 per round on average, against ~6 for `ah` (entry 1). If each punished low
   contributor gains ~0.7 contribution for ~3 rounds (entry 3), that is ~3.4 pool points
   per 2 spent, before exits; an extra ~7 percentage points of leaving among punished
   low contributors (entry 3) costs little, since they give little. It is a close call,
   which is why amount=1 and a low thr (only zero contributors) are in the ranges, and
   why m=1 (punish only right after a switch) is allowed.

## Key findings

1. Never punishing beats `ah` in the reference sims: the zero group pools 59.1 and 61.8
   vs `ah`'s 44.9 and 48.1, because it grows to ~4.6 members while `ah`'s shrinks to
   ~3.4 and `ah` spends ~6 pool points a round on punishment (exploration 1).
2. Punishment raises a low contributor's contribution, concavely: for c=0, +0.7 at p 1-2
   and only +1.5 at p>10 over the unpunished +1.7, still visible three rounds later; for
   c>15 it lowers contributions (-3.8 at p>10 vs -1.4) (exploration 3; humans the same
   shape, exploration 2).
3. Being punished in the decision round drives leaving the most (26% unpunished, 33% at
   p 1-2, 65% at p>10); with no punishment in that round, the 4-round mean matters less
   (22% at 0, 32% at 1-3) (exploration 3; humans 19% to 59%, exploration 2).
4. Unpunished members do not leave because others are punished (21-24% at any group
   total), and joiners are deterred only by heavy group totals (>8 in the decision
   round, >60 over a block), while they are drawn strongly by a high contribution total
   (17% to 47%) (explorations 3, 4).
5. Human managers punish mainly low contributors and mainly early (3.9 in rounds 0-3,
   ~1.2 at the end) (exploration 2).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes little and with precision: a small, flat punishment to the
members who contribute (almost) nothing, dealt right after a switch so that it has
worn off by the next switch decision, and never at the end of the game, where it can
no longer pay back. That buys the persistent part of the contribution gain at the
lowest cost in pool points and exits, keeps the group's pool high, and a high pool
draws joiners, which grows the pool further. High contributors are never punished.

The rule (configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261104-4.yml):
- `thr` (int, 1-12): members contributing less than `thr` are punished. I expect 2-7:
  the gain is largest for c=0-5, nil for c 5-10, negative above.
- `amount` (int, 1-8): the flat punishment. I expect 1-3, where the gain per point is
  largest; up to 8 lets the sweep test whether a stronger deterrent pays.
- `m` (int, 1-4): punish in the first `m` rounds of each 4-round block (4 = always). I
  expect 1-2, keeping the decision round (and the one before it) clean; 4 tests the
  opposite.
- `t_stop` (int, 11-23): the last punished round. I expect 17-21: a gain lasting ~3
  rounds needs a few rounds left; 23 lets the sweep reject the idea.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 4 say group size and the pool feed each other and that only the
punished themselves (and heavy totals) drive members off, so punishment must be
small and targeted: hence a flat `amount` with a low range and a `thr` that spares
the cooperators (finding 2's harm above 15 caps `thr` at 12). Finding 2's concavity
argues for a flat small amount over a shortfall-proportional one. Finding 3 is the
reason for `m`: the same points cost more members when dealt in the decision round.
Finding 2's persistence of ~3 rounds, and that punishment in round 23 can only cost,
are the reason for `t_stop`; finding 5 shows human managers also front-load. What
would contradict it: if the sweep's best points sit at `amount`=1 with `thr`=1 or
`t_stop` low, punishment does not pay at all on this stack and the zero manager is the
answer; if `m`=4 wins, the decision-round exit effect is weaker than the contribution
effect. The sim response numbers are correlational (the `ah` manager chose whom to
punish), although the zero-managed baseline (+1.61 vs +1.68 for unpunished zero
contributors) suggests little selection. Not modelled: reacting to the other group
(its size or punishment), each player's history (repeat offenders, who might deserve
escalation), and timeouts beyond not punishing them; each would add parameters to a
256-point design for an effect I could not measure here without the models.
