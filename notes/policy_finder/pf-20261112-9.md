# pf-20261112-9

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and never-punish (`zero`) fare against each other, and what is the score exactly?** Looked at the reference sims (`per_round.parquet`) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-9/ref_sims.py and ref_scores.py, and read `simulation/pool_scores.py`. The score is the group's *total* pool, sum over members of 1.6*c - p, averaged over rounds (not the per-capita `common_good` column, which is pool / valid members). `zero` leads `ah` by 13.97 (se 1.44) in pool: own 60.5 vs 46.5, mean members 4.57 vs 3.43. Per member `zero` is actually worse: mean contribution ~8.0 and drifting down (8.6 -> 8.1) vs `ah`'s ~9.0, while `ah` pays ~1.8 punishment per member per round. So group size dominates: one extra member is worth ~1.6*8 = 13 pool points, while punishment buys at best ~1 point of contribution per member. Next: what drives switching in the AH switching model and in the human data, and does punishment raise contributions enough to ever pay for itself?

2. **What makes human players leave a group?** Looked at the human data (one copy per game, 50 games) at the 5 switch decisions with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-9/human_switch.py, using the 4-round block before each decision. Overall switch rate 28.6%. It rises steeply with the punishment the player itself received (mean/round 0: 19%, 1-3: 36%, 3-6: 45%, >6: 58%) and with how much more its group punished than the other group (diff < -2: 13%, ~0: 28%, > +2: 59%). Logit: own punishment +0.11/pt, group-vs-other punishment gap +0.21/pt, own contribution ~0. So every point of average punishment above the other manager's costs members; since `ah` punishes ~1.8/member/round, a manager that punishes clearly less than `ah` should draw players from it. The relative gap matters more than the own level. Next: does punishment buy contributions at all, in humans and in the sims?

3. **Does punishment buy contributions in humans, and whom did human managers punish?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-9/human_contrib.py, next-round contribution change of players who stayed, by contribution and punishment bin. Low contributors (0-4) rise +0.5 unpunished, +1.6 after 2-5 points, +3.7 after >10 (part of it regression to the mean); 5-9 contributors: -0.3 unpunished vs +1.1 after 2-5. Punishing high contributors backfires hard: 20-givers drop -1.8 unpunished but -9.4 after 2-5 points. Human managers did punish mainly the low/below-group-mean givers (7.1 for 8+ below the group mean, 0.85 near it), and fell from ~3.5/round in the first block to ~1.1 later. So at best ~1 point of contribution (1.6 pool) per ~2-3 punishment points (pool cost 2-3): punishment roughly breaks even or loses within the round, before counting departures. Next: the same responses in the simulated players (ah's group in the reference sims), and what drives simulated switching.

4. **The same responses in the simulated players.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-9/sim_response.py on the `ah`-managed groups of the reference sims (all three pairings with `ah`). Contribution response is weaker than in humans: 0-4 givers rise +1.08 unpunished, +1.92 after 2-5, +2.90 after >10; 5-9 givers +0.12 vs +0.56; 15-20 givers drop more when punished (-1.7 -> -2.0 at 2-5, -4.6 at >10). So one round of punishment buys ~0.25 contribution per point (0.4 pool for 1 point of pool cost). Switching (logit at the decisions): own punishment +0.10/pt, group-minus-other-group mean punishment +0.11/pt, own group's mean contribution -0.12/pt (cooperative groups keep members), other group's mean contribution +0.09/pt, group size -0.15/member (big groups keep members). `ah`'s groups switch out at 31%, `zero`'s at 23%. But across pairings punishment does pay per capita in the long run: ah_vs_ah late-game contribution ~10.4 vs zero_vs_zero ~7.4 (per member 1.6*10.4-0.9 = 15.7 vs 11.8 pool). Against `ah` the zero group per member is no worse (~13.9 both) and wins on size. So the target is a group that punishes clearly less than `ah` on average (to win members) while still keeping its contribution level up, which points to cheap, narrowly targeted punishment of free riders only: their own punishment pushes them (the low contributors) out toward `ah`, which also lowers `ah`'s mean contribution, while the group-average punishment the others see stays small. Next: write the rule.

5. **Can I simulate locally?** No: `artifacts/` holds only the linear baselines and copula calibrations, not the GNN contribution/switch weights, so the rule cannot be played here. I design from the analyses above and give the sweep ranges that include "never punish" (the known +14 baseline) as well as punishment heavier than `ah`'s.
6. **Drafted the rule.** Shortfall punishment against a relative free-rider line: a member (valid, in my group) giving less than `frac` x the group's mean contribution this round gets `rate` points per point of shortfall, at most `cap`, and nobody is punished from round `t_stop` on. Relative line because human managers punished below-group-mean givers (exploration 3) and because it never touches the high givers whose contributions collapse when punished (explorations 3, 4). `cap` bounds the own-punishment that drives individual exits; `t_stop` lets the sweep find whether punishing pays only early (norm setting while groups are still 4 and 4, as human managers did, ~3.5/round in block 1 vs ~1.1 later) or never/always; in the final round punishment cannot pay back at all. Validated: 256 distinct design points pass.
7. **How much punishment does that range imply?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-9/rule_load.py applies the rule statically to the contributions of the `zero`-managed groups (no response modelled). 34% of member-rounds are 0-4 givers. With cap 10: frac 0.5/rate 1 gives 0.23 per member-round (12% punished), frac 0.8/rate 2 gives 1.35, frac 1.0/rate 4 gives 3.48 (48% punished); frac near 0 or rate below ~0.3 gives essentially nothing (punishments are floored). So the ranges span from "never punish" through `ah`'s ~1.8/member-round to roughly twice it. I expect the best near 0.2-0.8 per member-round: frac 0.4-0.8, rate 0.5-2, cap 3-10, t_stop either early (8-12) or 24.

## Key findings

1. The score is the group's total pool (sum of 1.6*c - p over members), so group size dominates: `zero` leads `ah` by 13.97 (se 1.44) with 4.57 vs 3.43 mean members, despite contributing ~1 point less per member (exploration 1).
2. Players leave groups that punish them and that punish more than the other group: in humans switch rate goes 19% -> 58% from no own punishment to >6/round, and 13% -> 59% across the group-vs-other punishment gap; the simulated switch model shows the same (+0.10 and +0.11 logit per point), plus it keeps members in groups with higher mean contribution (-0.12/pt) and larger size (-0.15/member) (explorations 2, 4).
3. A round of punishment buys little immediate contribution in the simulated players (~0.25 contribution per punishment point among low givers; humans ~0.3-0.4), and punishing high givers costs contributions (20-givers fall -4.6 after >10 points in sims, -9.4 after 2-5 in humans) (explorations 3, 4).
4. Over the whole game punishment does sustain contributions: ah_vs_ah groups rise to ~10.4 while zero_vs_zero groups fall to ~7.4, ~15.7 vs ~11.8 pool per member late on; against `ah`, `zero`'s per-member pool is no worse (~13.9) and its edge is all size (exploration 4).
5. Human managers punished below-mean givers (7.1 for 8+ below the group mean vs 0.85 near it) and front-loaded punishment (~3.5/round in rounds 0-3, ~1.1 later) (exploration 3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager against `ah` wins mainly on members: it must keep its group's average punishment well below `ah`'s (~1.8 per member-round early, ~1 later) so that players flow towards it, while not letting contributions slide as they do under no punishment at all. The way to have both is cheap, narrowly targeted punishment: only clear free riders, relative to what the group gives, in proportion to their shortfall, capped, never the high givers, and only while it can still pay back. Pushing free riders out is no loss: they add little to my pool and lower `ah`'s mean contribution, which the switch model reads as a reason to leave `ah`'s group.

The rule: a member giving less than `frac` x the group's mean contribution this round gets `rate` x the shortfall, at most `cap`, and no one is punished from round `t_stop` on.
- `frac` (float, [0, 1.2]): the free-rider line relative to the group mean; 0 reproduces `zero`.
- `rate` (float, [0.1, 4], log): punishment points per point of shortfall; below ~0.3 almost nothing is dealt.
- `cap` (float, [1, 30], log): the most one member gets in a round.
- `t_stop` (int, 4..24): first round without punishment; 24 punishes throughout.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 2 say punishment that is visible at group level costs members, the dominant term, so the rule punishes only below a relative line (finding 5: the line human managers used) and the ranges reach down to "never punish" (`frac` 0 or small `rate`), the known +14 baseline. Finding 4 says some punishment keeps contributions and so the pool and attractiveness of the group up, so the ranges also reach punishment as heavy as `ah`'s and beyond (exploration 7: up to ~3.5/member-round), letting the sweep locate the trade-off; I expect it at 0.2-0.8 per member-round (frac 0.4-0.8, rate 0.5-2). `cap` follows from the own-punishment effect on exits (finding 2) and the weak, saturating response of free riders (finding 3), log-scaled because its effect is in ratios at the low end. `t_stop` follows from finding 5 and from punishment not paying back late in the game (finding 3, no switches after round 19); a full integer range because I cannot tell whether early norm-setting or none is better. What would contradict this: a sweep peak at the top of `frac`/`rate` (heavy punishment pays despite exits) or a flat landscape at "punish nobody". Not modelled: reacting to `ah`'s actual punishment level or to the switch calendar (e.g. sparing members right before a decision), and punishing persistent free riders harder from their history; with 256 points I kept to four parameters, and these are candidates for a later version.
