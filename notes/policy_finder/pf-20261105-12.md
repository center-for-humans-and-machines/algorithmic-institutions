# pf-20261105-12

## Explorations

1. **How do the two reference managers score, and why?** Looked at
   `plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/per_round.parquet` (payoff, contribution,
   punishment, contribution_valid) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-12/ref_sims.py.
   `zero` scores 589.3 (se 2.1), `humanlike` 550.6 (se 3.4). Under zero the mean
   contribution starts at 11.4 and decays steadily to 5.3 in round 23; under
   humanlike it climbs to about 13.2 by round 8 and stays flat. Timeouts are rare (1.9%).
   The score is exactly `24 * (20 + 0.6 * mean c - 2 * mean p)`: humanlike gains
   0.6 * 5.3 = 3.2 per round in contributions but pays 2 * 2.41 = 4.8 in punishment.
   So punishment works on the artificial humans (it lifts and holds contributions),
   but the human-like manager buys it ~1.5x too dearly. Next: where its punishment
   points are spent and which of them actually move contributions.

2. **Which punishments move the artificial humans?** Same file, ref_sims.py and
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-12/response.py: next-round change in contribution by
   (own contribution, punishment) under humanlike. Humanlike spends a lot on players who
   already contribute a lot (mean p 1.05 at c 15-19, 0.29 at c=20, and these are 50k of
   the 94k valid rows) and there the response is negative (c 19-20: dc -0.75 unpunished,
   -0.94 at p=1, -2.7 at p>12). For low contributors any punishment helps, and the first
   point does most per point: at c 1-4 dc is +0.22 unpunished, +0.80 at p=1, +1.66 at
   p 3-5, +4.1 at p>12; at c 5-8, -0.06 vs +0.96 (p=1) vs +1.75 (p 3-5). Mid
   contributors (9-12) gain ~+0.9 from a small punishment, then flat. A linear fit gives
   c_{t+1} = 0.18 + 0.755 c + 0.217 others_mean + 0.07 p (others' punishment +0.025), and
   the effect persists: still +0.063 per point at t+4. The group-level persistence is
   0.755+0.217 = 0.97, so a lift in one player's contribution leaks into the others and
   decays slowly. So: punish only players below a standard, gently (a few points),
   proportional to the shortfall, never the high contributors. Next: the human pilots,
   whose random_1 rule managers give less confounded punishments.

3. **Do the human pilots say the same?** Looked at
   `experiments/pilot_random1_player_round_slim.csv` (contribution, punishment,
   player_no_input, payoff) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-12/pilots.py. random_1
   (49 groups, 16 rounds, random rule managers) punishes heavily early (7.2 in round 0,
   falling to ~2) and contributions climb from 10.6 to 15.3; payoffs per round go from 12
   to 25.6, above the zero manager's AH level late on. Its regression puts the effect at
   0.20 per point (vs 0.07 in the AH under humanlike; 0.09 for human managers). Low
   contributors respond strongly (c<=4: +1.1 at p 1-2, +2.2 at p 3-5, +7.1 at p>10);
   punished full contributors in trail_rounds_2 collapse (-3.9 to -11.9). Both pilots
   show a last-round dip (random_1 15.3 -> 14.6). So the picture is consistent: a
   shortfall-based punishment of low contributors works, hitting high contributors
   backfires, and its value falls as the game nears its end. The AH's per-point
   response is weaker than the humans', so the rule has to be thrifty. Next: an
   accounting of what a punishment point must buy to pay off.

4. **What must a punishment point buy?** Arithmetic on the score from entry 1. One
   point on one player costs the group 2 points (0.5 on the per-player mean), and
   each extra contributed point is worth 0.6/4 = 0.15 on the mean. So a point pays
   only if it raises the group's summed future contributions by 3.33. With the linear
   effect of entry 2 (0.07-0.09 per point, group persistence 0.97) one point buys about
   0.09 * 15 = 1.4 over 20 rounds: linear, proportional punishment does not pay on the
   AH. What can pay is the step from 0 to 1-2 points on a low contributor (+0.6 to +1.0
   next round in entry 2's table), which buys ~10 over the remaining rounds if it
   persists, and it needs at least ~4 rounds left to break even. So: a small fixed
   punishment for falling below a standard, at most a mild slope on the shortfall,
   and none in the last few rounds. The AH's inputs (prev contribution, punishment,
   validity, an RNN; configs/training/artificial_humans/contribution/script_21_no_grid.yml)
   hold no round number, so it has no end-game of its own: the cutoff is pure horizon
   accounting. Next: a rough surrogate to see the rule's shape in numbers.

5. **A surrogate of the AH (dead end, mostly).** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-12/surrogate.py
   (linear fit with step and sqrt punishment terms, mean rollout) collapses to c=2.7
   under zero (the real sim: 5.3), so its rankings are worthless. surrogate_np.py
   resamples c_next from reference-sim rows with the same own c, others'-mean bin and
   punishment bin (no memory, confounded by the humanlike manager's choices). Its zero
   scores 599 (real 589), and over 80 rules at 150 games each (se roughly 5-8) the top
   ones are flat punishments with no slope: T=15, b=2, k=0 at 606/603; T=12, b=1, k=0
   at 598; T=15, b=1 at 595. Slopes of 0.6 per point of shortfall cost 20-60 points.
   Too noisy to trust beyond the direction: flat small punishments are about break-even
   or slightly better, and steep ones lose. memory.py: under humanlike, the punishments
   other players get do not hold up unpunished high contributors (dc -0.53 with none
   dealt to others, -1.17 with >20), so there is no visible bystander effect to exploit.

6. **Drafted the rule.** For each valid player: if t < 24 - L and c_i < T, punish
   b + k * (T - c_i), else 0 (the manager floors and clamps). Four parameters: the
   standard T, the base b (the cheap signal step that does most of the work), the
   slope k (kept, but with a range topping out at 0.6 since steep slopes lose), and the
   no-punishment tail L. A b below 1 with k=0 is the zero manager, so the sweep can
   fall back to it if punishment does not pay. I did not add a relative standard
   (punishing below the group mean): entry 2 shows the response depends on the own
   level, and a uniform low group is exactly the one a relative rule would let decay.

7. **Validated and set the ranges.** `validate-rule` passes: 256 distinct design points.
   Ranges: T in [2, 20] (expect 10-15: below ~12 the AH still responds to a small
   punishment, above ~15 it responds negatively, entry 2); b in [0, 4] (expect 1-2;
   below 1 with small k it is no punishment at all, so the zero manager is inside the
   design); k in [0, 0.6] (expect near 0-0.2, entries 4-5); L integer 0-16 (expect 3-6
   from the break-even horizon in entry 4; up to 16 so the sweep can test punishing only
   in the first third, as random_1's front-loaded punishment did in entry 3).

## Key findings

1. The score is 24 * (20 + 0.6 * mean c - 2 * mean p): zero scores 589.3 and humanlike
   550.6, because humanlike's lift in contributions (13.1 vs 5.3 by round 23, +3.2 per
   round) costs 4.8 per round in punishment (entry 1).
2. Punishing high contributors backfires on the AH (c 19-20: dc -0.75 unpunished vs
   -2.7 at p>12), and humanlike spends 13% of its points on players at c>=15 (entry 2).
3. For low contributors the first point does most per point: at c 5-8 dc is -0.06
   unpunished and +0.96 at p=1, +1.75 at p 3-5 (entry 2); the human pilots agree in
   direction (entry 3).
4. The linear per-point effect on the AH is only 0.07-0.09 next round, with group-level
   persistence 0.97; a point must buy 3.33 summed future contributions to pay, so
   proportional punishment does not pay, and even a cheap one needs ~4 rounds left
   (entry 4).
5. A rough resampling surrogate ranks flat small punishments (b 1-2, T 12-15, no slope)
   at or slightly above zero and steep slopes 20-60 points below (entry 5); noisy.
6. The AH has no round input, so there is no end-game of its own; the last-rounds cutoff
   is horizon accounting only (entry 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager on these artificial humans punishes rarely and cheaply: a small, fixed
punishment for a player who falls below a contribution standard, perhaps a mild extra
per point of shortfall, never anything for players at or above the standard, and
nothing in the final rounds, where a punishment has no time left to pay back its double
cost. The aim is to stop the zero manager's steady decay (11.4 to 5.3) at a fraction of
the human-like manager's cost. The rule: if t < 24 - L and c_i < T, punish
b + k * (T - c_i), else 0.
- T, the contribution standard, float, [2, 20].
- b, the base punishment for any shortfall, float, [0, 4].
- k, the extra punishment per point of shortfall, float, [0, 0.6].
- L, the final rounds without punishment, int, 0-16.

### Justification

Finding 1 says punishment does move the AH but humanlike overpays, so the gain must
come from spending less per unit of lift. Finding 2 sets the standard T: punishing above
~15 lowers contributions, while below ~12 players still respond, so T's range spans both
sides and the best is expected at 10-15. Finding 3 motivates b, the step that buys the
most per point, and finding 4 together with 5 keeps the slope k small (range up to 0.6,
expected near 0-0.2). Finding 4's break-even horizon and finding 6 motivate L (expected
3-6). The design includes the zero manager (b < 1, k ~ 0), so if no punishment pays on
the AH, the sweep can find that. What would contradict the hypothesis: the sweep's best
points at high k or high T, or a flat landscape in b with zero on top, which would mean
the first-point effect in finding 3 is a confound of the human-like manager's choices
(it is not randomised in the reference sims). Not modelled: timeouts (1.9% of rows, a
timed-out player cannot be punished, and none of the effects hinged on them), a
standard relative to the group (the response depends on the own level, finding 3),
reacting to trends or past punishments (memory.py found no usable effect), and a
punishment cap separate from b + k * T (with k <= 0.6 the slope already bounds it).
