# pf-20261104-11

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other in the reference sims?**
   Looked at per_round.parquet (pool = 1.6*sum c - sum p per group and round,
   empty groups 0, 1000 episodes per pairing) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/ref_sims.py. Found `zero` beats `ah`
   clearly: 61.8 vs 48.1 (zero as group 1) and 59.1 vs 44.9 (mirrored). The
   gap comes from size: after the first switch the zero group holds 4.9
   members vs 3.1, settling at 4.55 vs 3.45. Per member the ah group
   contributes more (9.8 vs 8.5) and pays 5.9 points of punishment per round.
   But in ah_vs_ah the pool grows over the game (43.6 in rounds 0-3 to 60.9
   in 20-23) while in zero_vs_zero it decays (53.9 to 48.4). So punishment
   does lift contributions over time in these artificial humans, yet `ah`'s
   way of punishing costs it members and pool. Next: what exactly makes
   artificial humans switch, and how contributions respond to a punishment.
2. **What makes players switch?** Looked at each player's switch decision
   (group in round 4k vs 4k-1) against the mean punishment they got in the
   block before, their mean contribution and their payoff minus the other
   group's mean payoff, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/switching.py
   (ah_vs_ah sims, 40000 decisions; humans, 2000). Found the own punishment
   in the block is the strongest lever: switch rate 0.21 at 0 points per
   round, 0.24 at 0-1, 0.31 at 1-3, 0.42 at 3-6, 0.53 at 6-10, 0.66 above 10
   (humans: 0.19, 0.24, 0.36, 0.45, 0.51, 0.67, nearly identical). The
   payoff gap to the other group works the same way (0.59 at <-6, 0.13 at
   >+6). High contributors switch less (0.19 at 15-20 vs 0.39 at 0-5), and
   within a contribution band the punishment effect holds. So every point of
   punishment is paid twice: once in the pool and once in the chance the
   member leaves, and members are what the pool is made of (each member who
   gives c adds 1.6c). A mild 0-1 per round costs only +3 points of switch
   probability, though. Next: how much does a punishment raise the next
   contribution, to weigh against these costs.
3. **How does a punishment move the next contribution?** Looked at
   c(t+1)-c(t) of players who stayed, by punishment at t and contribution at
   t, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/response.py (ah_vs_ah sims;
   humans). Found a modest, graded push for low and middle givers: at c in
   1-5 the next change is +0.67 unpunished, +1.07 at 1-2 points, +1.33 at
   3-4, +1.60 at 5-7, +1.82 at 8-12 (humans +0.08, +0.54, +1.23, +1.29,
   +2.66); at c in 6-10, -0.05 vs +0.17 to +0.71. For givers above 10
   punishment backfires: at c=20, -1.70 unpunished vs -3.35 at 8-12 and
   -4.51 above 12 (humans -1.84 vs -9 to -20). Zero givers rebound anyway
   (+3.8 unpunished, partly timeouts). So a punishment of p buys roughly
   +0.1 contribution per point next round (+0.16 pool per point) for a
   p-point pool cost now: a single-round trade is a clear loss, it only pays
   if the lift persists over many rounds and does not drive the player out.
   Never punish the high givers. Next: is the lift persistent; look at the
   artificial humans' model inputs.
4. **How do `ah` and the human managers punish?** Looked at punishment by
   contribution, by contribution minus the group mean and by block, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/ah_policy.py (ah_vs_ah, valid
   players; humans, valid players). Found `ah` is graded but blunt: mean 4.99
   at c=0, 2.51 at 4-6, 1.45 at 10-12 and still 0.79 at 16-19, with 31% of
   10-12 givers and 21% of 16-19 givers punished. Humans are sharper on the
   relative gap: 10.6 for 8+ below the group mean (ah 4.65), 0.6-0.9 for at
   or above the mean (ah 0.84-1.49). Both punish most in rounds 0-3 (ah
   3.44, humans 3.56) and decay to ~1.1 by rounds 20-23. So `ah` spends much
   of its punishment on middle and high givers, where entry 3 says it backfires
   and entry 2 says it drives them out. A rule that punishes only clear free
   riders, relative to the group, and spares everyone else should keep the
   lift of entry 1 at a fraction of the cost. Next: does a punishment's lift
   persist over the following rounds.
5. **Does the lift persist, and does it spill over?** Regressed
   c(t+k)-c(t) on p(t) with c(t), c(t-1), group mean and round as controls,
   stayers only, rounds 0-16, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/persistence.py (ah_vs_ah). Found for
   givers at 0-5 a lift of +0.046 per point at k=1 and still +0.050 at k=4,
   +0.036 at k=6; for 6-10, +0.038 rising to +0.062 at k=4; for 11-20 none
   (-0.017 to +0.002). Roughly linear in the dose (k=4: +0.08 at 1-2 points,
   +0.33 at 5-7, +0.65 at 8-12, +1.17 at 13+). Unpunished members gain
   +0.009 per point dealt to the others (about +0.027 for three others). So a
   point of punishment buys about 0.075 contribution per remaining round,
   0.12 pool per remaining round: it pays back only with roughly 8+ rounds
   left, before counting departures. The ah_vs_ah vs zero_vs_zero gap (56 vs
   51 at equal sizes, for 7.4 points per round) says the compounded return
   is larger than this, about 1.7 pool per point. So punish early, taper to
   nothing well before the end. Next: whether switching reacts to when in
   the block the punishment came.
6. **When in the block does punishment drive switching?** Regressed the
   switch decision on the punishment in each of the block's four rounds plus
   the block's mean contribution, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/switch_recency.py. Found strong
   recency: +0.0030, +0.0065, +0.0083, +0.0123 switch probability per point
   in block rounds 0..3 (sims); humans +0.0054, +0.0041, +0.0072, +0.0178.
   A point dealt just before a decision costs about 4x the members of one
   dealt right after a switch. Rounds 20-23 come after the last decision and
   cost no members at all, but they also leave too few rounds for the lift
   to pay back (entry 5). So the timing within the block is a lever of its
   own: hold back in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19.
7. **Drafted the rule.** From entries 1-6: punish only members who give
   clearly less than the rest of the group (relative, as humans do, since
   high givers react badly and are the ones the group must keep), in
   proportion to the shortfall, tapered linearly to zero over the game, damped
   in the round before each switch, and capped so no single member is hit
   hard enough to leave. Five parameters: `margin` (how far below the
   others' mean before punishing), `slope` (points per point of shortfall),
   `horizon` (round at which the taper reaches zero), `pre_switch` (multiplier
   in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19), `cap` (most points per member per round).
   The reference is the mean of the other valid members this round; a
   timed-out player is neither counted nor punished. Ranges are wide enough
   to contain near-`zero` behaviour (slope 0.05 with margin 8 punishes almost
   nobody), so the sweep can fall back to it if punishing does not pay.
8. **Replayed the draft offline, and a code fix.** Ran the rule on the
   zero group's recorded histories of zero_vs_ah (300 episodes, no feedback,
   so only the dose is meaningful) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-11/replay_dose.py. The first draft
   crashed there with `KeyError: '__import__'` although validate-rule had
   passed: in a fresh process `th.clamp(x, min=...)` and
   `x.sum(-1, keepdim=True)` trigger a lazy import inside torch, which the
   builtin-free scope cannot serve. I rewrote the code with `th.maximum`,
   `th.minimum`, `sum(-1).unsqueeze(-1)` and `t - 4*floor(t/4)` instead of
   `th.remainder`; it now runs in both. Doses per group and round: a middle
   point (margin 3, slope 0.5, horizon 20, pre_switch 0.3, cap 8) deals 2.3
   in round 0, falling to 0 by round 18, almost all of it to givers at 0-5
   (0.29 per member-round) and none to givers above 15; a mild point deals
   0.8 down to 0; a harsh corner (margin 0, slope 1.5, horizon 40,
   pre_switch 1, cap 30) deals 14 down to 4, more than `ah`'s ~6. So the
   design spans from almost `zero` to beyond `ah`, and the sweep can settle
   where the trade of entries 2, 5 and 6 balances.

## Key findings

1. `zero` beats `ah` (61.8 vs 48.1, and 59.1 vs 44.9 mirrored) because it
   holds 4.55 members against 3.45; yet at equal sizes punishing wins
   (ah_vs_ah pools grow from 43.6 to 60.9, zero_vs_zero decay from 53.9 to
   48.4). (Exploration 1)
2. A member's switch probability rises with the punishment they got in the
   block: 0.21 at none, 0.31 at 1-3 per round, 0.42 at 3-6, 0.66 above 10,
   the same in sims and humans. (Exploration 2)
3. Punishment lifts low and middle givers by about +0.05 per point per
   round, persisting at least 4-6 rounds, plus +0.009 per point to each
   other member; it backfires on givers above 10 (c=20: -1.7 unpunished vs
   -3.4 to -4.5 punished). (Explorations 3, 5)
4. `ah` punishes bluntly: 1.45 on average to 10-12 givers and 0.79 to 16-19
   givers, where humans punish mainly members 4+ points below the group
   mean (4.6 to 10.6). (Exploration 4)
5. Switching reacts mostly to the latest punishment: +0.012 per point in the
   round before a decision vs +0.003 in the round right after one (humans
   +0.018 vs +0.005). (Exploration 6)
6. A point of punishment pays back only with roughly 8+ rounds left in the
   per-point estimate, so its value falls over the game. (Exploration 5)

## Hypothesis

A good manager keeps the `zero` manager's advantage, members, and adds
part of `ah`'s, a contribution norm that stops the decay. It punishes only
clear free riders, relative to what the rest of the group gives this round,
in proportion to how far they fall short, never the middle and high givers
who carry the pool and react badly; it punishes most at the start, when the
lift has the most rounds to pay back, and tapers to nothing before the end;
it holds back in the round just before each switch decision, when a
punishment drives members out most; and it caps the blow to any one member.

The rule: a member's shortfall is max(0, mean of the other valid members'
contributions this round - `margin` - own contribution); punishment =
round(min(`cap`, `slope` x shortfall x max(0, 1 - t/`horizon`) x d)), d =
`pre_switch` in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 and 1 otherwise; timed-out players
and members with no valid peer get none.

- `margin` (float, uniform [-2, 8]): tolerance below the others' mean.
  Expected best around 2-5.
- `slope` (float, log [0.05, 3]): points per point of shortfall. Expected
  best 0.3-1.
- `horizon` (float, uniform [6, 40]): round at which the taper reaches 0.
  Expected best 14-26.
- `pre_switch` (float, uniform [0, 1]): multiplier before switch decisions.
  Expected best 0-0.4.
- `cap` (float, log [1, 30]): most points to one member per round. Expected
  best 4-10.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the frame: membership decides the comparison with `ah`, but
punishment, done right, still raises the pool per member over time; so the
rule must punish far less, and better aimed, than `ah`. Findings 3 and 4
give the target and `margin`: only low givers respond, and humans start at
about 4 below the group mean, so the best tolerance is likely 2-5; the range
runs from -2 (punish nearly everyone below the mean) to 8 (almost only
zero-givers among givers of 8+), which also gives the sweep an almost
`zero` corner. Finding 3's linear dose response and finding 2's roughly
linear exodus make a proportional `slope` the natural form; a log range
0.05-3 covers from almost nothing to over twice `ah`'s dose (entry 8), with
the best likely under 1 since each point also costs switch probability.
Finding 6 gives `horizon`: with per-point payback needing ~8 rounds, the
punishment should end around round 14-18, but the compounded gain in
finding 1 (~1.7 pool per point) argues for later, so 6-40 leaves room for
both an early stop and an almost flat schedule. Finding 5 gives `pre_switch`:
a point there costs four times the members, so I expect it near 0, with
[0, 1] letting the sweep say otherwise. `cap` follows finding 2's top bins
(0.53-0.66 switch rates above 6 points per round): a cap of 4-10 keeps any
single member from being pushed out, while 30 means no cap.

What would contradict this: a sweep peak with tiny `slope` and large
`margin` (punishing does not pay at all against `ah`, `zero` is optimal),
or a peak at `margin` -2 with high `slope` (the artificial humans reward
broad punishment). I chose not to model: an absolute contribution floor
(the relative reference handles both high- and low-cooperation groups and
humans use it), the players' punishment history or repeat offending (the
lift persists, so re-punishing every round is already the dose), newcomers
after a switch, and the other group's state (its payoff matters for
switching, but the rule controls it only through its own punishment,
which it already keeps low). Rounds 20-23 need no special case: no switches
follow, and the taper already brings punishment down there.
