## Explorations

1. **How does never punishing fare against `ah` on this stack?** Looked at the reference sims (per_round.parquet, common_good, group sizes, contributions, punishments per group and round) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-10/ref_sims.py. Found `zero` beats `ah` by +1.17 (zero in seat 0) and +1.27 (seat 1), so about +1.2 pool points, while ah vs ah is -0.46 (seat noise ~0.25). The zero group grows to ~5.1-5.2 members vs ~3.8 for ah after the first switch, its members contribute ~8.0 vs ~9.2 in ah's group, and ah punishes ~3.5/player early, ~1-1.5 late. So ah's punishment buys ~1 point of contribution per player (worth 1.6) but costs ~2-3.5 in pool per player and drives members away. Baseline to beat: zero ~ +1.2. Next: how the artificial humans' contribution and switching models respond to punishment, to see if any targeted punishment can pay.
2. **How do human contributions respond to punishment?** Looked at the human data (one copy per game, 50 games; contribution, punishment, next-round contribution within a block) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-10/human_data.py. Found that for low contributors (0-5) next-round change rises with the punishment: +0.29 unpunished, +0.68 at 1-2, +1.26 at 3-5, +2.41 at 6-10, +3.68 above 10. For 6-10 contributors: -0.44 unpunished, +0.6 to +1.3 at 1-5, then no further gain. For contributors of 11+ punishment does nothing good, and for full (20) contributors it is a disaster (-5.5 to -16 vs -1.8 unpunished). Relative to the group the same: those 5+ below the others gain +0.8 unpunished vs +1.7 at 3-5. Human managers punished mostly 0-contributors (9 in rounds 0-3, ~3 later) and tapered over the game (3.6 -> 1.2 mean). So the useful lever is a small punishment (~2-5) on clear free riders only, never on high contributors; the gain is ~1 point of contribution per punished free rider (1.6 pool), against a pool cost equal to the punishment, so it pays only if the gain persists more than a round or two. Next: what drives switching, since group size seemed to dominate entry 1.
3. **Correction to 1: the score is the total pool, not `common_good`.** Read src/aimanager/simulation/pool_scores.py: the score sums 1.6*c - p over the group's members per round (an empty group 0), so group size counts directly; `common_good` in the parquet is the per-member share. So ah-style punishment does raise the long-run pool on this stack once nobody can flee from it; against ah it loses because members flee to the non-punishing group. Members are worth ~1.6*8 = 13 pool points each per round, so membership is the dominant lever; contribution is the second one. Next: in the sims, how switching and contributions of the artificial humans respond to the punishment a player got.
4. **Switching drivers in the human data.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-10/switching.py, per player and 4-round block. By own group's mean punishment minus the other group's: 0.13 when 2+ lower, 0.28 equal, 0.59 when 2+ higher. 6. **What makes an artificial human leave, by timing and by whom is punished.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-10/sim_leave_logit.py, logit of leaving at a switch over player-blocks of the reference sims. Own punishment matters more the closer it is to the switch: per point 0.010 in the block's first round, 0.025, 0.040, 0.052 in the round just before the decision. 7. **Does the contribution gain last?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-10/sim_persist.py: OLS of contribution k rounds later on this round's punishment, controlling own contribution, group mean and round, ah groups of the ah/zero pairings, players staying put. Per punishment point +0.027 (k=1), +0.024, +0.048, +0.034, +0.043 (k=6), +0.058 (k=8), a bit more for <=5 contributors. The effect does not fade over 8 rounds (selection of stayers inflates it somewhat). 8. **Hypothesis taking shape, drafted the rule.** Being less punishing than ah wins members (~+14 for zero). On top of that, small fixed punishments on clear free riders only, early in each block (not just before a switch), and stopping some rounds before the end, should add contributions (and via the group-contribution term, members) at little membership cost. Small rather than graded: the response of 0-contributors is concave (+0.7 at 1-2 points, +0.9 at 3-5, +1.35 at 6-10 over unpunished, entry 5) while leaving rises about linearly. Drafted 4 int params: `thr` (punish c < thr), `amt` (flat amount), `quiet` (no punishment in the last `quiet` rounds of a block before a switch), `t_end` (no punishment after this round).

## Key findings

1. The score is the total pool (1.6*sum c - sum p over members), so members dominate: never punishing beats `ah` by ~+14 (+14.24 / +13.69 by seat), mostly by holding 4.6 members to ah's 3.4 (exploration 3).
2. Punishment does raise the long-run pool when no one can flee it: ah vs ah pools rise 40 -> 61 (contributions 8.7 -> 10.4) while zero vs zero decay 56 -> 48 (8.7 -> 7.4) (exploration 3); the per-point effect on a player's own contribution lasts at least 8 rounds (~+0.03-0.06/pt, exploration 7).
3. The gain sits with free riders and is concave in the amount: an artificial 0-contributor rises +1.65 unpunished, +2.36 at 1-2 points, +2.53 at 3-5; 6-10 contributors gain only ~0.2-0.4; high contributors lose more when punished (exploration 5, same in the human data, exploration 2).
4. Leaving rises with own punishment (0.22 unpunished -> 0.46 at 3-6/round), with groupmates' punishment (+0.093/pt) and falls with the other group's punishment (-0.123/pt) and with the own group's contribution lead (-0.104/pt); a punishment in the round just before a switch weighs 5x one in the block's first round (0.052 vs 0.010 per point) (explorations 4-6).
5. Late punishment cannot repay: its gain is ~0.08 pool per point per remaining round directly, against 1 point of pool spent now (exploration 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager against `ah` stays clearly the less punishing group, so members drift to it, and spends punishment only where it buys most: small flat amounts on clear free riders, timed right after a switch decision rather than right before one, and not in the last rounds where no future contributions can repay it. The higher contributions then also hold and attract members through the group-contribution term of switching.

Rule: each valid member of my group contributing less than `thr` this round gets `amt` points, except in the last `quiet` rounds before each switch decision (rounds 0-19) and after round `t_end`.
- `thr` (int, [1, 12]): free-rider line. Expect 3-8: below 5 is where the response is large, 6-10 gains little, and punishing 11+ backfires; 1 means zeros only.
- `amt` (int, [1, 8]): flat amount. Expect 1-3, as the response is concave and leaving linear; 8 is above ah's own mean on zero-contributors (5.8) as an outer check.
- `quiet` (int, [0, 3]): quiet rounds before a switch. Expect 1-2, from the rising weights of late-block punishment on leaving; 3 = punish only the first round of each block.
- `t_end` (int, [3, 23]): last punishing round. Expect 15-20 (stop around the last switch); 3 = first block only (close to zero), 23 = never stop.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the frame: anything that loses members to ah is expensive (a member is worth ~13 pool/round), so the rule never punishes contributors above `thr` and uses small `amt` (findings 3, 4). Finding 2 says punishing is not pointless on this stack, so a targeted dose may beat zero; finding 3 locates it on free riders and in small amounts (`thr`, `amt`). Finding 4's timing weights motivate `quiet`, and the groupmate and contribution-lead terms say targeting few players with small amounts keeps the group-level punishment, which pushes everyone out, low. Finding 5 motivates `t_end`. The hypothesis is contradicted if the sweep's peak sits at thr=1, amt=1, t_end=3 (i.e. zero is best and any punishment loses members faster than it gains contributions), or if `quiet` shows no effect (the switching model then ignores timing). Not modelled: graded punishment by shortfall (concave response favours flat), thresholds relative to the group mean (the pool value of a contribution is absolute and a relative line would spare an all-free-riding group), reacting to the other group's punishment or sizes, and punishing persistence (repeat offenders) — each would cost a parameter in a 256-point design for an effect I could not size from the data here.
