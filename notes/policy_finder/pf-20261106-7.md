# pf-20261106-7

## Explorations

1. **What does the game look like in the human data?** Looked at contribution, punishment and group sizes per round with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-7/explore1.py (50 games after deduplication). Mean contribution is flat around 8.2-9.7 over all 24 rounds; human managers punish 3.4-3.7 per player in rounds 0-3, then about 1-2. Group sizes after the first switch are spread from 1 to 8 almost uniformly. So the group size is the dominant lever on the pool: a group of 8 contributing 9 makes ~115 per round, a group of 4 ~58. Next: what makes players leave.

2. **What drives switching?** Logit of switching at each decision on block averages (explore2.py). Switch rate 0.29 (0.44 at the first decision). Own mean punishment in the block +0.12 per point, own group's mean punishment minus the other group's +0.24 per point, own group's mean contribution minus the other's -0.10 per point, group size difference -0.08 per member; own contribution irrelevant. Players punished ~7/round leave at 50% vs 18% for unpunished. So punishment is a strong push out of the group, and it is judged *relative to the other manager*; a cooperative group holds people. Next: does punishment buy contributions that offset this?
3. **Does punishment buy contributions?** Next-round contribution change by own contribution and punishment received, for stayers (explore3.py). For contributors at 0-4, the change is +0.5 unpunished, +1.6 at 3-5 points, +2.3 at 5-10, +3.7 above 10; at 5-9 similar but smaller (+1.5 at 5-10). For high contributors (15-20) punishment backfires hard (-6.7 at 5-10 vs -1.7 unpunished). OLS: +0.09 per punishment point, controlling own level and the gap to the group. Human managers punished by the gap to the others (0.9 at no gap, 6.8 at gap > 10). So punishment should go only to low contributors and never to cooperators; next whether the gain persists and whether manager styles differ in outcome.

4. **Which human manager styles got the largest pools?** Per (game, group) averages, styles by mean punishment quintile (explore4.py). Pool: none 53, low 51, mid (1.1-1.7/player/round) 77, high 36, very high 43. Mean contribution 7.7 / 8.5 / 11.2 / 9.0 / 9.4; mean size 4.5 / 4.2 / 4.6 / 3.4 / 3.4. A group punishing more than 1.2 above the other group holds 2.7 members vs 4-5.3 otherwise (n = 4 - 0.26 * dp). Per-capita contribution hardly depends on size. So there is a sweet spot: mild punishment lifts contributions by ~3 points without losing members; heavy punishment shrinks the group. Noisy (100 group-games). Next: how the mid style punished.

5. **How did the mid-style managers punish?** explore5.py. Mid managers gave 3-6 points to contributors at 0-4, 2.6 at 5-9, <1 above 9, and front-loaded it: 2.5/player in rounds 0-3, ~1 from round 12 on. Their groups climbed from 9.5 to 12.7 per player while "none" stayed at 7-8. Size stayed ~5-5.5. So: targeted, mild, front-loaded punishment of free riders. Next: does the effect persist (it must, for punishment to pay for itself)?

6. **Does the effect persist, and does it pay?** explore6.py, contributors at 0-9, contributions 1-4 rounds later. For 0-4 contributors: unpunished 2.6/3.0/3.2/3.3 at t+1..t+4, punished 7+ 4.8/5.4/5.3/5.4 (partly because their groupmates contribute more). OLS for c(t+3) on c, others' mean and punishment: +0.10 per point (se 0.017), no extra early-round effect. So one point of punishment (one pool point) returns ~0.1*1.6 = 0.16 pool per later round: it pays back only with ~6+ rounds left, i.e. punishment should fade toward the end and be zero in the last rounds. Weighed against switching (explore 2), the amount must stay modest and close to what the other manager does.

7. **Drafted the rule.** punishment = k * max(0, c0 - c) for my group's members, times a horizon factor max(0, 1 - t/T) (fade to zero, by T), then scaled down so my group's mean punishment never exceeds the other group's mean punishment so far (seen in `p` for players outside my group) plus `slack`. The relative cap targets the switching driver of explore 2 (own minus other group's punishment, 0.24 logit per point), and lets the rule punish more freely next to a harsh manager and stay gentle next to a lenient one. Round 0 has no history, so no cap there. Four parameters: c0, k, T, slack.
8. **A technical dead end: th calls with float scalars.** Replaying the draft on the human histories (replay.py) crashed with `KeyError: '__import__'`, though validate-rule passed. In a fresh process, `th.clamp(x, min=0.0)`, `x.clamp(0.0)`, `th.full_like(x, 30.0)` and `x < 0.5` fail on their first call inside the builtins-free exec (torch imports something lazily); validate-rule only passed because its random histories warm torch first. The sweep is probably warm too, but I rewrote the code without them: `th.relu` for max(0, .), `th.minimum`/`th.maximum` with tensors, `1 - in_group` instead of a comparison, `th.ones_like(..) * 30`. It now runs cold.

9. **What punishment levels does the rule produce on human histories?** replay.py, mean punishment per member of group 0 per round. (c0 10, k 0.5, horizon 30, slack 1): 1.3 in round 0, ~0.9 in rounds 1-7, ~0.5 by round 12, 0.2 at the end, close to the "low"/"mid" human managers. (c0 14, k 1, horizon 20, slack 2): 5.6 in round 0 (no cap yet), ~3 in rounds 1-8, zero from round 19, like the "high" style. (c0 6, k 0.3): ~0.1, nearly a non-punisher, since the manager floors to integers and 0.3 * shortfall < 1 for most players. So the ranges span from no punishment to the harsh human styles, with the mid style (explore 4-5) inside. Validation passes on all 256 points.

## Key findings

1. Group size is the main lever on the pool: after the first switch, group sizes are spread from 1 to 8, while per-capita contributions barely depend on size (8.0-9.8) (explorations 1, 4).
2. Punishment drives players out. Switching rises by 0.12 logit per point of own punishment and 0.24 per point of the own group's mean punishment *minus the other group's*. Players punished ~7 per round leave 50% of the time vs 18% unpunished. A group punishing more than 1.2 above the other holds 2.7 members vs 4-5 (explorations 2, 4).
3. Punishment raises low contributors' later contributions by about +0.10 per point, still present three rounds later (OLS, se 0.017). It backfires on high contributors (-6.7 vs -1.7 for 15-20 contributors punished 5-10) (explorations 3, 6).
4. Human managers who punished mildly (1.1-1.7 per member per round, aimed at 0-4 contributors, front-loaded) had the largest pools (77 vs 36-53 for the other quintiles) and the highest contributions (11.2 vs 7.7-9.4) without losing members (explorations 4, 5).
5. At +0.1 contribution per point (0.16 pool per later round), a punishment pays for itself only with roughly six or more rounds left, so late punishment is wasted (exploration 6).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes only free riders, mildly and in proportion to how far they fall short. It concentrates punishment early, when the lift in contributions has many rounds to pay back, and fades it to nothing toward the end. It never lets its group's punishment run far above the other manager's, because players compare the two groups' punishment when deciding where to play, and a group that loses members loses pool. Next to a harsh manager it can afford more punishment and still attract players; next to a lenient one it must stay gentle.

The rule: for each valid member of my group, raw = k * max(0, c0 - c) * max(0, 1 - t / horizon). All raw punishments are then scaled down so my group's mean punishment is at most the other group's mean punishment per valid member so far (from `p`) plus `slack` (no cap in round 0).
- `c0` (int, [2, 20]): the expected contribution; only members below it are punished. I expect 8-14, around the group mean and the 5-9 band where punishment still works.
- `k` (float, [0.1, 3], log): punishment per point of shortfall. I expect 0.3-1. At 0.1 the rule is nearly a non-punisher (the manager floors to integers), and at 3 it is harsher than any human style.
- `horizon` (float, [6, 60], log): the round by which punishment has faded to zero. I expect 15-35. At 6 it punishes only the first block; at 60 it barely fades.
- `slack` (float, [0.25, 8], log): how far my mean punishment may exceed the other manager's. I expect 0.5-2, around the 1.2 at which human groups started losing members. At 8 the cap practically never binds.

### Justification

Findings 3 and 4 justify punishing at all, and only below a contribution level: the lift is real for low contributors and negative for cooperators, so c0 and k set whom to punish and how hard. Finding 4's best style (about 1-2 per member, aimed at the lowest contributors) sits in the middle of the c0 and k ranges (exploration 9). Finding 5 gives the horizon: payback needs remaining rounds, and the mid-style managers front-loaded too. Finding 2 gives the relative cap: switching responds to the difference between the two groups' punishment, so the rule's budget is tied to the other manager's observed punishment, which is all it can see of that manager. Findings 1-2 explain why the cap matters: members are worth more than the contributions punishment buys.

The hypothesis would be contradicted if the sweep's best points sat at k = 0.1 or slack = 0.25 against every opponent. That would mean the artificial humans' switching is so punishment-sensitive that any punishment loses, and a non-punisher is best. A best horizon at 60 would mean late punishment pays after all, against finding 5.

What I chose not to model:
- Rewarding (not punishing) high contributors beyond leaving them alone: punishment can only be 0 or more.
- A threshold relative to the group mean: humans punished by the gap to others, but an absolute c0 is simpler, and the group mean hovers around 9 anyway.
- Extra punishment just before switch decisions, a recency window on the other manager's punishment, and repeat-offender memory: each would need a parameter, and the data gave no strong signal for them. The early-round interaction in exploration 6 was zero (0.008, se 0.023).
