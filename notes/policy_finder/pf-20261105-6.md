# pf-20261105-6

## Explorations

1. **What do the reference managers score?** Looked at `per_round.parquet` of
   29_EXP1_reference with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-6/ref_overview.py.
   `zero` scores 589.3 (se 2.1), `humanlike` 550.6 (se 3.4); ~3.92 valid
   players per round under both. Under zero, mean contribution decays from
   11.6 (round 0) to 5.5 (round 23); humanlike holds it at ~13.2 but punishes
   34% of player-rounds with mean 2.4 points, which costs the group ~2x that
   per player (player + pool), ~4.8/round, against a contribution gain of ~5
   points x 0.6 net = ~3/round. So the bar is 589: punishment must be cheap
   per unit of contribution it buys. Next: how the artificial humans respond
   to being punished (size, and relative to their own contribution).

2. **How do players respond to punishment, one round on?** Looked at next-round
   change in contribution by (own contribution bin x punishment bin) in the
   humanlike sim and both pilots, scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-6/response.py.
   In the sim a player at c<=4 rises +0.8 unpunished, +1.1 at p in (0,2],
   +1.9 at (4,6], +4.9 at p>14: the response is concave, so per point of
   punishment small sanctions buy most (~0.3-0.7 contribution per point vs
   ~0.2 for p>14). Players at c=20 drop more when punished (-0.75 unpunished,
   -1.0 to -3.0 punished): never punish full contributors. At c in (14,19]
   a small punishment helps only ~+0.5. Pilots agree in direction, with
   larger responses in random_1 (c<=4, p>14: +8.2).
   Also, per-round accounting: a point of punishment costs the group-mean
   payoff 2 points (player + pool), a point of contribution earns 0.6. So
   punishment pays only if each point buys >3.3 contribution-rounds. Next:
   how the managers punished, and what ratio humanlike achieves.

3. **What do the managers punish, and how efficiently?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-6/manager_pattern.py
   and random1_fit.py. Humanlike punishes c=0 only half the time (mean 6.6),
   c in (9,14] 55% of the time (2.9), c in (14,19] 26% (1.05), and keeps
   punishing ~2.0/player in the last rounds (pure waste in round 23). Its
   gain over zero is +5.4 contribution (avg over rounds) for 2.4 punishment,
   a ratio of 2.25 contribution per point, below the 3.3 break-even: hence
   550 vs 589. The random_1 pilot rule is close to p = a - b*c with slope
   b in ~[-1.7,-0.1], i.e. roughly s*(20-c); episodes with a steep slope
   (s~1) end at c~17-20 (corr(a, last-3-round contribution) = 0.69), but
   pay heavily early (round-0 punishment 7.2). Under zero, the share at c=0
   grows from 8% to 28% by the last quarter; under humanlike it stays ~10%
   (a hard core of non-responders the manager keeps paying for).
   So: a good rule must beat 3.3 contribution-rounds per point; levers are
   (a) no punishment in the final rounds, (b) only clear shortfalls, (c)
   front-loading so the norm sets early and the long 24-round tail pays.
   Next: how persistent a punishment's effect is (lags 1..8).

4. **How long does a punishment's effect last, and does it spill over?**
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-6/persistence.py on the humanlike sim,
   low contributors (c<=9), matched on own c and others' mean. Gain in
   c_{t+k}-c_t over unpunished: p in (0,3]: +0.04/+0.22/+0.23/+0.29 at
   k=1/2/4/8; (3,8]: +0.46/+0.77/+0.87/+0.79; (8,30]: +1.64/+1.88/+1.81/+1.81.
   The effect does not fade over 8 rounds (the clones are RNNs with memory).
   Unpunished peers also rise +0.054 per point punished on others at k=4,
   and own contribution follows others' mean (+0.36 at k=4): herding. Per
   point of punishment that is ~0.12-0.2 (own) + ~0.16 (3 peers) contribution
   per round, sustained: ~6 contribution-rounds per point if dealt with ~20
   rounds left, against the 3.3 break-even, but below it with fewer than ~10
   rounds left. Episode level: late (12-23) contribution rises +0.70 per point
   of mean punishment in rounds 0-3 (controlling for round-0 contribution),
   while later punishment correlates negatively (it chases chronic low
   contributors). So: punish early, taper off, stop before the end.
   Surprise: per point, small punishments (<=3) look less efficient here
   (+0.29 for ~2 points) than in the one-step table of entry 2; mid-size
   punishments (3-8) seem the sweet spot. A cap on the punishment is worth
   a parameter rather than a fixed guess.

5. **Drafted the rule.** p_i = min(cap, slope * (target - c_i)+) * 2^(-t/half_life),
   rounded, and 0 in the last `quiet` rounds. Target/slope set who is punished
   and how hard (the random_1 pilot rule, ~s*(20-c), is the special case
   target 20, slope ~1, no cap); the cap captures the concave response
   (with a high slope it becomes a flat sanction for any shortfall); the
   half-life front-loads (entry 4) and stops paying for chronic
   non-responders late; quiet removes the endgame waste (humanlike still
   spends ~2.0/player in round 23). Full contributors are never punished
   (target <= 20). I chose an absolute target over one relative to the
   group mean: the clones herd toward the others, so a relative threshold
   would chase a falling group downward (as under zero, where 0-contributors
   grow from 8% to 28%).

6. **Validated and tested the rule.** validate-rule passes (256 distinct
   design points). scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-6/test_rule.py runs the
   code on a toy history: with target 20, slope 1, no cap, a 0-contributor
   gets 20 at round 1 and 17 at round 21 (half-life 100), a 19-contributor 1,
   a full contributor and a timed-out player 0, and nobody in the quiet
   rounds; with target 15, slope 3, cap 5, half-life 6 the 0-contributor gets
   4 at round 1 and 2 at round 10. I could not run the artificial humans
   here (no model weights in the container), so the values are left to the
   sweep. Open question I could not settle: whether the clones keep their
   contributions up once punishment stops in the quiet rounds; the
   persistence in entry 4 (no fade over 8 rounds) suggests they do.

## Key findings

1. The bar is `zero` at 589.3; `humanlike` loses 38 points (550.6) because it
   buys only ~2.25 contribution per point of punishment, while a point costs
   the group mean 2 payoff points and a contribution point earns 0.6, so
   break-even needs ~3.3 contribution-rounds per point (entries 1, 3).
2. Full contributors drop when punished (-1.0 to -3.0 next round vs -0.75
   unpunished); low contributors rise, concavely in the size of the sanction
   (c<=4: +0.8 unpunished, +1.9 at p 4-6, +4.9 at p>14) (entry 2).
3. A punishment's effect on a low contributor does not fade over 8 rounds
   (+0.8 for p 3-8, +1.8 for p>8 at lag 8), and spills over to unpunished
   peers (+0.054 per point at lag 4), so its return scales with the rounds
   left: ~6 contribution-rounds per point with ~20 rounds left (entry 4).
4. Early punishment (rounds 0-3) goes with +0.70 late contribution per
   point; later punishment chases chronic low contributors (entry 4); a hard
   core of ~10% zero contributors persists even under humanlike (entry 3).
5. Humanlike keeps punishing ~2.0 per player in round 23, where no future
   can repay it (entry 3); the random_1 pilot rule ~s*(20-c) with s~1 lifted
   late contributions to 17-20 at a heavy early cost (entry 3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes only clear shortfalls below a high contribution
norm, with moderate, capped sanctions, concentrated early in the game when
the persistent, spilling-over response has the most rounds to repay the
double cost, tapering off later (so it stops paying for players who never
respond), and never in the last rounds. Full contributors are never
punished. The rule: p_i = round(min(cap, slope*(target - c_i)+) *
2^(-t/half_life)), 0 in the last `quiet` rounds.

- `target` (float, [8, 20]): the contribution norm; shortfall below it is
  punished. I expect 14-20.
- `slope` (float, [0.1, 5], log): points per point of shortfall. I expect
  0.3-1.5; with a high slope and low cap the rule becomes a flat sanction.
- `cap` (float, [1, 30], log): the largest single sanction. I expect 3-15.
- `half_life` (float, [2, 100], log): rounds for the scale to halve; 100 is
  effectively no decay. I expect 8-40.
- `quiet` (int, [1, 10]): final rounds with no punishment. I expect 2-6.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the economics: every point must buy >3.3 contribution-rounds,
which is why the rule spends nothing where returns are lowest. Finding 2
supports never punishing c=20 (target <= 20) and the cap (concave response);
the cap's range [1, 30] runs from a token sanction to no cap at all, since
entries 2 and 4 disagree on whether small (<=3) or mid (3-8) sanctions are
most efficient. Finding 3 and 4 support the half-life: returns shrink with
the rounds left, and late spending goes to non-responders; its range reaches
100 so the sweep can also choose no decay. Findings 3 and 5 support `quiet`:
with under ~10 rounds left a point no longer repays itself, and round 23 is
pure loss, so quiet >= 1; its upper end 10 is where entry 4's arithmetic
puts break-even. `target` [8, 20] spans a lenient norm to full contribution
(the random_1 rule's 20); `slope` [0.1, 5] log spans ~zero-like (floored to
nothing) to the steep pilot rules and beyond. A best point with target 8 and
tiny slope (i.e. near-zero punishment) would contradict the hypothesis that
punishment can pay at all in these clones; quiet at 10 or half-life at 2
would say only the very first rounds are worth punishing. Not modelled:
a threshold relative to the group (herding would let it drift down with the
group), escalation or forgiveness per player based on past responses (more
parameters than 256 points can map, and the half-life already limits
spending on chronic non-responders), and reacting to timeouts (timed-out
players cannot be punished, and timeouts are ~2% of player-rounds).
