# pf-20261106-4

## Explorations

1. **What does the game look like in the human data?** Looked at contributions, punishments and group sizes per round in experiments/2group_8agent_50ep.csv (deduplicated, 50 games) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/a1_overview.py. Found mean contribution flat around 8.2-9.7 of 20 over all 24 rounds, mean punishment falling from about 3.5 (rounds 0-3) to about 1.1 (late), 71% of punishments zero. Group sizes spread hugely after the first switch: std 2.4 players around a mean of 4.6, min 1, max 8. Mean pool per group-round 51.9. So the score (pool, empty group = 0) is dominated by how many members a group holds: an 8-player group at c=9 pools ~115, a 1-player group ~14. Next: what drives a player to switch.

2. **What drives a player to switch groups?** Looked at the decision rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 (switch = next round's group differs) against own punishment, block (4-round) punishment, per-capita pool and mean-punishment differences between the groups, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/a2_switch.py. Found the switch rate falls from 44% (round 3) to ~24% (later). It rises steeply with the decision round's own punishment: 22% at 0, 29% at 1-2, 39% at 3-5, 58% at 6-10, 69% above 10. Against the per-capita pool difference own-other: 46-53% when own is 4+ lower, 9-12% when own is 4+ higher. Against mean punishment own minus other: 12% when own group is punished 3+ less per head, 62% when 3+ more. A logit with all of them keeps each (dpc -0.065/pt, dmp +0.16, own punishment per round +0.14, group size -0.14, all p<0.001), and players contributing above their group's mean leave slightly more (+0.06/pt). The artificial switch model reads exactly common_good, punishment, group and round at the decision round (configs/training/artificial_humans/switch_predictor). So punishment costs members twice: through the pool and through exits, and comparatively (the other manager's punishment level matters). Next: does punishment buy enough contribution to pay for that?

3. **Does punishment buy contributions?** Looked at next-round contribution of players who stayed, against this round's punishment, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/a3_response.py. Raw buckets show punished low contributors (0-4) rising by 1.0-3.7 next round vs 0.5 unpunished, but punished high contributors (15-20) dropping 5.5-7.8 when hit with 6+. Controlling for own contribution (AR 0.74) and the others' mean (0.23, herding), one punishment point raises next contribution by 0.11 (0.13 for contributors at or below 8), and 0.05-0.12 two to four rounds on. With AR 0.74 the summed gain is at most ~0.13/(1-0.74) = 0.5 contribution, i.e. 0.8 pool points, for each punishment point, which itself costs 1 pool point at once. So punishment roughly breaks even on contributions alone even at best, and loses once exits (entry 2) are counted; punishing anyone above the group mean is clearly harmful. I now think the base policy is near-zero punishment, with at most light, targeted punishment of clear free-riders in rounds far from a switch decision and from the end. Next: what the human managers did and how their policy relates to their group's pool and size.

4. **What did human managers do, and did it pay?** Looked at each manager's mean punishment in rounds 0-3 and over the game against its group's score (mean pool, empty = 0) and final size, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/a4_managers.py. Found that the manager who punished less in rounds 0-3 than the other scored 62.5 vs 42.5 and ended with 4.8 vs 3.3 members; within a game the difference in mean punishment correlates -0.43 with the difference in final size and -0.27 with the pool difference. Across groups pool correlates 0.53 with final size and -0.31 with punishment. Mean contribution per head barely depends on group size (8.6-9.8 for sizes 1-7, 8.0 at 8), so the pool scales with members: ~14 at 1, ~47 at 4, ~97 at 7. Human managers punished by shortfall from the group mean (6.5 on average for those 6+ below, 0.7 even for those 3+ above, so they also hit cooperators). This confirms the hypothesis: keep punishment below the other manager's, never touch cooperators. Next: is it the decision-round punishment or the whole block's that drives exits, which would tell whether punishing early in a block is safe.

5. **Is it the decision round's punishment that drives exits, or the block's?** Split each switcher's block punishment into the decision round's (p_dec) and the other three rounds' (p_rest), plus the punishment of earlier blocks, in a logit with the per-capita pool difference, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/a5_timing.py. Found a point of punishment in the decision round raises the switch log-odds by 0.075-0.087, a point earlier in the same block by only 0.014-0.026, and punishment from earlier blocks not at all (-0.07 per round-mean, if anything negative). Per-capita pool difference beats the total pool difference as a predictor (AIC 1864 vs 1943). So if a rule punishes at all, it should do it early in a 4-round block, so the contribution rise (entry 3) shows up in the pool by the decision round, and keep the decision round (and maybe the round or two before it) clean.

6. **Drafted the rule.** The manager only controls punishment, so the design is about when, whom and how much, bounded by what the other manager does. Punish only valid members below the leave-one-out mean of their valid group-mates, k points per point of shortfall (no cooperator is ever touched, entry 3/4); only in rounds t < t_stop (late punishment cannot pay back, last-round punishment is pure loss); never in the last `quiet` rounds of each 4-round block, decision round included (entry 5); and never more per player than cap0 + rel x the other manager's mean punishment per member so far (read from p and in_group: the players outside my group). That cap is how the rule adapts to the opponent: against a harsh manager it can afford some discipline and still be the milder group (entry 2's dmp effect, entry 4's 62.5 vs 42.5); against a lenient one it stays near zero. A zero-punishment manager sits inside the design (k near 0, t_stop = 0, or cap0 and rel near 0), so the sweep can find it if discipline does not pay in simulation.

7. **Does the draft run, and how much does it punish?** Replayed it from group 0's seat on the 50 human histories with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-4/t1_replay.py. Hit a snag first: in a fresh process `th.remainder(t, 4)` and `.sum(-1, keepdim=True)` raised KeyError '__import__' under the rule's no-builtins scope (torch lazily imports on those paths), although validate-rule had passed; replaced them with `t - 4 * th.floor(t / 4)` and `.sum(-1)[..., None]`, so the code no longer depends on what ran before it in the process. With k 0.5, t_stop 12, quiet 1, cap0 2, rel 0.75 it deals ~0.6-0.7 per member in the punishing rounds, 0 at decision rounds and from round 12 on, a fifth of what the human managers dealt early (3.5) and about half their late level; at the top of every range (k 1.5... here k 1, cap0 8, rel 1.5, quiet 0) about 1.5-2.3 per member, close to the human managers; k = 0 gives exactly zero. So the ranges span from "no punishment" up to about human-manager harshness, which is where I expect the optimum to sit at the low end.

## Key findings

1. The score is driven by group size: after the first switch group sizes have std 2.4 around 4.6 and range 1-8, while contribution per head is nearly size-independent (8.6-9.8), so the pool runs from ~14 (1 member) to ~97 (7 members) (explorations 1, 4).
2. Punishment drives players out: switch rate 22% unpunished vs 58-69% at 6+ points in the decision round; a group punished 3+ more per head than the other loses 62% of its players at a decision vs 12% for one punished 3+ less (exploration 2).
3. The decision round's punishment weighs about 3-4 times as much per point (logit +0.075-0.087) as punishment earlier in the same block (+0.014-0.026); earlier blocks' punishment does not raise exits (exploration 5).
4. One punishment point raises a low contributor's next contribution by about 0.13 (AR 0.74), worth at most ~0.8 pool points over the game against the 1 point it costs at once; punishing cooperators (15+) cuts their contribution by 5-8 points (exploration 3).
5. Among human managers, the one punishing less in rounds 0-3 scored 62.5 vs 42.5 and ended with 4.8 vs 3.3 members; within games the punishment difference correlates -0.43 with the final size difference (exploration 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager wins and keeps members, because the pool scales with them, and punishment is the main thing that sends members away, especially punishment in the round right before a switch decision and punishment heavier than the other group's. Punishment buys little contribution (at best about break-even) and only from free-riders. So the manager should punish rarely and lightly: only members below their group-mates' mean, only early in each 4-round block and not late in the game, and never more than the other manager does, so that its group is always the milder one. It reads the other manager's harshness from `p` of the players outside its group and lets its own cap scale with it.

Rule (5 parameters):
- `k` (float, [0, 1.5]): punishment per point a valid member falls below the mean of its valid group-mates this round. Expected best 0-0.6.
- `t_stop` (int, 0-20): punish only in rounds t < t_stop; 0 = never. Expected best 4-12 (or 0 if discipline does not pay in simulation).
- `quiet` (int, 0-3): the last `quiet` rounds of each 4-round block, decision round included, are punishment-free. Expected best 1-2.
- `cap0` (float, [0, 8]): per-player cap when the other manager has punished nothing so far. Expected best 0-3.
- `rel` (float, [0, 1.5]): cap growth per point of the other manager's mean punishment per member so far. Expected best 0.3-1 (below 1 keeps the rule milder than the opponent).

### Justification

Finding 1 makes membership the objective; findings 2 and 5 make low punishment relative to the other manager the main way to win members, which is what `cap0` and `rel` bound: with a lenient opponent the cap stays near `cap0` (low), with a harsh one the rule may discipline free-riders and still be the milder group. Finding 4 justifies punishing only below the group mean (`k` on the shortfall, cooperators never touched) and keeping `k` small, and supports `t_stop`: a point dealt late has too few rounds left to pay back its immediate cost. Finding 3 gives `quiet`: punishment early in a block costs few exits and its contribution gain reaches the pool before the decision, while decision-round punishment is the costliest. Every range includes the zero-punishment manager (k = 0, t_stop = 0, or cap0 = rel = 0), because finding 4 says discipline may not pay at all; the tops reach roughly human-manager harshness (about 2 per member early), beyond which the data say only harm. A result where the best point has large k and t_stop at 20 against all opponents, or where groups under this rule lose members to a harsher manager, would contradict the hypothesis. Not modelled: rewarding or targeting individuals by history (the rule uses this round's shortfall only), and per-capita pool comparisons with the other group (the manager cannot change contributions other than through punishment), and recency weighting of the opponent's punishment (a cumulative mean is steadier against noisy opponents); the artificial humans could not be simulated here since the model weights are not in the container.
