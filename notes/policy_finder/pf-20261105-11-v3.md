# pf-20261105-11-v3

## Explorations

1. **Where do v1 and v2 stand, and where is the payoff still lost?** Read both
   earlier versions' notes, rules, landscapes and final sweeps, and ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-11/c1_v2_final_rounds.py on both final
   per_round files. The two finals are the same within noise:
   - v1 (T=16, P=25, g=1.15, last punished round 13): 648.8 (se 1.4).
   - v2 (T=14.3, A=1.7, B=1.56, S=8): 649.7 (se 1.3).

   v2's landscape is interior on every parameter and very flat in S: 650.9 at S=8,
   648.6 at 12, 643 at 16, 639 at 2. So moving the stop round does not help any
   more. The payoff losses against a "perfect" 32/round are now:
   - the start-up: the round-0 payoff is 12.0 (punishment 7.45 per player, 61%
     punished), then 18.5, 22.0, 24.2, 25.5. That is ~60 points below the ~30 of a
     silent cooperative round, spread over rounds 0-8;
   - the post-stop slide: c falls from 17.2 to 13.8 by round 23, with the share at
     c<=9 rising from 5% to 22%. That is ~1.5 payoff/round by the end, ~15 summed.

   The start-up is the big lever. Next: is all of the round-0/1 bill needed for the
   lift? The sweeps hold 2 x 256 points x 500 episodes with varied T, A, B, P, g,
   which I can use to study how the first rounds' punishment shapes the later
   contributions.
2. **Can I build a stand-in for the artificial humans to screen rule shapes offline?**
   The model weights are not in the container, but the two sweeps hold 256k
   simulated games under 512 different rules, plus the reference sims.
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-11/c2_prep_arrays.py turns them into arrays.
   My first attempt was surrogate.py with c3_fit_surrogate.py: an MLP that predicts
   next-round contribution (21 classes) from hand-made history features (own c, p
   and lags, running means, the others' mean/min, the round).
   - Teacher-forced it is well calibrated (c5_surrogate_calib.py): predicted minus
     actual mean c is within +-0.1 at every round, under zero, humanlike and v2.
   - Rolled out on its own, it fails (c4_validate_surrogate.py). Under zero it
     decays to c=3.0 against the real 5.5, and its scores sit 45 points below the
     real ones. Correlation is 0.93 over held-out sweep points but only 0.57 over
     the top points.

   The features do not carry the persistent player state the clones' RNN keeps, so
   a rollout regresses every player toward the mean. That is a dead end for
   absolute numbers. Next: a GRU surrogate (surrogate_gru.py, c6_fit_gru.py) that
   learns its own hidden state from the sequences.
3. **The GRU drifted too, and the cause was timeouts, not memory.** The GRU surrogate
   (val NLL 1.682 against the MLP's 1.702) rolled out even lower: zero reached 3.2.
   Debugging one step from the real zero games (/tmp scratch) turned up the cause.
   A timed-out player is recorded in the parquet, and fed to the clones, as
   contribution **15** (timeout_contribution "default"). The rule sees 0. Players
   back from a round-0 timeout contribute 16.9 next round, against 10.6 for the
   others.

   My rollouts fed the surrogate c=0 for a timeout, a state it had never seen in
   training. With timed-out players fed as 15 (and the rule still seeing 0), both
   surrogates match the real sims (c4_validate_surrogate.py,
   c7_validate_gru.py):
   - zero: MLP 587.9, GRU 589.5, real 589.3; c by round 11.5 -> 5.1, real 11.4 -> 5.3.
   - The 6 v1/v2 final points: MLP within -6..+5 of real (v2 best 648.8 vs 649.7,
     v1 best 647.8 vs 648.8). The GRU runs 1-16 low.
   - Held-out sweep points: MLP corr 0.958, bias +6, rmse 9.8. GRU corr 0.953,
     bias -1, rmse 8.2.

   Among the top points (real >630) the correlation is 0.58-0.73, much of it the
   real sweep's own noise (se 3.7 per point). So I now have a usable screen,
   good to ~5-10 points, for families near the data. It is less trustworthy for
   shapes the training rules never produced. I will use both surrogates and trust
   only effects that both agree on and that are larger than ~5.
4. **First screen of structural ideas on the surrogates.**
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-11/screen.py (helper) and c8_screen_ideas.py,
   4000 games per rule, MLP / GRU scores (se ~1). v2's best: 650.2 / 641.1. The
   surrogates reproduce v2's flat S profile: S=6..12 all within 2, S=23 at 636 / 623.
   - **Round-0 punishment is essential.** Dropping it costs 26-29 (624 / 612), and
     halving it costs 9-11. Raising it 1.5x changes nothing (649 / 639). The
     start-up bill I hoped to trim (entry 1) is what buys the regime.
   - A late maintenance phase (after S=8, punish c<=F by Q) never helps: -2 to -15.
   - A late ratchet tap (punish a drop of >=d from one's own last contribution)
     helps nothing, and hurts with large taps.
   - An adaptive switch (the v2 schedule only while the group mean is below M)
     hurts: at best 648 / 636. Once the group is cooperative, a single defector is
     better left alone than punished.

   So the shape "harsh at first, then silence" holds, and more time-structure in the
   late game does not pay. Next: the time profile within the punishment window.
   Is the optimum a strong round 0 that tapers, rather than v2's constant
   schedule up to S?
5. **Time profile and history-based variants: v2 is a local optimum.**
   - c9_time_profile.py: a coordinate search over a multiplier k_t of v2's schedule
     for each round 0..12, with candidates 0-4x, scored on the mean of both
     surrogates. No round wanted anything but 1x (everything within ~1 of 645.5).
     Rounds 0-1 are the steepest: x0 costs 10-18 and x0.5 costs 4-7.
   - c10_screen_history.py tried rules that read the history or the group:
     - Crediting players who raised their contribution (punishment x q): q=0 costs
       6-8, and q=0.6 costs 1.
     - "Giving up" on a player punished n times before: n=1 costs 21-26, n=5 is
       neutral (+1).
     - Relative targets: max(T, others' mean - d) and min(T, others' mean + d)
       are at best neutral (-1..-12).
     - Punishing only the lowest contributor costs 11-14, or 30 with no stop.

   So the clones respond best to a plain, predictable "your contribution below T
   costs you A + B*(T-c)" in every round of the window. Conditioning on
   improvement, history or the group does not help. They seem to learn the price
   schedule, not the episode. Next: is v2's (T, A, B, S) just mis-tuned in some
   direction that a different round-0 schedule could fix? A local search with
   round 0 parametrised separately.
6. **Separate opening schedule; who stays low.**
   - c11_local_search.py: a random local search over (T, A, B) for round 0, (T, A, B)
     for rounds 1..S, and S, on the mean of both surrogates. After 150 tries it
     gained only +0.7: a slightly lower opening target (12.6) with a steeper price
     (1.95). So the opening is not mis-tuned.
   - c13_who_decays.py, v2's final best, by player: the ~20% of players punished in
     5 or more of the 9 window rounds sit at c=13.3 in round 8, just under the
     target of 14.3. They stay lowest after the stop (12.5 in rounds 18-23, against
     15.6 for never-punished players). The others reach 17-19 by round 8, and
     their late c falls to 14.5-16.

   So the target works as an anchor. A sizeable group learns to sit just below it
   and pays a small fine every round. That suggests a target that rises during
   the window, which v2's fixed T cannot express: ask little at first, when most
   of the group is below, and more once the group has climbed. Next: a target ramp
   T_t = T0 + r*t (c14_target_ramp.py). In parallel I run a 56-cell table policy
   (round bin x contribution bin) optimised by an evolution strategy on the
   surrogates (c12_table_es.py) to look for structure I have not thought of.
7. **A rising target hurts; the free-form table finds nothing.**
   - c14_target_ramp.py: T_t = min(20, T0 + r t) over T0 in {11, 13, 14.3},
     r in {0, 0.5, 1}, S in {6, 8, 10}, B in {1.2, 1.56, 2.2}, 3000 games each.
     Every top setting has r=0. The best is T0=13, S=8, B=2.2 at 651.1 / 642.8,
     against v2's 650.3 / 641.3. r=0.5 costs ~3-5, and r=1 costs more. Demanding
     more of the anchored players (entry 6) does not lift them, it only fines them.
   - c12_table_es.py: a 56-cell table (8 round bins x 7 contribution bins) under an
     evolution strategy on the surrogates, started from v2. It wandered within
     noise (641 -> 643.5 -> 641.5 on its own scale) in 20 generations, and I
     stopped it. Its cells kept v2's shape: ~21-23 for c 0-3, ~15 for 4-7, ~9 for
     8-11, ~3 for 12-14, ~0 above, and ~0 from round 12 on.

   So within "punish now by the current contribution" the surrogates put the
   ceiling at v2's level. The one history lever that came out non-negative was
   "give up on a player after n punishments" (+1 at n=5, entry 5). The anchored
   players of entry 6 are its natural target: fining them round after round buys
   nothing. Next: a finer test of give-up, and whether it lets the window run
   longer or the target sit higher.
8. **Give-up: "after n punished rounds a player is left alone" is a small but steady gain.**
   - c15_giveup_grid.py: a grid over T {13, 14.3, 16} x S {8, 11, 14} x n {3, 4, 5,
     6, off} x B {1.56, 2.2}, 3000 games each. The best is T=13, B=2.2, S=11, n=5 at
     654.2 / 644.7. The best with give-up off is 651.3 / 642.8.
   - c16_giveup_check.py, the same candidates on fresh seeds (2 x 4000 games):
     - v2 best: 648.9 / 642.0.
     - T13 B2.2 S11, no give-up: 650.5 / 642.9.
     - with n=5: 651.7 / 645.3; with n=6: 652.5 / 645.1.
     - A points budget instead (no more than K points per player in total) hurts at
       every K (K=60: 645; K=20: 629), because it caps the big round-0 fines on
       free riders.
   - c17_giveup_mech.py (GRU surrogate, 8000 games): give-up trades a little
     contribution for a lower bill. c at round 11 is 16.5 against 17.0, and late c is
     14.0 against 14.6. But the bill from round 5 on falls from ~1.2-1.7 to
     ~0.65-1.4 per player. With give-up the window can run longer: S=14, n=5 gives
     653.0 / 644.9, while S=20-23 falls back to 645 / 638.
   - c18_giveup_variants.py (2 x 4000 games, T13 B2.2):
     - total count n=5: +2.1 at S=11 and +3.5 at S=14, over no give-up.
     - a count of consecutive punished rounds (reset when unpunished): -0.3 to -3.
     - a smooth decay d^k of the fine: -1 to -8.

   So what pays is a hard cap on how many rounds one player is fined, not softer
   fines. My reading: a player who is still below the target after ~5 fines is one
   the fines do not move (the anchored ~20% of entry 6). Every further fine on them
   costs 2x its size, while players who drop for the first time are still fined.
   The gain is modest, ~2-3 on the surrogates. That is about the size of the gap I
   could hope to close, and within the noise of one surrogate at one seed, but it
   holds across both surrogates and three seed sets.
   Next: put it in the rule as a fifth parameter and see on the surrogates where
   the five-parameter optimum lies, to set the ranges.
9. **Drafted v3 and probed its ranges on the surrogates.**
   configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261105-11-v3.yml keeps v2's core: for c < T,
   punish A + B*(T - c) in rounds t <= S. It adds N: a player already punished in
   N earlier rounds is not punished again. Ranges: T [11, 17], A [0, 4], B [1.0, 3.2]
   log, S int [6, 16], N int [3, 12].

   c19_range_probe.py ran the sweep's own 256-point Sobol design (sobol_design)
   on the surrogates, 1000 games per point (noisy, se ~2-3 per point).
   - The top 25 points have median T 13.3, A 1.5, B 2.0, S 11, N 7, all inside the
     ranges.
   - Restricted to the good T/B region, the mean score by N peaks at N=4-5
     (644.5-646.1, against 641.7 at N=3 and ~642-644 at N=8-12). By S it peaks at
     10-11 (645-646, against ~641-642 at S=6 and S=15-16).

   N=12 is close to "off" for most S, so the sweep can return to v2 if give-up does
   not pay on the real clones. t3_rule_check_v3.py checks the rule in a fresh
   process on a hand-made history: a free rider is fined rounds 0-4 and then left
   alone, and a player who rises above T after two strikes is never fined again.
   validate-rule passes on all 256 design points.

## Key findings

1. v1 and v2 tie in their finals: 648.8 and 649.7, se ~1.4. v2's landscape is
   interior and flat in its stop round S (650.9 at S=8, 648.6 at 12). The payoff
   still lost sits in the start-up (round-0 payoff 12) and the post-stop slide
   (c 17.2 -> 13.8) (1).
2. A timed-out player is recorded and fed to the clones as contribution 15, while
   the rule sees 0. Players back from a round-0 timeout contribute 16.9 next round,
   against 10.6 for the rest. With this handled, surrogates fitted on the v1/v2
   sweeps reproduce the real sims: zero 588-590 against 589, v1/v2 finals within
   ~5 (MLP), held-out correlation 0.95 (2, 3).
3. On the surrogates, the round-0 fine is essential: dropping it costs 26-29, and
   halving it 9-11. No round in the window wants a different multiplier from v2's
   constant schedule (4, 5).
4. Late maintenance, ratchet taps, adaptive (group-mean) switching, crediting
   improvement, relative targets, punishing only the lowest contributor, a rising
   target and a free-form 56-cell table all fail to beat v2's shape (4, 5, 7).
5. About 20% of players under v2 are fined in 5 or more of the 9 window rounds. They
   sit just under the target (c 13.3 at round 8) and stay lowest after the stop
   (12.5, against 15.6 for players never fined) (6).
6. Capping the number of rounds a player can be fined at N ~5 adds ~2-3.5 on both
   surrogates and on three seed sets. It lowers the bill from round 5 on by ~40% for
   a ~0.5 lower contribution, and lets the window run to S ~11-14. A consecutive
   count, a smooth decay of the fine and a points budget all do worse (8).
7. A mock sweep over the v3 design on the surrogates puts the optimum inside every
   range: T ~13.3, A ~1.5, B ~2.0, S ~10-11, N ~4-7 (9).

## Hypothesis

A good manager sets up the cooperative regime with an immediate, predictable price
on contributing below a standard: from round 0, a tap plus a steep price per
missing point. It then goes silent once the regime runs by itself, because the
clones keep contributing with only a slow decay. Not everyone responds. A minority
settles just below the standard and pays the fine round after round. Fining them
further costs the group twice the fine and buys nothing, so after N fines the
manager leaves a player alone. Players who slip for the first time are still
fined, and the window can stay open longer at a lower bill. The rule:
`punishment = A + B*(T - c)` for c < T, if t <= S and the player has been
punished in fewer than N earlier rounds; else 0.
- `T`, the target contribution: no punishment at or above it. Sweep [11, 17],
  uniform.
- `A`, the tap any shortfall gets. Sweep [0, 4], uniform.
- `B`, the price per point of shortfall. Sweep [1.0, 3.2], log-uniform.
- `S`, the last punished round. Sweep int [6, 16].
- `N`, the strikes before the manager gives up on a player. Sweep int [3, 12].

### Justification

Findings 1, 3 and 4 keep v2's core. The real landscape (1) and every surrogate
variant (3, 4) agree that a fixed target with tap plus linear price, applied from
round 0 to a stop round, is the best shape of its kind.

The ranges sit around the known optima, narrower than v2's, so the 256 points
cover the good region more densely:
- `T`: v2's real peak was 14.3, v1's 16, and the surrogates prefer ~13 (7). [11, 17]
  spans these with room on both sides. v2's real profile had fallen ~25 points by
  T=10-11, so a lower bound of 11 should not cut off the optimum.
- `B`: real peak 1.56, surrogate ~2.0-2.2 (7). The log range [1.0, 3.2] holds both
  and v2's good final point at 2.9.
- `A`: real peak 1.7, small effect (9 points). [0, 4] keeps the pure linear rule
  and a strong tap.
- `S`: v2's real peak was 8. With give-up the surrogates move it to 10-14 (6, 7).
  [6, 16] covers both.
- `N`: finding 5 is the reason it exists and finding 6 the evidence. I expect N at
  4-7. N=3 tests an early give-up (the surrogates say it costs). N=12 is
  practically "never give up" for S up to ~11, so the sweep can fall back to v2.

What would contradict it: a real sweep whose best points sit at N=10-12 (give-up
does not pay on the real clones, and finding 6 was a surrogate artefact), or at N=3
(the fines stop working sooner than I think). Both would show in landscapes.json's
N profile. A best S at the top of the range with N small would suggest the window
should be open-ended once give-up removes the waste.

Not modelled:
- A late maintenance phase or a later pulse: the surrogates say they cost (4).
- Targets relative to the group, or a target that rises within the window: both
  cost (4, 7).
- Points budgets per player: they cut the round-0 fines on free riders, which is
  where the lift comes from (3, 6).
- Timeouts: a rule sees them as 0 and cannot punish them. The clones' high
  contributions after a timeout (2) are outside the manager's control.

My evidence for N and for the shifted T/B/S comes from surrogates of the clones,
not from the clones themselves. They are good to ~5-10 points, and the gain from N
is ~2-3. So I chose ranges that contain both the surrogate optimum and v2's real
optimum, rather than trusting the surrogate alone.
