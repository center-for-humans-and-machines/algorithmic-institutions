# pf-20261105-7-v2

## Explorations

1. **What did version 1 teach?** Read v1's notes, rule, sweep.json, landscapes.json
   and final/ (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/11_v1_rounds.py). v1 scored
   649.7 +- 1.3 at target 14, slope 2.08, window 9, taper 0.18, against 589.4
   for zero: my surrogate's verdict (soft, 2-4 round campaigns, slope ~0.5) was
   badly wrong; the real clones respond far more strongly. Landscape: slope is
   the strongest axis (effect 73; below ~0.6 the score is *below* zero, 581-584,
   it climbs steeply to a peak at ~2.1 and turns down by 2.6), target peaks at
   14 (effect 44; 20 is 609, 10 is 626), window peaks at 9 but is flat from 7 to
   12 (645-654), taper hardly matters (7). Per round under the best point:
   contributions 11.5 -> 13.8 -> 15.0 -> 16.9 by round 8, punishment 8.3 per
   player in round 0, 5.1, 3.1, 2.3, ... 1.1 by round 8; after the window the
   level decays by ~0.25 per round (16.9 -> 13.6 at round 23), the same speed
   as under zero. Longer windows (s044, window 15) hold 17.4 but pay ~1.2 per
   player-round to do it and score lower (643). So the costs are: the big
   round-0 bill (8.3 per player = 16.6 of round-0 mean payoff), the
   ~1 per player-round tail of the window, and the post-window decay. Next:
   who is being punished in the tail of the window, and how they respond; use
   the 256-point sweep data (random rules = varied punishment) as a response
   dataset.
2. **How do the clones respond to punishment, by size?** v1's sweep is a
   far better response dataset than the reference sims: 128,000 games under
   256 random deficit rules, so who gets punished and by how much varies
   with the design, not with a human manager's judgement. Arrays built by
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/12_sweep_response.py, tables by
   13_response_tables.py and 14_persistence.py. Next-round change
   c(t+1)-c(t) for c in 1-4 at rounds 7-12: +0.3 unpunished, +0.5 after 1-3
   points, +1.5 after 3-7, +3.4 after 7-12, +6.8 after 12-20, +9.7 after 20-30.
   Per point of punishment that is 0.1, 0.25, 0.33, 0.4, 0.38: **small fines
   are inefficient, large fines on low contributors buy ~0.4 contribution
   points per point**, at every stage of the game (the table barely changes
   from round 0 to round 20). Mid contributors (10-13) respond ~0.3/point;
   13-16 respond little to fines over ~3. Persistence, taken at the last
   window round (no punishment afterwards): a c=1-4 player fined 12-20 is
   +6.1 next round and still +6.3 eight rounds later; fined 20-30, +9.8 then
   +8.3; c=0 fined 20-30, +11.9 then +10.5. Unpunished players drift down:
   -1 per round at 9-20, accumulating -3 to -4.5 over 8 rounds. So a big
   fine on a low contributor pays for itself many times (0.4 x 0.15 payoff
   per contribution point x ~15 rounds = 0.9 per point against a cost of
   0.5), while the fines of 1-3 points that make up v1's window tail buy
   little. The weak spot of v1 is the decay of the 13-20 contributors once
   fines stop. Next: a surrogate fitted on this sweep, checked against v1's
   actual landscape, to compare rule shapes.
3. **A surrogate that knows the real response.** Since the clones' weights
   are not in the container, I fitted a new surrogate on v1's sweep games
   plus the zero reference (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/sur2.py,
   15_train_sur2.py): an MLP over the last three rounds of own and
   others' contributions and punishments, cumulative punishment, running mean
   and round, giving a 21-way next contribution. Timeouts ignored. Checked
   with 16_validate_sur2.py by replaying v1's 256 design points (300 games
   each): correlation with the real sweep scores 0.966, mean 605.4 vs 602.1,
   zero 591.6 vs 589.4 real, and the zero decay curve matches (11.7 ->
   5.4). The v1 top points come out 637-654 against 641-653 real. So within
   deficit-type rules the surrogate is good enough to compare shapes to
   within a few points; it will extrapolate badly to fines far outside what
   v1 produced (e.g. heavy fines on full contributors). Next: rule variants
   against v1's best on this surrogate.
4. **Do simple extensions of v1 help?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-7/17_variants.py
   and 18_variants2.py, 4000 games each on the surrogate, common seeds.
   v1's best replays at 648.9 (real 649.7), zero 589.2 (real 589.4).
   Longer windows lose steadily (w12 649.3, w16 646.7, w20 641.5, w23
   635.7): the contribution level holds at 17 but the ~1 point per
   player-round of fines costs more. A fixed floor on every fine (a + slope *
   shortfall) loses 2-9; fining only shortfalls of 2+ / 4+ / 6+ loses 0-9; a
   softer or harder round 0 (round-0 slope 1 / 1.5 / 3) gives 645 / 649 /
   647; a maintenance phase after the window (fining shortfalls below 6-12,
   or fining drops from the previous round) holds the level at 16-17 to the
   end but scores 632-648, never above v1. So the surrogate says v1's shape
   is close to a local optimum: every extra point of fine after round ~10
   costs about what it brings. Next: a random search over a wider family
   (target rising over time, convex fines, escalation for repeat offenders,
   peer-relative target) to see if any axis gives more than noise.
5. **Wider family, marginal values, leniency.** 19_search.py: 400 random
   rules (1500 games each) over target, slope, window, a curvature exponent
   on the shortfall, a target rising over the rounds, escalation for repeat
   offenders and a peer-relative target: the best is 649.5 (target 16,
   slope 2.07, window 12, exponent 1.45), within noise of v1's 646.8-649.6 on
   the same surrogate. 20_marginal.py / 21_marginal_remove.py, under v1's best:
   adding 8 points of fine to any contribution bin at any round is worth
   -3.1 to +0.1 (zero to slightly negative for c <= 9 in rounds 1-10,
   clearly negative for 14-19); removing v1's fines costs 5.5 (c 0-4 at
   round 0) down to ~0 (c 5-13 at round 8). So v1's best sits at a local
   optimum of the surrogate: its fines are each worth about their cost at
   the margin, and nothing outside them pays. 22_lenient.py: shrinking the
   fine with the number of earlier fines, or stopping after K fines, never
   beats v1 (best K=6 w14: 650.6 vs 649.6, noise). The real-clone ceiling for

   design points: slope x target, the fine a full free rider gets, is
   25-32 in all of them (14 x 1.82, 17 x 1.48, 14 x 2.26, 16 x 1.84, 13 x
   2.13), i.e. right at the 30-point cap. v1's slope and target sit on a

   rider's fine F, the target and a curvature, and see how flat the
   surrogate is in those coordinates.
6. **Peer drift after the window (real sweep data).** 24_peer.py: next-round
   change of unpunished players once the window is over, by own c and the
   others' mean. A full contributor among others averaging 19.9+ still loses
   0.37 per round; among others at 10-13 it loses ~3, below 5 it loses 6-10.
   A c=13-16 player gains nothing when the others are at 20. So the decay
   after the window is intrinsic and conformist: high contributors follow
   the group down, nothing a punisher can cheaply stop (entry 4: maintenance
   fines cost what they bring). The window's job is to set the group as high
   as possible, as cheaply as possible, before it starts coasting.
7. **The reparametrised rule on the surrogate.** fine = F *
   (shortfall/target)^gam in rounds < window, F the fine for a full free
   rider, gam the curvature (1 = v1's linear shape).
   23_reparam.py (grid, window 10, 3000 games): the best cells are

8. **Drafted the rule.** punishment = F * (max(0, target - c)/target)^gam *

## Key findings

1. v1's real result (649.7 vs zero 589.4) came from large, deficit-shaped
   fines in the first ~9 rounds; its landscape was dominated by slope
   (below ~0.6 worse than zero, peak ~2.1) and target (peak 14), with taper
   flat (effect 7) (exploration 1).
2. In the real clones, a fine on a low contributor buys ~0.4 contribution
   points per point when it is large (12-30), only 0.1-0.25 when it is small,
   and the gain persists undiminished for 8+ rounds (+6.3 eight rounds after
   12-20 points on a c=1-4 player); mid-high contributors (13-16) respond
   weakly (exploration 2).
3. A surrogate trained on v1's sweep reproduces v1's real landscape
   (correlation 0.966 over 256 points; zero 591.6 vs 589.4) (exploration 3).
4. On that surrogate, nothing beyond v1's shape pays: longer windows,
   maintenance or drop fines, floors, gaps, escalation, leniency, rising or
   peer-relative targets all score at or below v1 (~649); marginal extra
   fines are worth <= 0 everywhere, removing v1's fines costs up to 5.5
   (explorations 4, 5).
5. After the window, unpunished contributions decay conformistically (a
   full contributor loses 0.37 per round even among full contributors, ~3
   among others at 10-13), so the opening campaign must set the level high
   (exploration 6).
6. v1's good points all lie on a ridge slope x target ~ 25-32, the fine of a
   full free rider; in coordinates (free-rider fine, target, curvature,
   window) the surrogate is a plateau at 646-651 over F 22-38, target 12-18,
   curvature 0.7-2.2, window 9-13, and v2's design averages 638.6 on it
   against 605.4 for v1's (explorations 5, 7, 9).

## Hypothesis

A good manager spends its punishment in an opening campaign: it fines every
player below a target in proportion to (a power of) how far short they fall,
so that a free rider gets close to the maximum fine and a near-target player
little or nothing, for the first ~10 rounds; then it stops for good and lets
the raised level coast down through the clones' conformist dynamics, since
late fines no longer have enough rounds left to repay their double cost.
The rule: punishment = free_rider_fine * (max(0, target - c)/target)^curvature
* [t < window].
- `free_rider_fine` (float, 18-40): the fine of a player contributing 0.
- `target` (int, 12-20): contribution at and above which nobody is fined.
- `curvature` (float, 0.6-2.5, log): shape of the fine in the shortfall
  (1 = linear, v1's shape).
- `window` (int, 6-14): opening rounds with fines; none afterwards.

### Justification

Findings 1, 2 and 4 support the opening campaign with large fines on low
contributors and no punishment afterwards; finding 5 says why the campaign
cannot be made to last cheaply, and finding 4 that every maintenance scheme
tried costs what it brings. `free_rider_fine` is the ridge of v1 (finding 6):
I expect 24-32 (the 30-point cap matters little to it, but above 30 it
raises the fines of players between 0 and the target when curvature > 1).
`target` I expect at 13-16 with curvature near 1, higher with a more convex
fine; 12-20 covers both. `curvature` is the new shape question: finding 2
(large fines efficient on low contributors, weak response at 13-16) argues
for >= 1, the surrogate is indifferent between 0.7 and 2.2, so 0.6-2.5 on a
log scale, centred on 1. `window` I expect at 8-12 (v1 real peak 9, flat
7-12; surrogate 9-13), so 6-14. The ranges include v1's best (F 29, target
14, curvature 1, window 9), so the sweep can at worst recover it. The
hypothesis is contradicted if the best points sit at window 14 (fines keep
paying late) or at curvature 0.6 or 2.5 (the shape matters more than the
surrogate saw). Not modelled: v1's taper (flat in its real landscape),
timeouts (the manager cannot fine them, ~2% of rows), escalation or leniency
and peer-relative targets (no gain on the surrogate, entry 5), and any late
safety net for collapsing players (neutral on the surrogate, entry 4), each
left out so that the 256 points are not spread over axes that showed
nothing.
