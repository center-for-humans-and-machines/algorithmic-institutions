# pf-20261112-3

## Explorations

1. **How does the score work, and how do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other?** Read
   `src/aimanager/simulation/pool_scores.py` and scored the reference sims with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261112-3/ref_score.py (and ref_sims.py for per-round tables).
   The score is the group's *total* pool, 1.6*sum(c) - sum(p) over members, so group size
   enters linearly. `zero` (never punishes) leads `ah` by +14.0 (se 1.4): 60.5 vs 46.5,
   because it holds 4.57 members vs 3.43. Punishment under `ah` lifts contributions
   (9.0 vs 8.1 per member) but costs ~1.8 per member per round and drives members away.
   So membership is the dominant lever; next I want to know what makes players switch.

2. **What drives switching in the human data?** Looked at decisions at rounds 3,7,11,15,19
   (one copy per game, 50 games) with human_switch.py. Switch rate 0.29 overall, 0.44 at
   the first decision. It rises steeply with punishment received in the 4-round block:
   0.19 (none), 0.24 (0-1 mean), 0.36 (1-3), 0.45 (3-6), 0.58 (>6). Logit: block mean
   punishment +0.14/pt, own group's mean punishment minus other's +0.13/pt, own group's
   mean contribution minus other's -0.08/pt, group size difference -0.09/member;
   punishment at the decision round itself adds nothing beyond the block mean.
   So people flee punishing groups and groups with low contributions; they are drawn to
   bigger, more cooperative groups. Next: does punishment raise contributions enough to
   be worth anything, and to whom?

3. **Does punishment raise contributions, in the humans and in the sims?** human_contrib.py
   and sim_response.py: next-round change in contribution by own contribution x punishment.
   Humans: a 0-4 contributor rises +0.45 unpunished, +1.5 at 2-5 points, +3.7 at >10; a
   15-20 contributor who is punished drops sharply (-5 to -16). OLS: +0.15/pt at c=0,
   shrinking by 0.02/pt per contribution point (zero effect around c=7). The `ah`-managed
   groups in the sims show the same shape but weaker: 0-4 contributors +0.99 unpunished,
   +1.74 at 2-5, +2.94 at >10. Human managers punished by shortfall from the group mean:
   10.9 at >8 below, 4.9 at 4-8 below, under 1 near or above the mean; and they punished
   most in the first block (4.3 in round 0, ~1.2 by the end). So punishment works only on
   low contributors and only modestly; punishing anyone near the mean backfires.
   Next: how long the effect lasts, and when in the block punishment costs most members.

4. **When does punishment drive members out (sims)?** sim_switch_timing.py: logit of
   switching at decisions in `ah` groups on own punishment at lags 0-3 rounds before the
   decision, the others' mean punishment, group mean contribution and size. Own punishment
   in the decision round and the one before weigh +0.041 and +0.047 per point; 2 and 3
   rounds before only +0.023 and +0.019 (se 0.003). Others' mean punishment +0.055/pt,
   group mean contribution -0.082/pt, group size -0.22/member. First decision has much
   higher exits (0.50 from `ah` vs 0.27 from `zero`). So punishment early in a block costs
   half as many members as punishment late in a block, and people prefer large,
   cooperative groups.

5. **Is the contribution gain persistent, and does it pay?** sim_persist.py: c at t+1..t+3
   on punishment at t, controlling for c_t, c_{t-1}, group mean and others' punishment.
   Effect at c=0 is +0.072, +0.070, +0.064 per point at h=1,2,3 (falls 0.006/pt per
   contribution point), and others' mean punishment adds +0.02..0.045 to bystanders.
   Back of envelope per point dealt to a free rider: ~1.6 pool points of extra
   contributions over ~10 rounds against 1 point of cost, so +0.6 net; but ~0.02 expected
   members lost (own +0.009, bystanders +0.011 exit probability) at ~100-300 pool-rounds
   swing each (a leaver adds to `ah`'s pool what it takes from ours) is ~-4 if dealt late
   in a block, ~-2 early. In the final block (no more decisions) the gain over <=3 rounds
   (~0.5) still does not cover the point spent; round 23 punishment is pure loss.
   So my prior is that the best rule is close to `zero`: at most a light, targeted
   punishment of clear free riders, early in a block. The sweep should be able to reach
   exactly zero so it can tell.

6. **Drafted the rule.** Punish a member only for shortfall below the mean of the other
   valid members of the group this round, beyond a tolerance `gap0`, at `k` points per
   point of shortfall, capped at `cap`, only in the first `w` rounds of each 4-round block
   (where exits react least), and never in round 23. Small `k` (k*20 < 1) or large `gap0`
   floor everything to 0, so `zero` is inside the design.

7. **Validated, and checked what the design spans.** validate-rule passes (256 distinct
   points). rule_on_humans.py replays the rule over the 50 human games' histories (group 0
   as the rule's group): mean punishment per member-round is 4.0 at the harshest corner
   (gap0=0, k=3, cap=15, w=4), 0.16 at gap0=4, k=0.5, cap=5, w=2, ~0.01-0.02 at mild points
   and exactly 0 at k=0.03; human managers dealt 1.94. So the design runs from `zero` to
   twice the humans' severity, with most of its mass on light, targeted punishment, which
   is where I expect the optimum.

## Key findings

1. The score is the group's total pool, so membership dominates: `zero` leads `ah` by
   +14.0 (se 1.4) pool points, holding 4.57 members vs 3.43, although `ah`'s members give
   ~0.9 more each (exploration 1).
2. Players leave punishing groups: human switch rate 0.19 with no punishment in the block
   vs 0.58 above 6 per round; in the sims 0.23 vs 0.65 (explorations 2, 4).
3. Exit reacts about twice as strongly to punishment in the last two rounds before a
   decision (+0.041, +0.047 logit/pt) as to punishment 2-3 rounds before (+0.023, +0.019);
   bystanders react to the group's punishment too (+0.055/pt) (exploration 4).
4. Punishment raises contributions only of low contributors: about +0.07/pt per round at
   c=0, persisting at least 3 rounds, zero near c=7-10, and high contributors who are
   punished drop sharply (exploration 3, 5).
5. Rough accounting says one point dealt to a free rider returns ~1.6 pool points in
   contributions but costs ~2-4 in expected lost members; in round 23 it returns nothing
   (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here wins by holding members, not by disciplining them: it should punish
little or not at all, and when it does, only clear free riders (far below what the rest
of the group gives), lightly, and early in a block between switch decisions, when exits
react least; never in the last round. The rule punishes a member
min(cap, k * max(0, mean of other active members' contribution - own - gap0)), only in
rounds with t mod 4 < w, and never at t = 23.

- `gap0` (float, [0, 12]): tolerated shortfall below the others' mean. Expect 4-10:
  human managers barely punished within 4 points of the mean, and punishment near the
  mean backfires.
- `k` (float, [0.03, 3], log): points per point of shortfall beyond gap0. Expect 0.1-0.7;
  the log range reaches down to no punishment at all (k*20 < 1 floors to 0), so `zero`
  is inside the design, and up to harsher-than-human punishment.
- `cap` (int, [1, 15]): maximum per member per round. Expect 2-6 from the costs above.
- `w` (int, [1, 4]): punish only in the first w rounds of each 4-round block. Expect 1-2.

### Justification

Finding 1 makes membership the main lever, and finding 2 shows punishment is what
removes members, so the default must be light; that is why `k` is log-scaled down to an
effective zero and the expected optimum sits low. Finding 4 says only low contributors
respond, and punishing near-average contributors loses contributions, so punishment is
keyed on shortfall from the group's others with a tolerance `gap0`, not on absolute level.
Finding 3 motivates `w`: the same punishment dealt early in a block costs about half the
exits. Finding 5 motivates `cap` (diminishing value of large punishments against linear
cost) and the fixed rule of no punishment in round 23. If the sweep's best points sit at
k=0.03 (all zero), punishment never pays against these artificial humans and `zero` is
the answer; if they sit at high k, gap0 near 0 or w=4, my membership accounting
overstates exit costs. I chose not to model the other group's state (e.g. punishing less
when `ah` punishes less, to compete for switchers) or group size, to keep the rule small
and because the exit model's per-point coefficients did not differ enough to justify the
extra parameters; nor did I target players by their switching history.
