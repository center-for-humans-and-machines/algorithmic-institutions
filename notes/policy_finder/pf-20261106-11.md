# pf-20261106-11

## Explorations

1. **What does the human data look like overall?** Looked at contributions, punishments, group sizes and pools per round with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a1_overview.py (50 games, one copy each). Mean contribution is flat around 8.2-9.7 over the 24 rounds; mean punishment falls from ~3.5 in rounds 0-3 to ~1.1 late. 71% of punishments are 0. The pool per round scales almost linearly with group size (4 members: 46.7, 6: 83.3, 7: 96.8), while contribution per head hardly depends on size. So the biggest lever on the score is how many players are in my group; next I want to see what makes players switch.

2. **What makes players leave a group?** Looked at the switch decisions after rounds 3,7,..,19 (2000 decisions) against the block's own punishment, payoff gap to the other group and group size with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a2_switch.py. Switch rate is 28.6% (43.5% after the first block, ~24% later). It rises steeply with the player's own mean punishment in the block: 18.9% at 0, 35.6% at 1-3, 44.7% at 3-6, 58.2% above 6. The payoff gap to the other group is the strongest driver (logit coef -0.12 per point; 70% leave when 10+ points behind, 10% when 10+ ahead), and own punishment adds to it (+0.11 per point) on top. Large groups keep members (gn coef -0.14). Players contributing 15-20 barely react to punishment of up to 5 (18%). So punishment is costly twice: it lowers the pool directly and drives members (and the pool) to the other group. Next: does punishment buy anything, i.e. do contributions rise after it?

3. **Does punishment raise contributions?** Looked at next-round contribution change (same group, valid rounds) by this round's contribution and punishment, the human managers' punishment by contribution, and early punishment against late pools, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a3_response.py. For low contributors it works: c in 1-5 changes +0.08 unpunished, +1.25 at p 3-5, +1.93 at p 6-10, +3.3 above 10; c in 6-10: -0.40 unpunished vs +1.15 at p 3-5. For c above 10 punishment backfires: full contributors (20) punished 3-5 drop 9.4 points next round vs 1.8 unpunished. Human managers punished 4.4 at c=0, 2.8 at 1-5, 1.6 at 6-10, 0.7-1.0 at 11-19, 0.26 at 20. Groups whose manager punished moderately in rounds 0-7 (mean 2.1-3.5 per head) had the best late pools (71.8, late c 11.7), versus 56.6 for near-zero punishers and 38.8 for the harshest quartile (mean 7.2, which also kept fewest members, 3.4). So an inverted U: targeted, moderate punishment of low contributors only; never punish contributors above ~10-12. Next: how long does the effect last, and does timing relative to the switch matters?

4. **How long does the effect last, and does timing matter for exits?** Looked at contribution changes 1-4 rounds after a punishment (players with c<=8, staying in the group) and at a logit of the switch decision on the punishment received in each of the block's four rounds, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a4_persist.py. The gain persists and even grows: c 3-5 punished 4-8 is +1.34 after 1 round and +2.30 after 4 (unpunished: ~0); c 0-2 punished 4-8 is +2.2 to +2.6 vs ~+1.0. Exit is driven far more by punishment in the last round before the decision: logit coefs 0.027, 0.021, 0.038 for block rounds 0-2 and 0.088 for round 3 (rounds 3,7,11,15,19). So: punish low contributors early in a block, go easy in the decision round; and since the benefit is in future rounds, punishment in the last round (23) is pure loss and late rounds are worth less. Next: is the right threshold absolute or relative to the group?

5. **Absolute or relative threshold?** Looked at next-round change for punished (p>=3) vs unpunished players, by own contribution and the mean of the other group members, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a5_relative.py. The response depends on where the player stands against the others: c 8-12 gains +2.54 punished vs +0.58 unpunished when the others average 14+, but loses -1.63 vs -0.43 when they average 6-10. Players at or above the others' mean react to punishment by cutting hard (c 16-20 with others at 6-10: -11.8 vs -3.5). Very low contributors (0-4) respond whatever the others do (+1.8 to +3.8 vs +0.4 to +1.4). So the target should mix a relative norm (others' mean) with an absolute anchor, and both should be swept. There are no model weights in artifacts/ (only copula json), so I cannot simulate here; the design rests on the data.

6. **Drafted the rule.** target = w * (mean contribution of the other valid members of my group this round) + (1 - w) * anchor; punishment = rate * max(0, target - c), times lam in the switch-decision rounds (3,7,11,15,19), times a horizon factor clamp((23 - t)/h, 0, 1) that is 0 in round 23 and fades in over the last h rounds. When a player is alone in the group (no others), the relative part falls back to the anchor. I left out an explicit reaction to the other manager's punishments: its effect on my group goes through the payoff gap and so through the members' contributions and switching, and with 5 parameters I would rather spend them on the levers the data shows clearly (who to punish, how much, when). Moderate, targeted punishment also keeps my group's payoffs competitive against a lenient opponent, and against a harsh one it lets me absorb migrants.

7. **A trap in the rule sandbox.** My first draft passed validate-rule but crashed with `KeyError: '__import__'` when I ran it on the human histories (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-11/a6_apply.py). In a fresh process, torch does a one-time lazy import the first time it parses certain scalar arguments (th.clamp with a number, tensor < 2.0, th.remainder, sum(keepdim=True)); inside the rule's builtins-free scope that import fails. validate-rule passed only because dry_run_inputs had already done `th.rand(...) > 0.1`, which warms it. The sim probably warms it too, but I rewrote the code to need no warm-up: constants are built from zeros_like/ones_like, clamps are th.maximum/th.minimum against tensors, comparisons are tensor against tensor, and the round-in-block is t - 4*floor(t/4). I checked it in a cold process on a hand-made case: the shortfalls against the others' mean, the halving in round 3 with lam 0.5, the 0.5 horizon factor in round 22 with h 2, and zero in round 23 all come out as intended.

8. **Does the rule's punishment level make sense on human histories?** I applied the rule to the 50 human games from group 0's seat with a6_apply.py. At rate 0.5, w 0.5, anchor 10 it deals 0.69 per head per round, about 1 outside the decision rounds. At rate 1, w 0.7, anchor 12 it deals ~2.4 per head early, close to the best human quartile in entry 3 (2.1-3.5). Rate 3 would give ~7, the harsh quartile that lost members. Human managers dealt 4.5 per head in rounds 0-3 and ~1.2 later. So rate [0.1, 3] log runs from near-lenient to harsh, and I expect the best values near 0.5-1.5, inside the range.

## Key findings

1. Group size is the main lever on the pool: the pool per round is 46.7 with 4 members, 83.3 with 6 and 96.8 with 7, while contribution per head hardly changes with size (entry 1).
2. Players leave groups that punish them and that pay less than the other group. The switch rate goes from 18.9% at zero own punishment to 58.2% above 6 per round, and each point of payoff gap to the other group shifts the log-odds by -0.12 (entry 2).
3. Punishment raises the contributions of low contributors, and the gain lasts. Players at c 3-5 punished 4-8 are +2.3 four rounds later, against ~0 unpunished (entries 3, 4).
4. Punishing players at or above their group's norm backfires. Full contributors punished 3-5 drop 9.4 next round, and c 8-12 punished among others averaging 6-10 drops 1.6. The same c 8-12 gains 2.5 when the others average 14+ (entries 3, 5).
5. The effect follows an inverted U at the group level. Moderate early punishers (2.1-3.5 per head) had the best late pools (71.8), above near-zero punishers (56.6) and the harshest quartile (38.8, fewest members) (entry 3).
6. Punishment in the round just before a switch decision drives exits about 3x as much as punishment earlier in the block (logit 0.088 vs 0.021-0.038) (entry 4).
7. The benefit only arrives in later rounds, so punishment in round 23 is pure loss and late punishment is worth less (entry 4, by construction of the game).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes sparingly and only where it pays. It targets members who fall short of the group's norm, in proportion to the shortfall, and never punishes those at or above it. It eases off in the rounds just before players choose groups and stops near the end of the game. The pool depends mostly on keeping and attracting members. Indiscriminate or harsh punishment costs the pool twice, once in the deduction and again in the members it drives to the other group, whatever that group's manager does. Targeted moderate punishment still lifts the free riders' contributions for several rounds. Against a lenient opponent this keeps my group's payoffs competitive. Against a harsh one my group pays better and takes in its leavers.

The rule: target = w * (mean contribution of the other valid members this round) + (1 - w) * anchor, and punishment = rate * max(0, target - c), times lam in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, times min(1, (23 - t) / h).
- `rate` (float, [0.1, 3] log): punishment points per point of shortfall. I expect 0.5-1.5.
- `w` (float, [0, 1]): weight of the relative norm against the absolute anchor. I expect 0.5-1.
- `anchor` (float, [4, 18]): the absolute contribution level the target is pulled towards. I expect 8-14.
- `lam` (float, [0, 1]): leniency multiplier in switch-decision rounds. I expect 0-0.5.
- `h` (float, [0.5, 8] log): rounds over which punishment fades out before round 23. I expect 1-4.

### Justification

Findings 3 and 5 support punishing at all, at a moderate level set by `rate`. Its range covers per-head levels from ~0.25 to ~7, from below the lenient human quartile to the harsh one (entry 8). Finding 4 says whom to punish: those below the others' mean, and very low contributors in any group. The target therefore mixes the group norm (`w`) with an absolute `anchor`. Both ranges are wide because the data cannot separate the two cleanly, and the artificial humans may weight them differently. Findings 1, 2 and 6 give `lam`: exits cost far more pool than a free rider's contribution, and the decision round drives exits most. Its full [0, 1] range lets the sweep choose between staying silent there and treating it as any other round. Finding 7 gives `h` and the hard zero in round 23. The log range lets the sweep pick anything from cutting off only the last round to a long fade.

Several results would contradict this hypothesis. Within the sweep, a best `rate` at the top edge would mean harsher punishment pays in the artificial humans, and a best `lam` near 1 would mean exits there do not react to timing. Against a specific opponent, a gain from punishing all members would undercut the targeting.

What I did not model:
- Any explicit reaction to the other manager's punishments `p`. Its effect on my group runs through the payoff gap, which my own leniency already serves, and with five parameters I put them where the data is clearest.
- Punishment history and escalation for repeat free riders.
- Group-size-dependent scaling.
- Different treatment of newcomers after a switch.
The data is too thin (50 games) to fit those reliably, and the moderate, targeted core should hold up whichever manager runs the other group. I also could not simulate here, because there are no model weights in artifacts/.
