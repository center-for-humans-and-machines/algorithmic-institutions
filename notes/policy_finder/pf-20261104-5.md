# pf-20261104-5

## Explorations

1. **What do the reference sims say about punishing at all?** Looked at
   `per_round.parquet` of the ah/zero pairings (pool = 1.6*sum c - sum p per
   group and round, empty groups 0) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/ref_sims.py. Found that `zero` beats
   `ah` head to head: zero's group averages a pool of 59.1 (as group 0) and
   61.8 (as group 1), ah's 44.9 and 48.1, so ~60.5 vs ~46.5. ah members contribute more
   (9.7 vs 8.2 per member and round from round 4 on) but ah charges 1-3.6
   points per member per round, and after the first switch ah's group holds
   ~3.8 members against zero's ~5.0-5.2. Most of zero's lead is group size,
   and it builds at the first switch (round 4: 51.6 vs 69.9). Group 1 also has
   a small built-in edge (ah_vs_ah 57.6 vs 54.3). So punishment buys
   ~1.5 contribution points per member (~2.4 pool points) at a cost of ~1.5
   pool points directly, and loses members; next I want to see in the human
   data how a punishment changes the next contribution, and what makes
   players leave.
2. **How does a punishment change the next contribution in the human data?**
   Looked at `contribution`, `punishment` (one copy per game, 50 games, 9600
   rows), next-round change of players who stayed in their group, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/human_response.py. Found that it
   depends on what the player gave: contributors of 0-4 who get 5-10 points
   raise their next contribution by 2.4 (0.45 unpunished), slope ~0.16-0.19
   contribution points per punishment point for contributions below 10, ~0.03
   at 10-14 and -0.45 at 15-20 (a punished full contributor drops by 5-16).
   Human managers punished low contributors most (3.3 at 0-4, 0.3 at 20) and
   punished less as the game went on (4.1 in round 0, ~1.1 from round 17).
   So only low contributors should ever be punished, and one point buys at
   most ~0.18*1.6 = 0.3 pool points per later round against 1 pool point now:
   it only pays if its effect lasts several rounds, which also says to stop
   punishing towards the end. Next: what makes players switch.
3. **What drives switching in the human data?** Looked at the 4-round blocks
   before each decision (switch = group differs next round) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/human_switch.py. Found a 28.6% switch
   rate (43.5% at the first decision, ~24% later). Switching rises with the
   punishment received in the block (18.9% at 0, 54.5% above 20 points), with
   the group's mean punishment relative to the other group's (12.8% when 2+
   points per member-round lower, 58.8% when 2+ higher) and above all with the
   payoff gap (54% when 4+ lower, 11% when 4+ higher). Low contributors switch
   more (33% vs 19% for 14-20). So punishing costs members, because payoff
   is what players compare and the punished pay twice. Next: in the sims,
   does the timing of a punishment within a block matter for switching?
4. **Do the artificial humans respond like the humans, and does timing
   within a block matter for switching?** Looked at the ah-managed groups of
   the reference sims (switch = group differs after rounds 3/7/11/15/19) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/sim_switch.py. Found that the
   artificial switchers behave like the humans (21% switch at 0 punishment in
   the block, 58% above 20 points; ah's group loses 30.6% at a decision,
   zero's 22.8%) and weigh recent punishment most: per point, +0.36, +0.61,
   +0.84 and +1.24 percentage points for the 4th, 3rd, 2nd and last round
   before the decision. Their contribution response is much weaker than the
   humans': slope 0.04-0.06 contribution points per punishment point for
   contributors below 10, -0.11 at 15-20 (humans 0.16-0.19 / -0.45). The
   per-point gain looks biggest for small punishments (contributors 5-9:
   +0.13 unpunished, +0.38 at 1-2 points, +0.57 at 3-5). So in this
   simulation a punishment point returns ~0.07 pool points a round; light
   punishments should be cheapest per unit of effect, and the decision round
   is the worst round to punish. Next: does the effect persist?
5. **Does a punishment's effect persist?** Regressed c(t+h)-c(t) on c(t),
   p(t) and the group mean for contributors below 10 who stay, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/sim_persist.py. Found the slope flat at
   ~0.045 for h = 1..6 in the sims (0.095-0.13 in the human data), so the
   effect lasts (though later punishments are mixed into it). One point at t
   then returns ~0.07 pool points in each remaining round: below break-even
   unless ~14 rounds are left, before counting the members it drives away.
   Yet in steady state (rounds 12+) ah's members give 9.7 against zero's 8.2
   at ~1.2 points of punishment per member, so a light, steady, well-aimed
   punishment may still pay, partly through who stays. My hypothesis: the
   best manager here is close to `zero`, punishing only clear free riders,
   lightly and capped, early in the game, never right before a switch
   decision and not in the last rounds. Next: draft the rule so that its
   parameters span from `zero` to a moderate punisher.
6. **Drafted the rule.** Punishment for a valid member i is
   min(cap, k * max(0, thr - c_i)), times `quiet` in the decision rounds
   (3, 7, 11, 15, 19) and 0 after round `t_stop`. A small k (k*(thr-c) < 1
   floors to 0) or small t_stop recovers `zero`, so the sweep can fall back
   to it; the cap keeps punishments at signal size, where the per-point
   effect looked best (entry 4).
7. **A step back on the code: `th.clamp(..., min=)` and `th.remainder(t, 4)`
   fail in a fresh process.** Running the rule alone with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-5/test_rule.py raised
   `KeyError: '__import__'` (torch imports lazily on these calls and the rule
   has no builtins); the validator passed only because torch was already warm.
   Replaced them with `th.relu` and `t - 4 * th.floor(t / 4)`. The test now
   gives, for k=1, thr=8, cap=5, quiet=0.5, t_stop=17 and contributions
   0/4/7/8/20: 5/4/1/0/0 in round 0, halved in rounds 3 and 11 (0.5 floors
   to 0), 0 from round 18, and 0 for timed-out and other-group players. The
   validator passes on all 256 design points.

## Key findings

1. In the reference sims `zero` beats `ah` head to head, ~60.5 against ~46.5
   pool points a round, mostly because it ends up with ~5 members against
   ~3.8 after the first switch (exploration 1).
2. Punishment only helps against low contributors: humans giving 0-9 raise
   their next contribution by ~0.17 per punishment point, those giving 15-20
   cut it by ~0.45 per point (exploration 2); the artificial humans respond
   ~4x more weakly (0.04-0.06 and -0.11, exploration 4).
3. The effect persists over at least 6 rounds at ~0.045 per point in the sims
   (exploration 5), so a point at round t returns ~0.07 pool points per
   remaining round: worthwhile, if at all, only early in the game.
4. Switching rises with the punishment a player gets and with its group's
   payoff gap to the other group (humans: 19% at 0 punishment in a block, 55%
   above 20 points; 11% vs 54% by payoff gap, exploration 3), and the
   artificial humans weigh the decision round's punishment ~3.5x the one 3
   rounds earlier (+1.24 vs +0.36 points per punishment point, exploration 4).
5. Small punishments look most efficient per point (contributors 5-9 in the
   sims: +0.25 contribution for 1-2 points, +0.44 for 3-5, exploration 4),
   and with a lighter steady punishment (~1.2 per member) ah's members
   contribute 9.7 against zero's 8.2 in rounds 12+ (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager here is mostly a non-punisher: group size is what makes the
pool, punishment costs the pool point for point and drives members away,
and the artificial humans barely respond to it. What can still pay is a
light, capped signal to clear free riders, early enough that its lasting
effect is collected over many rounds, held back in the round just before a
switch decision (where it weighs most on leaving and least on later
contributions), and dropped in the final rounds, where nothing is left to
gain. The rule punishes each valid member i of the group by
min(cap, k * max(0, thr - c_i)), times `quiet` in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15 and 19,
and not at all after round `t_stop`:

- `k` (float, [0.05, 3], log): punishment points per contribution point
  short of the threshold. At its low end the rule floors to 0 and is `zero`.
- `thr` (float, [1, 15]): the contribution from which a member is safe.
- `cap` (float, [1, 20], log): the most a member gets in one round.
- `quiet` (float, [0, 1]): the factor on punishments in the round before a
  switch decision.
- `t_stop` (int, [0, 23]): the last round with punishments.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 3 say the default should be no punishment, so `k` is
log-ranged down to values where every punishment floors to 0 and the sweep
can recover `zero`; I expect its best values low (0.1-0.8), but the upper end
of 3 lets the sweep test a strong punisher in case the steady-state gain of
finding 5 dominates. Finding 2 says to punish only below a threshold; `thr`
spans "only near-zero contributors" (1) to "anyone below the cooperators'
range" (15); I expect 4-10, around and below the ~8-10 mean contribution.
Finding 5 motivates `cap`: I expect 2-6 (signal-sized), log-ranged because
the difference between 1 and 4 matters more than between 15 and 20. Finding
4 motivates `quiet`, which I expect near 0, with the full [0, 1] range since
the punishment's contribution effect also carries over the switch for those
who stay. Finding 3 motivates `t_stop`: I expect 8-17, but [0, 23] keeps
both "stop almost at once" and "never stop" open. The hypothesis would be
contradicted if the sweep's best points sit at high k and cap with late
t_stop (punishment pays in this sim after all), or if all low-k points tie
(nothing beats `zero`). I did not model the other group's manager (ah's
punishments are what make our group attractive, and we cannot see its
rule beyond the `p` history), reactions to past punishment or repeated free
riding (the effect per point looked persistent and linear enough), newcomers
after a switch, or the group-1 edge, which the rule cannot act on.
