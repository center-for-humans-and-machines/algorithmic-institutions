# pf-20261105-10

## Explorations

1. **What do the reference sims say about the size of the prize?** Looked at
   plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/aggregates.csv. Under `zero` the mean
   contribution decays from 11.6 to 5.5 over 24 rounds and the per-player payoff
   from 26.6 to 23.0; under `humanlike` contributions rise to ~13.2 and hold,
   but punishment of 2-3 per player per round keeps payoff at 21-24, below
   `zero` for roughly the first 13 rounds. Summed, `zero` beats `humanlike`
   (about 24.4 vs 23.0 per round). Since the group-average payoff is
   20 + 0.6*mean(c) - 2*mean(p), every punishment point must buy 3.3 points of
   (future) contribution. Humanlike buys ~5 contribution points per round for
   ~2.4 punishment points: a loss. So the gap to close is efficiency: the
   contribution gain is there, the punishment is too costly. Next: how
   contributions respond to punishment in the pilots.
2. **How does the next contribution respond to punishment in the pilots?**
   Looked at contribution, punishment, player_no_input in the pilot CSV with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-10/explore_pilot.py and response.py.
   Linear fit c_next = 0.07 + 0.15 p + 0.72 c + 0.26 mean(others' c)
   (random_1: 0.20 per punishment point, human managers: 0.10). The group sum
   persists with 0.72+0.26 = 0.98 per round, so a contribution gained early
   is kept for most of the game. By cell: low contributors (c<=4) rise by
   +0.9 unpunished, +3.8 with 5-10 punishment, +6.4 with >10; full
   contributors (c=20) who are punished drop sharply (-4 to -12, few cases);
   punishing 15-19 contributors does little. Punishment at t also predicts
   c at t+2 beyond c at t+1 (+0.13/pt), so the effect seems to last. Rough
   value: 0.17/pt * ~16 rounds of persistence * 0.6 = ~1.6 group payoff per
   point against a cost of 2, so only the more responsive cases (low
   contributors, early rounds) clear the bar; and punishment never pays in
   the last rounds. Human managers in the pilots punish c=0 by ~10 and taper
   punishment roughly linearly in c, still 1-2 at c=15-19. Next: the same
   response in the artificial humans (humanlike sims), which is what my rule
   faces.
3. **Do the artificial humans respond like the pilots?** Looked at
   plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/per_round.parquet with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-10/sim_response.py. Scores (sum of
   per-round mean payoff): zero 589.3 (se 2.1), humanlike 550.6 (se 3.4); this
   is the bar, the `zero` manager beats the human-like one by ~39 points.
   Timeouts are rare (1.9% of player-rounds) and independent of manager.
   Linear fit under humanlike: c_next = 0.26 + 0.073 p + 0.78 c + 0.19 others,
   half the pilots' punishment slope. By cell (change in contribution next
   round vs unpunished): c<=4: +0.3 for p 1-2, +1.8 for p 5-10, +4.0 for
   p>10 (about 0.25-0.3 per point); c 5-9: +1.1 / +1.6 / +2.1 / +3.0 for the
   four punishment bins, diminishing per point; c 10-14: +0.8 for p 1-2,
   +1.1 for p 2-5, nothing more beyond; c 15-19: +0.6 for p 1-2, then smaller;
   c=20: punishment only lowers it (-0.3 to -1.9). So the response is
   concave in p: the first few points buy most, and big punishments are
   wasted except on near-zero contributors. Unpunished contributions drift
   down by 0.75-1.7 per round above c=10 under zero, which is the decay a
   manager fights. Also read the contribution model's training config
   (configs/training/artificial_humans/contribution/script_21_no_grid.yml):
   inputs are only previous contribution, previous punishment and previous
   validity per player, with an RNN (hidden 5) and an edge model across
   players; no round number. So the clones have no built-in end-game; a rule
   should still stop punishing at the end because the last round's
   punishment cannot buy anything. Next: fit a surrogate of the clones on
   these sims to compare rule shapes, since I cannot run the clones here.
4. **A surrogate of the clones, and first rule shapes on it.** Fitted a
   gradient-boosted classifier of next contribution (21 classes) on the
   reference sims (scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-10/surrogate.py;
   features: own c and p now and one round back, running means of own c and
   p as a stand-in for the RNN, others' mean c and p). Simulated with
   simsur.py: under `zero` it scores 586.1 (reference 589.3) with the same
   decay 11.6 -> 4.6 (reference 5.5), good. A humanlike proxy (punishment
   drawn from humanlike's punishments at the same contribution) scores 508
   and keeps c flat at 11.7, while the real humanlike reaches 13.2 at 550; so
   the surrogate likely understates how much punishment buys, and I treat it
   as a guide to shapes, not to exact values. grid1.py, gap rule
   p = k*max(0, target - c) for rounds t < stop and a flat rule: punishing
   through round 23 loses everywhere (540-574); stopping after round 8 gains
   a little (best gap k=1, target 15: 599 vs 586). grid2.py: the gain
   persists after the punishment stops (contributions decay only ~0.3/round
   afterwards, still ~10-12 at round 23 vs 4.6 under zero). Best so far a
   linearly tapering weight (1 - t/stop): k=2, target 15, stop 10 at 607.7
   (se 2.3, +22 over zero), spending ~34 punishment points per player over
   the game. Targets relative to the others' mean do slightly worse
   (590-597) than an absolute target, probably because at the start the
   others' mean is itself low (~11.6) and the point is to lift the whole
   group. Next: harder early punishment, a cap per player (the response is
   concave), and the length of the taper.
5. **Harder, capped, offset, reshaped.** grid3.py and grid4.py on the
   surrogate (1500-2000 games, common seeds, se ~2). Raising k to 3 with
   target 15 and a 10-round taper gives 609-610; k=4 or target 18-20 spends
   more (44-54 points per player) and falls back to 587-602. Capping the
   per-player punishment at 5-10 hurts (590-599): the big punishments of
   near-zero contributors are the ones that pay, as the cell table in 3
   showed (c<=4, p>10: +4 next round). A fixed base on top of the gap
   (base 2, k 2) is as good (610.5); base 5, k 1 is worse (605). The taper
   shape (linear vs squared vs square root) and forgiving players who rose
   since last round change little (606-611). So the surrogate has a broad
   plateau around +24 over zero for: punish shortfalls below ~15 at 2-3 per
   point, front-loaded, fading to zero over 6-16 rounds. Since the surrogate
   seems to understate the punishment effect (4), the real optimum may sit at
   a longer horizon or a bigger k, so I keep the ranges wide in those
   directions. Drafted the rule: weight max(0, 1 - t/horizon) times
   (base + k*shortfall) for players below target, and nothing in the last
   round, which cannot buy any future contribution.
6. **Does early punishment go with later cooperation outside the surrogate?**
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-10/early_vs_late.py, per game. Pilots:
   mean contribution in rounds 4-7 = 3.3 + 0.68 * mean punishment in rounds
   0-3 + 0.78 * round-0 mean contribution - 0.67 * punishment in rounds 4-7.
   Humanlike sims: contribution in rounds 16-23 = 1.2 + 0.70 * punishment in
   rounds 0-3 + 0.92 * round-0 contribution - 0.35 * punishment in rounds
   4-15. Early punishment goes with higher contributions much later (in the
   sims, 12+ rounds on), which fits the persistence I found in 2 and 4; late
   punishment goes with lower contributions, which I read mostly as reverse
   causation (managers keep punishing groups that stay low), but it at least
   gives no sign that late punishment pays. This supports front-loading.
   Validated the draft rule (passes, 256 distinct points) and am running the
   rule's own code over all 256 design points on the surrogate
   (design_on_surrogate.py, 300 games each) to see where the plateau sits
   inside the ranges.
7. **Where does the plateau sit inside my ranges?** Ran the rule's own code
   (via run_rule) over all 256 Sobol points of the draft ranges on the
   surrogate (design_on_surrogate.py, 300 games each; design_summary.py).
   71% of the points beat zero (586). The best points (613-617) have k 1.5-4.5,
   target 10.5-18.6, base anywhere 0.2-5.8, horizon 3-13; none of the top
   15 sits at a range edge. By quintile, the mean score falls off for k > 4
   (574), target > 17.6 (569) and horizon > 24 (566; best 598 there), and
   is flat in base. The top points trade k against target (k 4.4 with
   target 10.5-11, k 1.5-2 with target 14-18.6), i.e. what matters is how much
   is spent on the lowest contributors. Horizon 3.1 still scored 614, so I
   lowered the horizon's floor to 2 (punish rounds 0-1 only) and cut its top
   from 40 to 30 (beyond 24 the rule never fades; kept up to 30 as a hedge
   since the surrogate may understate the punishment effect, see 4); raised
   k's floor from 0.3 to 0.5, where nothing scored well. target [8, 20] and
   base [0, 6] stay. Revalidated: passes, 256 distinct points.

## Key findings

1. The bar is high: the never-punishing `zero` manager scores 589.3 against
   550.6 for `humanlike`. Each punishment point costs the group 2 payoff
   points, so it must buy 3.3 contribution points later. Humanlike buys
   about 5 contribution points per round for about 2.4 punishment points, a
   net loss (1, 3).
2. Contributions are sticky. The group sum carries over at about 0.98 per
   round in the pilots (0.97 in the clones), and under `zero` it decays
   slowly, from 11.6 to 5.5 over 24 rounds. So a gain bought early lasts
   most of the game (2, 3, 6).
3. The response to punishment is concave and depends on the level. Near-zero
   contributors rise by about 4 points the round after a punishment over 10
   (roughly 0.25-0.3 per point). Players at 10-19 gain about 1 point from a
   punishment of 1-5, and more punishment adds nothing. Punishing a full
   contributor only lowers them (3).
4. The clones see only previous contribution, punishment and validity, with
   an RNN and no round number. They have no built-in end game, but a
   punishment in the last round has no future to buy (3).
5. On a surrogate of the clones (586 under zero, reference 589), punishing
   for the whole game loses (540-574). Front-loaded punishment that fades to
   zero by rounds 3-14 gains about +25 (613-617). After the punishment stops,
   contributions still sit at 10-13 in round 23, against 4.6 under zero.
   Capping each player's punishment at 5-10 hurts (4, 5, 7).
6. Early punishment goes with higher contributions much later in both the
   pilots and the humanlike sims (+0.7 per point of mean punishment in
   rounds 0-3). Later punishment goes with lower contributions (6).
7. The surrogate likely understates what punishment buys. A humanlike proxy
   on it keeps contributions at 11.7, where the real clones reach 13.2 (4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes hard and early, then stops. Contributions are sticky
(about 0.98 carry-over per round), so lifting them in the first rounds pays
for the rest of the game. A punishment late in the game has too few rounds
left to repay its double cost. Punishment should go to the players below a
cooperative level, and grow with how far below they are, because near-zero
contributors respond most. Players at or above that level should never be
punished, because punishing them is pure loss. The rule punishes
`weight(t) * (base + k * (target - c))` for every player with `c < target`.
Here `weight(t) = max(0, 1 - t / horizon)` fades linearly from 1 in round 0,
and the last round (t = 23) is never punished.

- `k`: punishment points per point of shortfall below the target, at full
  strength. Sweep [0.5, 8, log]. I expect 1.5-4.
- `target`: the contribution below which a player is punished. Sweep [8, 20].
  I expect 11-16.
- `base`: a fixed amount added for any shortfall, a nudge for players just
  below the target, where the response is concave. Sweep [0, 6]. I expect
  little effect, with the best value anywhere from 0 to 5.
- `horizon`: the round by which punishment has faded to zero. Sweep
  [2, 30, log]. I expect 4-14. Values above 24 never fade fully and hedge
  against the real clones responding more than the surrogate.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 4 say punishment must buy future contributions, and late
punishment cannot. Findings 2, 5 and 6 say early gains persist. Together they
give the fading `horizon` and the silent last round. Finding 3 gives
shortfall-proportional punishment with nothing at or above `target`: the
lowest contributors respond most per point, and punishing full contributors
backfires. Finding 5 says capping hurts, so the rule has no cap besides the
game's 30. `base` tests the concavity in finding 3 for players just below
the target. The surrogate found it flat (finding 5, entry 7), so it is the
parameter most likely to show little effect.

The ranges are set around the surrogate's plateau (entry 7). They are widened
toward longer horizons and larger k because of finding 7: if the real clones
respond more strongly, the optimum moves toward more and longer punishment.
The hypothesis would be contradicted if the sweep's best horizon came out
above 24, meaning steady punishment pays, or if the best points had tiny k
and base, meaning punishment does not pay at all and `zero` is near-optimal.
It would also be contradicted if contributions collapsed soon after the
punishment fades. The surrogate says they do not, but the real RNN may hold
memory differently.

Things I chose not to model:
- Targets relative to the others' mean. They did slightly worse on the
  surrogate (entry 4).
- Forgiving players whose contribution rose since last round, and the taper's
  shape. Both had no clear effect (entry 5).
- Timeouts. They are rare (1.9%), independent of the manager, and a
  timed-out player cannot be punished.
- Re-punishing a group that relapses after the fade. This would need state
  the surrogate cannot assess, and it costs a parameter.
