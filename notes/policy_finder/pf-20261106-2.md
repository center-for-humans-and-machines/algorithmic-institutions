# pf-20261106-2

## Explorations

1. **How do contributions respond to punishment?** Looked at next-round contribution change (same group, valid rows) by contribution bin and punishment bin in the deduped human data (50 games) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-2/a1_response.py. Mean contribution is flat around 9-10 over all 24 rounds. Low contributors (0-4) rise by +0.5 unpunished, +0.9 at p 1-2, +1.6 at p 3-5, +3.7 at p 11-30; mid contributors (5-9) +0.6 / +1.1 / +2.6. High contributors punished fall hard (c=20 punished 3-5: -9.4 vs -1.8 unpunished). So punishment works on low contributors but with a poor return: a 15-point punishment buys about 3 contribution points (4.8 pool) next round while costing 15 pool now, and punishing anyone at or above the group mean is clearly harmful. Part of the low-contributor rise is regression to the mean. Next: what drives switching, since the pool scales with group size.
2. **What drives players to leave a group?** Looked at the 5 switch decisions (rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19) per player against the block of 4 rounds before them with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-2/a2_switch.py. 31% of decisions are switches (44% at the first, ~25% later). The switch rate climbs with the player's own punishment over the block: 20% unpunished, 32% at 3-5 points, 46% at 11-20, 56% above 20. It climbs with the group's mean punishment relative to the other group's (13% when ours punishes >2/round/player less, 59% when >2 more) and falls with per-capita pool advantage (46-53% when ours is >4 points per head behind, 9-12% when >4 ahead). A logit has all three significant (own pun +0.031/pt, punishment gap +0.17, per-capita pool gap -0.065) and contribution not. Since our score is the group's total pool, which scales with members, punishing costs twice: the points themselves and members driven to the other group. The sim's switch model sees common_good and own punishment, so it inherits this. Next: what the human managers did and how their punishment level relates to their group's pool over the game.
3. **What did the human managers do, and how did it pay?** Looked at per-game, per-group means (pool incl. empty-group zeros, members, punishment per member-round) with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-2/a3_managers.py. Across 100 manager-games, pool by punishment-rate quintile: 0.24/member-round -> pool 57.8 (4.7 members), 0.97 -> 58.9, 1.51 -> 63.9 (4.0), 2.54 -> 50.8, 5.24 -> 28.1 (3.0 members). Within a game, the more-punishing manager's group averages 15.7 pool points less. Managers targeted low contributors (c=0 punished 4.4 on average, 43% of the time; c=20 only 0.26). So a mild, targeted level (around 1-1.5 per member per round) is at least not worse than none, and heavy punishment loses about half the pool, largely via members. Next: does the timing of punishment within the 4-round block matter for leaving, i.e. can punishment be put where it costs fewest members?
4. **Does timing within a block matter, and how long does a punishment's effect last?** Looked at a logit of switching on the punishment in each of the 4 rounds of the block (p0 = the decision round itself) plus the pool, and OLS of a low contributor's (c<=8) contribution 1-4 rounds later on this round's punishment, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-2/a4_timing.py. Punishment in the decision round weighs 0.063 per point on the switch logit, the round before 0.043, the first two rounds of the block 0.023 (not significant). The contribution gain of a low contributor per punishment point is 0.16 at t+1 and stays at 0.18-0.22 through t+4 (confounded by later punishment, so an upper bound). Rough budget: one point buys ~0.18 contribution per round, ~0.3 pool per round left, so it pays only with >= ~4 rounds left; at a decision round it also raises the punished player's exit odds about three times as much as early in the block. A free rider who leaves takes only ~3 pool per round with him, but the group-level punishment gap (entry 2) moves everyone. I could not simulate here (the artifacts hold no model weights, no sim checkout of the AHs), so the rule has to rest on these numbers. So: punish only clear under-contributors, by deficit, lightly; damp it in the decision rounds (3, 7, 11, 15, 19); stop in the last few rounds. Next: draft the rule.
5. **Drafted the rule.** Punishment = s * max(0, T - c) for valid members, times d in decision rounds (t = 3, 7, 11, 15, 19), zero in the last L rounds, and multiplied by (1 + k * r_opp), where r_opp is the other manager's punishment per contributing member per round over the last 4 rounds (from `p`, `in_group`, `valid`). The opponent term is my answer to the "whatever the other manager" requirement: entry 2 says players leave for the less punishing group, so when the other manager punishes hard we can afford to punish more and still look lenient, while against a lenient one we stay light. A tiny s (0.05 * 16 < 1 point, floored to 0) makes "never punish" reachable inside the range, so the sweep can find it if punishment does not pay in the sim at all.
6. **Validated and hand-checked the draft.** `validate-rule` passes (256 distinct design points). With scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261106-2/t1_rule.py on a toy history (our members give 0, 5, 10, 20) at T=10, s=0.5, d=0.3, L=2, k=0.5: round 5 gives 5, 2, 0, 0; round 7 (decision) 1, 0, 0, 0; round 22 nothing; with the other manager charging 4 to each of its members, round 5 gives 15, 7, 0, 0. Behaves as intended. Ranges: T [2, 16] uniform (human managers' punishment fell off around c = 10, the median contribution; 2 is "only near-zero givers", 16 "all but the generous"); s [0.05, 2] log (0.05 floors to no punishment at any shortfall, 2 hits the 30 cap at a 15-point shortfall; I expect 0.2-0.6, i.e. a few points on a free rider, the 1-1.5 per member-round band of entry 3); d [0, 1] (expect low, 0-0.4, from entry 4); L int 0-6 (expect 2-4: a point pays only with ~4 rounds left, and rounds 20-23 have no switch cost); k [0, 1.5] (expect 0.2-0.8; 0 switches the opponent term off, so the sweep can reject it).

## Key findings

1. Punishment raises a low contributor's contribution, but weakly: about +0.16-0.2 contribution per punishment point, sustained over the next 4 rounds (entries 1, 4); punishing a player at or above the group mean cuts their contribution sharply (c=20 punished 3-5: -9.4 next round vs -1.8 unpunished, entry 1).
2. Punishment drives players out: switch rate 20% unpunished vs 46-56% after more than 10 points in the block, +0.17 logit per point of punishment gap to the other group, and the per-capita pool gap matters too (entry 2).
3. Groups whose human manager punished most (5.2 per member-round) averaged pool 28 with 3.0 members, against 58-64 with 4-4.7 members for managers at 0.2-1.5; the more punishing manager of a game ends 15.7 pool points behind (entry 3).
4. Punishment in the decision round weighs about three times as much on leaving as punishment early in the block (0.063 vs 0.023 per point, entry 4).
5. A punishment point repays (~0.3 pool per remaining round) only with about 4 or more rounds left, so late punishment is a pure cost (entry 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager in this game keeps its group large and its pool high by being visibly milder than its rival: the pool is the group's contributions times 1.6 times its size, and members leave a punishing group, so punishment has to be cheap, targeted and well timed. It punishes only clear under-contributors, in proportion to their shortfall, lightly; it holds back in the rounds right before players choose groups and in the last rounds, where punishment cannot repay itself; and it reads the other manager's punishments, punishing more only when the rival is harsher, so it stays the more attractive group either way.

The rule: punishment = s * max(0, T - c) for each contributing member, times d in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, zero in the last L rounds, times (1 + k * r_opp) with r_opp the other manager's punishment per contributing member per round over the last 4 rounds.
- T, target contribution, float, [2, 16] uniform.
- s, points per point of shortfall, float, [0.05, 2] log-uniform (the low end is no punishment at all).
- d, factor in decision rounds, float, [0, 1] uniform.
- L, final rounds without punishment, int, 0-6.
- k, growth of severity with the rival's punishment rate, float, [0, 1.5] uniform.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 3 give the shape: punish by shortfall below a target (T), never above it, and lightly (s, expected 0.2-0.6), since heavy managers lost half their pool. T is centred on the median contribution of ~10 where human managers stopped punishing, with room either way. Finding 2 is why severity is low and why the rule watches the rival (k): the switch decision weighs the punishment gap, so the same punishment costs fewer members against a harsh rival than a lenient one. Finding 4 gives d (expected near 0-0.4), finding 5 gives L (expected 2-4). What would contradict it: a sweep peak at s high with T high (punishment pays in the sim despite exits), or k at 0 and d at 1 (timing and rival do not matter to the artificial humans, whose switch model sees only pool, own punishment and group). Not modelled: rewarding or treating newcomers differently, conditioning on a player's own history (repeat offenders), and group-size-dependent severity; the data are too thin to set them and they would cost parameters better spent on the main levers. All human-data numbers are correlational (bad groups both punish more and lose members), which is why the ranges are wide and the low end of s allows no punishment.
