2. **Do repeat offenders keep responding?** 30_repeat_response.py on all
   256k sweep games: change of c by t+1 and t+4, by own c, fine size and
   number of earlier fines >= 10. A c=0-4 player fined 20-30 in rounds 1-3
   with no earlier big fine: +12.3 / +14.9; with one: +9.4 / +13.9; three or
   more: +7.9 / +13.1. Unfined: +1.1 / +1.2. So repeat offenders still
   respond (by t+4 almost as much), and the gain grows from t+1 to t+4
   rather than fading: leniency for non-converters would be wrong, which
   agrees with v2's surrogate. Fined 10-19 instead of 20-30: +7.8 / +9.6.
   Per point, the marginal 10 points above ~15 on a free rider still buy
   ~0.45 contribution points that persist; at 0.6/4 payoff per point-round
   over ~15 rounds that is ~1.0 against a cost of 0.5. So fines on free
   riders should be as large as allowed (the cap 30), consistent with F
   running to the top of v2's range. Mid contributors (10-14) fined 1-9 in
   rounds 1-3: +0.8 / +1.6 vs -0.7 / -1.2 unfined, but this is confounded
   by the rule (the rules that fine c=10-14 also fine their peers harder).
   Next: since the old surrogate missed curvature, train a better one on
   real games and check it out of sample, train on v1's sweep, predict v2's.
3. **A richer surrogate, checked out of sample.** sur3.py (30 features:
   own c lags, 5-round and running means, own max; own fines, count, max,
   rounds since the last; the others' sorted contributions, fines and
   cumulative fines; round), trained on v1's real sweep games only
   (31_train_sur3.py), then replaying both designs (32_eval_sur3.py, 150
   games per point). In sample (v1): correlation 0.937. Out of sample (v2's
   256 points): 0.72, and it is wrong in the same direction as the old one:
   its favourites are low-curvature, low-target rules (curvature 0.74 ->
   662 on the surrogate, 634 real), i.e. it overrates fines on mid
   contributors. So surrogates learnt from one family do not transfer to
   the shape of the fine; I will trust the real sweep data directly for
   anything about shape, and use a surrogate only as a rough check of
   magnitudes inside the data's support.
4. **What does a late fine on a low contributor buy (real data)?**
   33_late_value.py, then 34_late_net.py: for rounds 8-18, players at c<=4
   (and 5-8), fined vs unfined, matched on round, number of earlier big
   fines and the others' mean (4 bins); own extra contribution summed over
   the rest of the game; value = 0.15 x gain - 0.5 x fine in group-mean

   long-window rule fines them too). Fines of 1-4 on the same players are

   own value is <= 0 from t8 on. The striking fact is in the finals:
   the post-window decay is carried by players falling to free riding. In
   v2's s132 the share of valid players at c<=4 is 1.8% at round 10, then
   2.8, 4.1, 5.6, 6.7, 8.6, 10.3% at rounds 12-22 (c<=8: 3.7% -> 15.6%).
   So the decay is not everyone sliding a little: it is defectors appearing
   one by one and staying low, with the conformist pull of entry 6 of v2
   dragging the rest. That makes a cheap target: a "defector net" after the
   opening that fines only players who fall to near zero, hard, as long as
   enough rounds remain to repay it. v2's flat window hides this, because
   its long windows also kept fining 8-12 contributors with mid fines that

## Key findings

1. Every good rule of the opening-campaign family scores 648-652 in the
   real finals (v1 649.7, v2's s132 652.4, s078 651.0): the family is
   saturated, and v2's landscape fixed its shape (convex fine, curvature
   ~2.2, target 18-20, F >= 35) while its window was flat over 6-14
   (exploration 1).
2. Repeat offenders keep responding to big fines: a c<=4 player fined 20-30
   in rounds 1-3 gains +14.9 by t+4 with no earlier big fine and +13.1 with
   three or more; small fines (1-4) on the same players buy nothing
   (explorations 2, 4).
3. The post-window decay is carried by defectors: under s132 the share of
   players at c<=4 grows from 1.8% at round 10 to 10.3% at round 22 (c<=8
   3.7% -> 15.6%) (exploration 4).
4. A big fine (~23) on a c<=4 player late is still worth its cost in the
   real sweep data: own value +5.8 per fine at round 8, +2.9 at 10, 0 at 12,
   +29.7 / +19.5 / +11.2 when the peers' (partly confounded) gains are
   counted; on c=5-8 players it is <= 0 from round 8 on (exploration 4).
5. Surrogates trained on one rule family transfer poorly to another
   (v1-trained: correlation 0.72 on v2's points, overrating fines on mid
   contributors); trained on both sweeps they fit in sample (0.94 / 0.83)
   but sit ~10-15 below the real scores, so only differences are used
   (explorations 3, 5).
6. On that surrogate, a narrow defector net (c <= 2-4, until round 14-16)
   after a 4-6 round opening adds +4 to +5 over the best plain window and
   over s132, cutting total punishment from 29.5 to ~24-26 per player;
   wide or end-to-end nets lose (floor 8 to round 23: -8) (explorations 5,
   6, 7).
7. The v3 design averages 5.7 above v2's design on the same surrogate
   (635.8 vs 630.1), its window optimum (4-6) lying inside the range
   (exploration 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager raises the group with a short, hard opening campaign and
then guards it cheaply. In the first few rounds it fines every player by a
convex function of the shortfall below full contribution, so free riders
get close to the 30-point cap and near-full contributors nothing; this lifts
the group from ~11 to ~16. After that the group's decline comes from
individual players dropping to near zero one by one, and the others
following them down; so instead of fining everyone a little for many more
rounds, the manager keeps only a net that fines those defectors, hard, until
too few rounds remain to repay a fine, and then stops.
punishment = F x (max(0, 20-c)/20)^curvature x ([t < window] +
net_scale x [window <= t < net_end] x [c <= net_floor]).
- `free_rider_fine` (float, 24-52): the fine of a player contributing 0
  before the cap; above 30 it raises the fines of players between 0 and 20.
- `curvature` (float, 1.5-4.0, log): convexity of the fine in the shortfall.
- `window` (int, 2-11): rounds of the opening campaign.
- `net_floor` (int, 0-8): after the opening, only players at or below it
  are fined.
- `net_end` (int, 10-24): round at which the net stops (<= window: no net).
- `net_scale` (float, 0.4-2.0, log): a net fine relative to the opening
  schedule at the same contribution.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 7 set the opening: the schedule is v2's best shape with the
target fixed at 20; I expect curvature 2-3 (v2's real peak 2.2, the
surrogate's top points 2.4-3.3) and F 32-45 (v2's real profile rising to 37
and flat to its top edge), hence 1.5-4.0 and 24-52. The window I expect at
4-7: v2's real profile peaked at its lower edge 6, and with a net taking
over the late fines the surrogate's optimum is 4-6 (exploration 7); 2-11
lets the sweep return to v2's long openings if the net does not pay.
Findings 3 and 4 motivate the net: the decline is defectors, and big fines
on them still pay around rounds 8-12 while small fines and fines on 5-8
contributors do not, hence a floor and a large fine. I expect net_floor 2-5
(surrogate best 2-4; 8 lets the sweep test a wider net), net_end 14-18
(own value of a fine falls to 0 by round 12 but peer gains extend it;
surrogate best 13-19; 24 = to the end, 10 = almost no net) and net_scale
0.6-1.2 (surrogate prefers 0.6-1, the real data large fines; 0.4-2 log).
Finding 2 is why the net does not go lenient on repeat offenders. The
hypothesis is contradicted if the sweep puts net_end <= window+1 or
net_floor at 0 at its peak (the net does not pay), or window at 11 (the
long opening was better after all). Not modelled: timeouts (cannot be
fined, ~2%), drops relative to a player's own past (a floor is simpler and
the defectors fall to near zero), and rewards for the cooperative, which
the game does not allow; finding 5 is why the net's details are left wide
for the sweep rather than fixed from the surrogate.
