# pf-20261104-10

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other on the stack I face?**
   Looked at the reference sims' per_round.parquet (pool = 1.6*sum c - sum p
   per group, as in pool_scores.py) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/ref_sims.py. Found that never punishing
   beats `ah`: zero's group scores 60.5 (se 0.95) against ah's 56.0, ah vs ah
   56.0. zero's group grows to 4.9 members after the first switch (4.6 on
   average), while its members' mean contribution drifts down from 8.6 to 7.9.
   Under ah contributions climb from 8.7 to 10.5 but ah spends ~3.7 points per
   member early (about 14 per group-round) falling to ~1 late. So punishment
   buys about 1-2 contribution points per head (worth 1.6-3.2 pool points) at a
   cost of the punishment itself plus lost members. The bar is ~60; next I want
   to know from the human data how much a punishment actually moves the
   punished player's next contribution, and what makes players switch.

2. **Does a punishment raise the punished player's next contribution (humans)?**
   Looked at experiments/2group_8agent_50ep.csv (one copy per game, 50 games),
   change in a player's contribution from t to t+1 within a block, by own
   contribution and punishment received, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/human_response.py. Found it depends on
   who is hit: contributors at 0-4 rise by +0.45 unpunished, +0.99 after 1-3
   points, +1.54 after 4-6, +2.9 after 7-10, +3.7 after more; 5-9 contributors
   go from -0.25 to +0.81 after a 1-3 punishment. Contributors at 15-20 drop
   sharply when punished (-5 to -16). Humans' managers punish 32% of the time,
   1.87 on average. So small punishments of low contributors buy about +1
   point next round, with diminishing returns per punishment point (about
   0.5/point for 1-3, 0.2/point for 10+); never punish high contributors. Next:
   the cost side, who leaves.

3. **What makes humans leave a group?** Looked at the same data per player and
   4-round block (switch after blocks 0-4) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/human_switch.py. Found leaving rises
   steeply with punishment received in the block: 19% when never punished, 24%
   at a mean of up to 1/round, 36% at 1-3, 45% at 3-6, 58% above. It also rises
   with the other group's payoff advantage (11% when own group pays 3+ more,
   53% when the other pays 3+ more) and with own group's punishment level over
   the other's (13% to 59%). Leaving is highest after the first block (44%).
   Since the score is a group *sum*, each departed member (~8.5 contribution,
   ~13.6 pool per round) costs far more than a punishment buys. So the rule
   must punish little, if at all, before switch decisions, and keep the
   group's payoff above the other group's. Next: check that the artificial
   humans in the reference sims show the same responses.


4. **Do the artificial humans respond like the humans?** Looked at the
   ah-managed groups of the reference sims with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/sim_response.py. Found the same shape,
   a bit flatter: 0-4 contributors rise +1.12 unpunished, +1.75 after 1-3,
   +2.1 after 4-6, +3.0 after 10+; 5-9 go from +0.23 to +0.52/+0.77/+1.22;
   15-20 fall further when punished (-1.1 to -4.3). Leaving per block: 22%
   unpunished, 25.5% at a mean of up to 1/round, 34% at 1-3, 46% at 3-6, 61%
   above. ah's groups lose 46% of members after block 0, zero's 27%. In zero
   groups leaving falls with contribution (26% for 0-5, 18% for 15-20). So the
   sim stack reproduces the human trade-off. Next: does the effect last?

5. **Does the effect of a punishment persist, and spill over?** Looked at
   c(t+k)-c(t), k=1..3, after punishments at block starts in ah_vs_ah, and a
   regression of the next change on own c, group mean c, sqrt(own p) and
   sqrt(mean p of others), with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/sim_persistence.py. Found the gap over
   the unpunished holds (0-4 contributors punished 1-3: +0.56, +0.54, +0.69
   at k=1,2,3). Regression: dc = -0.39 c + 0.35 gmean + 0.18 sqrt(p_own) +
   0.04 sqrt(p_others). Players revert to the group mean, and at the group
   level the two terms nearly cancel, so a raise persists. Spillover is
   negligible and the response is concave. So a raise is worth ~1.6 per point
   per remaining round: front-load punishment, keep it small and stop it
   before the end. Next: timing within a block.

6. **Does it matter when in a block a punishment falls?** Fitted a logistic
   model of leaving in ah-managed groups, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/sim_leave_timing.py. Found per point
   the punishment in the block's last round (just before the decision) weighs
   0.043 against 0.010 per point dealt in the first three rounds, about 4x;
   the other group's payoff advantage weighs 0.107 per point and own
   contribution -0.026. Unpunished early but hit 4+ in the last round: 37%
   leave vs 22%. So spare or soften the decision rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19.
   Next: what did the best human managers do?

7. **Which human managers had the largest pools?** With
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/human_managers.py. Found humans punish
   0-4 contributors most (3.7 mean) and 20s least (0.27). They punish much
   more in block 0 (3.9) than later (1.1-1.3), and no less in decision rounds
   (1.87 vs 1.78). Pool per group-episode by quartile of mean punishment:
   <=0.8: 55.2 (4.65 members); 0.8-1.5: 67.0 (4.39); 1.5-2.8: 47.3 (3.70);
   >2.8: 38.1 (3.26). This rests on only 100 group-episodes, but the light
   punishers did best.

8. **Drafted the rule.** Member i at round t gets min(cap, slope *
   max(0, c0 - c_i)), times `w_dec` in decision rounds and 0 after `t_stop`.
   I chose an absolute threshold because the response depends on the
   player's own level (exp. 2, 4), and reversion to the group mean already
   does the relative work. Small slopes floor to 0, so the design also
   contains near-`zero` managers.

9. **Does the rule's spend land where I want it?** Applied it statically to
   the zero-managed groups' contributions with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-10/rule_spend.py. A central point (c0
   9.5, slope 0.55, cap 6.5, t_stop 13, w_dec 0.5) spends 0.77 per
   member-round and punishes 28% of members, near the best human quartile.
   The low corner punishes no one. The high corner with c0 16 spent 9.4 per
   head, hitting 15s, which backfires (exp. 2, 4). So I narrowed c0 to
   [3, 14]. The rule validates on all 256 design points.

## Key findings

1. Never punishing beats `ah` on this stack, 60.5 vs 56.0, mainly because
   zero's group grows to 4.6 members on average (exp. 1).
2. Punishment raises only low contributors' contributions, concavely: 0-4
   contributors +0.6 after 1-3 points, +1.9 after 10+; 15-20 cut theirs
   (exp. 2, 4).
3. The raise persists, because players revert to a group mean that itself
   barely reverts (-0.39 c + 0.35 gmean). That makes a raise worth ~1.6 per
   remaining round (exp. 5).
4. Leaving rises steeply with punishment: 22% unpunished, 34% at 1-3/round,
   61% above 6 (exp. 3, 4).
5. A point dealt just before a switch decision drives ~4x the leaving of a
   point dealt earlier in the block (0.043 vs 0.010) (exp. 6).
6. Light human punishers (mean 0.8-1.5) had the largest pools: 67 vs 55 for
   near-zero and 38 for heavy (exp. 7).

## Hypothesis

A good manager keeps its group large and its payoff above `ah`'s. It punishes
only where that pays: small, capped punishments of clear free riders, early,
outside the rounds just before a switch, and never at the end. The rule
gives min(cap, slope * max(0, c0 - c)), weighted by `w_dec` in rounds 3, 7,
11, 15, 19, and 0 after `t_stop`.

- `c0` (float, [3, 14]): contribution threshold; expected 7-11.
- `slope` (float, [0.1, 3], log): points per point of shortfall; expected
  0.3-1. The low end is a `zero` manager.
- `cap` (float, [1, 12]): the most one member gets per round; expected 2-6.
- `t_stop` (int, 4-23): last round punished; expected 10-19.
- `w_dec` (float, [0, 1]): decision-round weight; expected near 0.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the bar: losing members loses to `zero`, so the rule's spend
starts far below ah's and the sweep can reach zero spend. Findings 2 and 6
support `c0` and `cap`. Finding 3 supports `t_stop`. Findings 4 and 5 support
a small `slope` and `w_dec`. Two outcomes would contradict the hypothesis: a
peak with `slope` at its low end (punishment does not pay on this stack), or
`c0` at 14 (targeting should be relative). I did not model three things: a
threshold relative to the group mean (the response depends on a player's
own level), reactions to the other group's state (the five-parameter limit),
and timed-out players (they are charged 0).
