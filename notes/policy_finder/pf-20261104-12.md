# pf-20261104-12

## Explorations

1. **What does the score reward, and how do `ah` and `zero` fare against `ah`?**
   Read `simulation/pool_scores.py` and ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/ref_pools.py on the reference sims.
   The score is the group's *total* pool, sum over members of 1.6c - p, so
   group size counts as much as contributions. Against `ah`, `zero` scores
   60.5 (4.57 members) and `ah` itself 56.0 (4.00). Almost all of `zero`'s
   edge appears at the first switch (after round 3): about 0.9 members move
   from `ah`'s group to `zero`'s, and the pool jumps from 52 to 65. Under
   `ah`, members contribute ~1.4 more per round late on (9.5 vs 8.0), but
   pay ~1-3.7 punishment each, which comes straight out of the pool. So
   punishment buys some contribution but costs both pool and members; next I
   want to know from the human data and sims how big the contribution
   response to punishment is, and what drives switching.
2. **In the human data, what drives switching and how do contributions answer
   punishment?** Ran scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/human_switch.py
   (one copy per game, 50 games). Switching is highest at the first decision
   (43.5%, then 24-29%). It rises steeply with punishment received in the
   4-round block before the decision: 19% with none, 24% for 1-4, 35% for
   5-10, 44% for 10-20, 48-67% above 20; also with the group's mean
   punishment (20% below 1/round, 40% at 2.5-5, 65% above 5). High
   contributors (16-20) barely leave even when punished. Next-round change
   in contribution of players who stayed: low contributors (0-5) rise +1.0
   unpunished vs +1.3 to +2.3 after 2-10 punishment; mid (6-10) -0.4 vs
   +0.7 to +1.2. So punishment of a few points buys ~1-1.5 points of
   contribution next round (~2 pool points) at a cost of the punishment
   itself and a higher chance of losing the member. Human managers punished
   by contribution level (3.3 at 0, 0.3 at 20) and much harder in rounds 0-3
   (3.4-3.7) than later (~1.2). Next: the same in the simulation, which is
   what the rule faces.
3. **Does the simulated stack behave like the humans?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/sim_response.py on the reference
   sims (1000 episodes per pairing). Yes, closely. Members of `ah`'s group
   switch out at 43.8% at the first decision vs 29.9% from `zero`'s, then
   25-28% vs 22-24%. Switching rises with punishment received in the block
   (23% none, 34% for 5-10, 43% for 10-20, 55% for 20-40), and with
   punishment in the *last* round before the decision alone (26% none, 42%
   for 3-5, 66% above 10). High contributors switch less (18% vs 28-29% for
   0-5 under `zero`), and punishment pushes them out less, but it still
   does (18% -> 32% at 10-20). Next-round contribution of stayers: for a
   0-5 contributor +0.5 unpunished, +1.0 at 1-2, +1.3 at 3-5, +1.9 at 6-10;
   for a 6-10 contributor -0.1 vs +0.4 at 3-5, +0.7 at 6-10. Punishing a
   contributor of 11+ gains nothing (and above 10 points, a loss). So the
   one-round return on a punishment point is about 0.2-0.3 contribution
   points, i.e. 0.3-0.5 pool points per point paid: punishment only pays if
   its effect persists over many rounds, and it costs members at switches.
   The models are not available here (artifacts has no .pt files), so I
   cannot simulate; the rule must be reasoned from these tables.
4. **Is the gain under `ah` composition or treatment, and does it last?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/sim_persist.py on ah_vs_zero and
   zero_vs_ah. Players who never switch contribute 10.65 in round 0 under
   `ah` vs 9.52 under `zero` (selection: the generous stay), but by round 4
   it is 12.5 vs 9.6 and stays ~12.6 vs ~8.9 to the end: a treatment gap of
   ~2.5 points per member per round (~4 pool points) against `ah`'s ~1-1.5
   punishment per member. In `ah`'s group, a 1-5 contributor punished 4-8
   gains +1.5-1.8 over the next 4 rounds vs +0.6-0.9 unpunished, flat from
   t+1 to t+4, so the effect persists rather than fading. A punishment
   point then returns ~0.2 contribution per remaining round, ~0.3 pool
   points per round: it pays back once 3-4 rounds remain, if the member
   stays. So punishment is productive inside the group; `ah` loses because
   it costs members at switches (0.9 member is ~13 pool points per round).
   Hypothesis forming: punish low contributors, but keep quiet in the
   rounds just before a switch decision, and stop when too few rounds remain
   to pay back. Next: does the timing of punishment within a block matter
   for switching?
5. **Does it matter when in the block a member is punished?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/sim_timing.py: a logistic
   regression of switching at a decision on the member's punishment in each
   of the block's 4 rounds, the group's mean punishment in each, the
   member's last contribution and a first-block flag (`ah`'s groups,
   ah_vs_ah and ah_vs_zero). Log-odds per punishment point: +0.004 in the
   block's first round, +0.015 in the second, +0.027 in the third, +0.031
   in the decision round; the group's mean punishment adds +0.016, +0.030,
   +0.029, +0.043. Contribution -0.037 per point; first block +0.54. So
   punishment early in a block is almost free in members, while punishment
   in the last two rounds before a decision drives members out. With a
   member worth ~16 pool points a round, a point dealt in the decision round
   costs about as much in expected members as it can earn back, while a
   point dealt in the block's first round earns ~0.3 per remaining round
   at almost no member cost.
6. **Drafted the rule.** Punish members below a contribution threshold, in
   proportion to their shortfall (`slope * (thr - c)`), only in the early
   rounds of each 4-round block (a `quiet` window of the last rounds before
   each switch decision is punishment-free), and not in the last `stop`
   rounds of the game, where a punishment can no longer pay back. After
   the last decision (round 19) there are no switches, so the quiet window
   no longer applies there; only `stop` does. Four parameters; I kept out a
   separate first-block scale (the quiet window already covers the first
   decision) and a newcomer grace (newcomers arrive at a block's first
   round, where punishment barely moves switching).
7. **Validated and checked the schedule.** `validate-rule` passes (256
   distinct design points). scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-12/check_schedule.py
   runs the code on a toy game: with thr 10, slope 0.8, quiet 2, stop 3 a
   0-contributor gets 8 and a 5-contributor 4 in rounds 0-1, 4-5, ... 16-17
   and 20, and nothing elsewhere; with quiet 0, stop 1 every round but 23.
   The space contains near-`zero` corners (thr 3 with slope 0.2 floors to
   0; quiet 3 leaves only each block's first round), so the sweep can
   fall back towards `zero` if punishment does not pay.

## Key findings

1. The score is the group's total pool, so members count as much as
   contributions: `zero` beats `ah` 60.5 to 56.0 against `ah`, almost all of
   it from ~0.9 extra members after the first switch (exploration 1).
2. Switching rises with punishment received, in humans and in the sim (sim:
   23% with none in the block, 43% at 10-20, 55% at 20-40), and less so for
   high contributors (exploration 2, 3).
3. Punishment raises low contributors' contributions, and the gain lasts:
   a 1-5 contributor punished 4-8 is +1.5-1.8 over the next 4 rounds vs
   +0.6-0.9 unpunished, flat from t+1 to t+4. Punishing contributors of 11+
   gains nothing (exploration 3, 4).
4. Under `ah`, members who never switch end up ~3.7 points above those
   under `zero` (12.6 vs 8.9), ~2.5 of it treatment rather than selection,
   worth ~4 pool points per member per round against ~1-1.5 punishment
   (exploration 4).
5. The switching cost of punishment depends on when it is dealt: +0.004
   log-odds per point in a block's first round, +0.027-0.031 in its last
   two; group-level punishment in the decision round adds +0.043 per point
   of the group mean (exploration 5).
6. A punishment point earns back roughly 0.3 pool points per remaining
   round, so it stops paying when fewer than ~3-4 rounds remain
   (exploration 4).

## Hypothesis

A good manager uses punishment as an investment in contributions and
avoids paying for it in members. It punishes members who contribute little,
in proportion to how little, because their raised contributions persist for
the rest of the game; it does so early in each 4-round block, and keeps
quiet in the rounds just before a switch decision, when punishment is what
members remember as they decide whether to leave (and what may deter
newcomers). It stops punishing near the end, where a punishment no longer
has the rounds to pay back; after the last switch (round 19) it no longer
needs the quiet window.

The rule: `punishment = slope * max(0, thr - c_t)` for members who
contributed this round, set to 0 in the quiet window and the final rounds.

- `thr` (float, sweep [3, 16]): contribution below which a member is
  punished. I expect 8-12: punishment helps 0-10 contributors and not
  11-15 ones (finding 3); 3 lets the sweep nearly switch punishment off,
  16 lets it press average contributors.
- `slope` (float, sweep [0.2, 3] log): punishment points per point of
  shortfall. I expect 0.4-1, giving a free rider 4-10 points, where the
  persistent response is clear (finding 3); log because its effect is
  multiplicative and the cheap end matters as much as the harsh end.
- `quiet` (int, sweep 0-3): rounds before and including each switch
  decision with no punishment. I expect 2 (finding 5: the last two rounds
  carry the switching cost); 0 and 3 test both ends.
- `stop` (int, sweep 1-6): final rounds with no punishment. I expect 2-4
  (finding 6); round 23 is never worth punishing, so 1 is the floor.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 2 say punishment is paid for in members, the biggest lever
on the pool; findings 3 and 4 say it still raises contributions durably, so
abandoning it (`zero`) leaves value on the table. Finding 5 lets both be had:
the member cost sits in the last rounds of each block, hence `quiet`.
Finding 3 grounds the threshold and the proportional shortfall (low
contributors respond, high ones do not; harder punishment brings bigger
responses), and finding 6 grounds `stop`. What would contradict it: a sweep
whose best points sit at thr 3 or slope 0.2 (punishment never pays, `zero`
is right), or at quiet 0 (timing does not matter in the switch model, my
regression in 5 being confounded by `ah`'s own patterns). Not modelled: the
group's mean contribution or relative norms (an absolute threshold is
simpler and the response tables are by absolute level), newcomer grace and
a separate first-block treatment (newcomers arrive at a block's first round,
where punishment barely moves switching), punishment history of a player
(the persistence table did not show decay needing a refresh schedule), and
timed-out players, who are charged nothing anyway. These causal readings
come from `ah`'s observational sims, not interventions; the sweep is the
test.
