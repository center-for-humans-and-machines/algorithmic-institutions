# pf-20261104-9

## Explorations

1. **How do `ah` and `zero` fare against each other on the stack I face?**
   Looked at the reference sims (per_round.parquet: group pool rebuilt as
   1.6*sum c - sum p per group and round, empty group = 0, group size,
   mean contribution, mean punishment by 4-round phase) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-9/ref_sims.py. Found that `zero` beats
   `ah` clearly: zero's group averages 59.1-61.8 pool per round vs ah's
   44.9-48.1. ah does raise its members' contributions by about 1 point
   (9.3-9.6 vs 7.8-8.3 per member from phase 1 on) but it pays ~3 points per
   member per round in phase 0 and ~1-2 later, and above all it loses
   members: after the first switch zero's group holds ~5.0-5.2 members and
   ah's ~3.8. In zero_vs_zero contributions decay from 8.4 to 7.4 without
   punishment. So the bar is high: "never punish" already wins by ~14 pool
   points per round, and the pool is a group *total*, so headcount is
   worth more than per-member compliance. Next: the human data, to see
   what punishment does to the punished player's next contribution and to
   their switching.
2. **What does punishment do to the punished player's next contribution
   and to their switching (humans)?** Looked at experiments/
   2group_8agent_50ep.csv (one copy per game, 50 games; contribution,
   punishment, group_id next round) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-9/human.py. Found: human managers
   punish by contribution (mean 4.4 at c=0, 0.3 at c 17-20). Next-round
   change in contribution, staying players: c=0 & unpunished +1.0, c=0 &
   punished 10-30 +4.0; c 5-8 unpunished -0.3, punished 10-30 +2.4; high
   contributors (13-20) drop whatever happens and punishing them is a
   disaster (-8.8 at 5-10, -15 at >10). So punishment of low contributors
   buys ~+2-3 points of contribution next round (worth ~3-5 pool points)
   at a cost of 10+ pool points paid now: one round of it does not pay
   for itself unless the lift persists for several rounds. Switching at
   decision rounds: base rate ~0.19 for players with no punishment over
   the last 4 rounds, ~0.37-0.44 with 1-6 average and ~0.6 above 6, almost
   regardless of own contribution. So punishing a player more than doubles
   the chance they leave. That's fine if they're a zero contributor (their
   leaving costs the pool nothing), but bad for a mid contributor.
   Next: does punishing *others* push the unpunished (good) contributors
   out, and what pulls players into a group?
3. **Does punishing others push unpunished members out, and what pulls
   players in?** Looked at the human data aggregated per player and
   4-round phase (own punishment, punishment of others in the group, both
   groups' mean payoff and contribution) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-9/switch_drivers.py. Found: unpunished
   players barely react to punishment of others (switch rate 0.16-0.20 up
   to 10 points per round dealt to others, 0.24 above). What drives moves
   is the comparison of groups: switch rate 0.12 when the other group's
   mean payoff is >1 lower, 0.43-0.54 when it is >1 higher; similarly 0.14
   vs 0.44 for mean contribution 3 lower vs 3 higher. Low contributors
   (mean <=8) switch more (0.33) than high ones (>16: 0.17). So targeted
   punishment of free-riders is not contagious, but every punishment point
   lowers the group's mean payoff, which is what outsiders (and insiders)
   compare. That's how ah loses members to zero. A good manager should
   punish rarely and only where it buys something: the low contributors,
   whose leaving costs the pool little and whose staying-and-improving
   adds to it. Next: does the lift from punishing a low contributor last
   beyond one round? That decides whether punishment pays at all.
4. **Does the lift last, and for whom?** Looked at the human data, players
   staying in their group for the next 3 rounds, change in contribution
   after 1 and 3 rounds by (own c / mean of the other members' c) x
   punishment, with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-9/persistence.py.
   Found: the lift persists. For players at <=0.75 of the others' mean, a
   punishment of 3-8 is followed by +1.8 to +2.5 after one round and +2.0
   to +3.5 after three, against +0.2 to +1.1 unpunished: about +1.5-2.5
   points kept for 3+ rounds, i.e. ~7-12 pool points bought for ~5 paid.
   Punishments above 8 buy little more than 3-8 (except for near-zero
   contributors). For players at 0.75-1.0 of the others' mean the lift is
   small (+0.3-0.6) and a hard punishment backfires (-1.3 after 3 rounds);
   above 1.5x the mean punishment is ruinous. Human managers punish ~3.5
   in rounds 0-3 and ~1-1.3 from round 13 on. Caveat: managers chose whom
   to punish, so this is not clean causation, but it is within-bin and
   matches what the AH contribution model was trained on. So I now think
   punishment pays only as a *moderate*, *targeted* sanction of clear
   laggards relative to their group, scaled to the shortfall and capped,
   and it should stop when there are too few rounds left for the lift to
   repay its cost (a punishment in round 23 buys nothing). Next: draft
   the rule.
5. **Drafted the rule.** Reference = mean contribution this round of the
   other valid members of my group. A member is short by
   `rel * reference - c` when positive; punishment = min(`cap`, `slope` *
   shortfall), zero after round `last_round`, multiplied by `pre_switch`
   in the rounds that precede a switch decision (3, 7, 11, 15, 19). The
   last factor is open in direction: punishing laggards right before a
   decision makes them likelier to leave (costs the pool little) but also
   lowers the group's mean payoff at the moment players compare groups
   (exploration 3); the sweep can tell. Members with no valid other member
   to compare against, and timed-out players, get no punishment. The
   zero manager sits inside the design (slope low, rel low), so the sweep
   can fall back to it if all punishment hurts on this stack.
6. **A snag: the code failed in a fresh process.** Running the rule
   through `run_rule` in a new Python process raised `KeyError:
   '__import__'` on `keepdim=True`, `th.clamp(..., min=)` and comparisons
   with Python scalars. It is torch importing lazily on its first call of
   those paths, which the rule's builtin-free scope blocks. Once torch has
   made those calls, the same code runs (validate-rule passed, and the sim
   runs torch models before any manager). To be safe I removed every
   keyword argument (`unsqueeze` for keepdim, `th.maximum`/`th.minimum`
   against tensors for clamps). replay.py warms torch once.
7. **What does the rule punish on the human games?** Replayed it on the
   50 human games (inputs from the data) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-9/replay.py. At a middle point (rel
   0.7, slope 1, cap 8, last_round 20, pre_switch 1) it punishes 18% of
   member-rounds vs the humans' 30%, 0.61 points per member-round vs
   1.77; 2.5 at c=0, 1.7 at c 1-4, 0.45 at 5-8 and nothing at 13+. Round
   0-3 average 0.96 vs the humans' 3.56. It is much lighter and more
   targeted than the human managers, which is what I wanted. At the harsh
   corner (rel 1.1, slope 4, cap 16, pre_switch 2) it would deal 7.2 per
   member-round and hit 13-20 contributors with 2.7-3.6, which exploration
   2 says backfires. So I narrowed `rel` to [0.2, 1.0]: punishing anyone
   at or above the others' mean has no support in the data (exploration 4:
   above 1.0x it lowers contributions). Validated: 256 distinct points.

## Key findings

1. Never punishing beats `ah` on this stack by ~14 pool points per round
   (59-62 vs 45-48), mostly through headcount: ~5 members vs ~3.8 after
   the first switch, while ah lifts contributions by only ~1 point per
   member (exploration 1).
2. Own punishment more than doubles a player's chance of switching (0.19
   unpunished, ~0.4 at 1-6 points per round, ~0.6 above), and players
   move toward the group with higher mean payoff (0.12 vs 0.54); punishing
   others does not push unpunished members out (0.16-0.24)
   (explorations 2, 3).
3. Punishing a low contributor (<=0.75x the others' mean) by 3-8 lifts
   their contribution by ~1.5-2.5 points over the unpunished, and the lift
   is still there 3 rounds later; larger punishments add little
   (exploration 4).
4. Punishing at- or above-average contributors lowers their contributions
   (-1.3 after 3 rounds at 0.75-1.0x with >8 points, -2 to -6 above 1.5x)
   (explorations 2, 4).
5. Free riders switch more (0.33 for mean c <= 8 vs 0.17 above 16), and a
   leaving free rider costs the pool almost nothing since the pool is a
   group total (explorations 1, 3).

## Hypothesis

A good manager mostly leaves its group alone, so its members' payoffs stay
high and the group keeps and attracts players (the pool is a total, and
headcount is what beats `ah`). It punishes only clear laggards relative to
their own group's current norm, moderately and in proportion to how far
they fall short. That lifts their contributions for several rounds, or
pushes them out, which costs little. It stops punishing near the end, when
too few rounds remain for the lift to repay the points it removes from the
pool.

The rule: shortfall = max(0, `rel` x mean contribution of the other valid
members this round - own contribution); punishment = min(`cap`, `slope` x
shortfall), times `pre_switch` in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, and 0 after
`last_round`.

- `rel` (float, [0.2, 1.0]): where a member counts as a laggard, as a
  fraction of the others' mean. I expect 0.5-0.8.
- `slope` (float, [0.2, 5] log): punishment per point of shortfall. I
  expect ~0.7-2. The low end is close to `zero` (the floor to integers
  wipes out most punishments), and the high end is a harsh punisher.
- `cap` (float, [2, 16]): the most one member is dealt in one round. I
  expect 5-9.
- `last_round` (int, 12..23): the last round with any punishment. I expect
  19-21.
- `pre_switch` (float, [0, 2]): the multiplier in the round before each
  switch decision. Could go either way: below 1 to keep members, above 1
  to push laggards out. Centred on 1.

### Justification

Finding 1 sets the baseline. Any punishment has to beat "none", so the
rule is built to punish little, and the design contains near-zero
punishers (low `slope`, low `rel`). Findings 3 and 4 give the targeting
(`rel` below 1, relative to the group's own norm because the contribution
model herds on it) and the moderate size (`slope` x shortfall with a
`cap`, since 3-8 points lift about as much as more). Finding 3's lift
lasting ~3 rounds gives the arithmetic behind `last_round`: about +2
points x 1.6 x 3 rounds ~ 10 pool points for ~5 paid, which no longer pays
in the last 2-3 rounds. Findings 2 and 5 motivate `pre_switch`. Punishing
just before a decision raises a laggard's exit, which costs little
(finding 5). But it lowers the group's mean payoff exactly when players
compare groups (finding 2). The sign is unclear, so the sweep decides.

What would contradict this: if the sweep's best points sit at the lowest
`slope`/`rel` (i.e. zero is best), then the AH contribution model gives
no lasting lift to punishment and the switch model's exit cost dominates.
If they sit at `rel` = 1.0, a broader sanction pays more than the human
data suggests.

Not modelled: punishment history (repeat offenders, past punishment by
the other manager), newcomers versus incumbents, and the other group's
state. Each would add a parameter with weaker support in the data, and
5 is the maximum. The human estimates are also selection-confounded,
since managers chose whom to punish, so the ranges are kept wide.
