# pf-20261104-1

## Explorations

1. **What is the score and how do the reference managers do on it?** Read
   `simulation/pool_scores.py` and ran scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/ref_scores.py
   on the reference sims. The score is the group's *total* pool
   1.6*sum(c) - sum(p) per round (empty group = 0), so group size counts as
   much as contributions, and every punishment point is a point off the score.
   `zero` beats `ah` in both positions: 59.1 (4.60 members) vs ah's 44.9
   (3.40), and 61.8 vs 48.1 mirrored; ah vs ah gives ~56, zero vs zero ~51.
   Within ah vs ah the pool rises 44 -> 61-66 over the blocks, while zero vs
   zero slides 54 -> 49: punishment does lift contributions over time, but
   against zero it loses members (ah 3.4 vs zero 4.6). So the target is
   zero's member advantage plus some of ah's contribution growth; next I want
   to know what makes artificial humans switch.
2. **What makes artificial humans switch?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/switch_drivers.py on the reference sims: one row
   per player and switch decision, features averaged over the preceding
   4-round block. Base rate 27.5% (31% out of ah groups, 24% out of zero).
   Own punishment is the sharpest personal driver: 0/round -> 23.6% leave,
   2-4 -> 39.5%, 4-8 -> 51%, >8 -> 64%. Group-level: low own-group mean
   contribution (logit -0.12/pt), high other-group contribution (+0.10/pt),
   small group (-0.23/member) and payoff below the other group's (-0.05/pt)
   all push people out. Big groups keep members (6-8 members: 17.6% leave).
   So punishing hard costs members, and members feed on themselves (big
   group -> fewer leave -> bigger). Next: does punishment actually raise the
   punished player's next contribution, and by how much?
3. **Does punishment raise the punished player's next contribution?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/punish_response.py on ah_vs_ah and on the
   human data (one copy per game), change of own contribution to the next
   round (same block) by contribution and punishment bins. In the sim the
   response is small and only at the bottom: a 0-4 contributor rises +2.2
   unpunished vs +2.9 when punished 11+; 5-9 contributors +0.1 vs +1.25.
   Punishing high contributors (15-20) makes them drop more (-1.7 -> -4.2).
   Regression c_next ~ c + others + p: +0.032 per point (humans 0.046),
   with c 0.65 and others' mean 0.30 (herding). Humans react more steeply
   in both directions (a punished 20-contributor drops 5-16). So a point of
   punishment buys well under a point of contribution next round; it only
   pays if the gain persists over many rounds (c and herding make it
   fairly persistent) and is aimed at low contributors only. Punishing
   anyone above ~10 is a pure loss.
4. **Per member, is ah's group better than zero's?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/percap.py on zero_vs_ah. Per member ah's group
   contributes 9.4 vs 7.9 (rounds 4-23), punishing 1-2 per member, so net
   per member 1.6*9.4-1.4 = 13.6 vs zero's 12.6. Ah's members are better
   per head, yet it loses 3.4 vs 4.6 members, and that decides the score.
   Leavers contribute less than stayers (ah: 8.4 vs 10.3), so ah does
   partly select for cooperators, but not enough to make up for size.
   So the goal: a fraction of ah's contribution lift at a much smaller
   switching cost, i.e. little punishment, aimed only at clear free riders.
   Next: what did human managers do, and what worked for them?
5. **What did human managers do, and what worked?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/human_managers.py on the human data. They
   punish by shortfall below the group mean: gap 0-2 -> 1.7 mean (39%
   punished), 2-5 -> 2.7, 5-10 -> 5.5, above-mean contributors ~0.7; heavy
   in round 0 (3.4) then fading to ~1.1. Per game-group, quartiles of mean
   punishment: 0.3/member-round -> pool 55, 4.65 members; 1.2 -> pool 67,
   c 10.1, 4.4 members (best); 2.1 -> 47; 4.8 -> 38, 3.3 members. Across
   the 50 games, the group punishing more has fewer members (corr -0.56).
   Confounded, but it points to a sweet spot of light, targeted
   punishment around 1 point per member-round, not to zero and not to
   ah's ~1.4-3.5. Next: does it matter *when* in a block punishment comes,
   i.e. do switchers weigh the round right before the decision most?
6. **Does the timing of punishment within a block matter for switching?**
   Ran scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/switch_timing.py (ah runs, players who
   stayed in one group for the whole block): logit of switching on own
   punishment in each of the block's 4 rounds. Weights per point: 0.028,
   0.030, 0.045, 0.060 for rounds 1-4 of the block. The round right before
   the decision counts twice as much as the block's first round. So a
   manager should go soft in the round(s) just before a switch decision
   (rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19) and do its disciplining right after one.
   Next: do others' punishments move a player's contribution (a spillover
   that would make punishing one free rider worth more)?
7. **Do group-mates' punishments move a player's contribution?** Ran
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/spillover.py: next contribution on own
   contribution, others' mean contribution, own punishment and others' mean
   punishment. Sim: own +0.021/pt, others' mean +0.032/pt (humans 0.002 and
   0.08). So punishing one free rider by X lifts the group's next-round
   contributions by about 0.021X + 3*0.032X/3 = 0.05X, and with
   contributions persistent (own 0.65 + herding 0.31 ~ 0.96 per round in
   group terms) that can add up to ~0.5X over ten rounds, i.e. ~0.8X pool
   for X spent: about break-even before the cost in members (exploration
   2). Punishment pays, if at all, only early (long horizon), on clear
   free riders, in small doses, and never late in the game.
8. **Drafted the rule.** Punishment = min(cap, slope * max(0, others' mean
   contribution - own contribution - tol)), with others = valid in-group
   members this round; scaled by pre_switch in rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19
   (exploration 6), and switched off after round t_stop (explorations 3,
   7: late punishment has no horizon to pay back, and after round 19 there
   is no switching to win). Relative to the group mean rather than an
   absolute level because humans managed that way (5) and because in a
   group that has collapsed an absolute bar would punish everyone and empty
   the group. The integer floor means slope*gap < 1 punishes nothing, so
   the low end of the slope range reproduces `zero`, which the sweep can
   fall back to if punishment does not pay against this stack.
9. **Does the code run, and how much does it punish across the ranges?**
   The validator passed the first draft, but my own replay script failed:
   in a fresh torch process, scalar-argument calls (`th.clamp(x, min=..)`,
   `keepdim=True`, `t == 3.0`) look up `__import__` once, which the rule's
   empty builtins lack. The validator (and surely the sim) had already
   warmed torch, so it is a latent issue, but I rewrote the code with
   tensor-tensor ops only (relu, maximum, minimum, eq against a tensor) and
   it now runs cold. Then replayed the rule open-loop on the human games
   and the ah_vs_ah sim with scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261104-1/replay.py: slope 0.1
   -> 0.01 points per member-round (practically `zero`); slope 0.3, tol 2,
   cap 4 -> 0.11; slope 0.7, tol 1, cap 8 -> 0.6 (20% of member-rounds
   punished); slope 2, tol 0, cap 15 -> 3.0-3.3 (above ah's 1.9 and the
   humans' 1.7). So the ranges span `zero` to harsher-than-ah, with the
   human sweet spot (~1.2, exploration 5) inside. Correlation with the
   human managers' actual punishments 0.3-0.4, so the shortfall-to-mean
   reading matches how they punished. Hand-checked one state: a member
   contributing 0 while two valid mates give 20 and 10 gets min(6,
   1.0*(15-1)) = 6, halved at a pre-switch round; a timed-out member gets 0.

## Key findings

1. The score is the group's total pool, so group size is decisive: `zero`
   beats `ah` 59.1 vs 44.9 (and 61.8 vs 48.1) mainly by holding 4.6 vs 3.4
   members (exploration 1, 4).
2. Per member, ah's group is better (contribution 9.4 vs 7.9, net 13.6 vs
   12.6 pool per member), so punishment does lift contributions; it just
   costs too many members (exploration 4); ah vs ah contributions rise
   over the game while zero vs zero decline (exploration 1).
3. Own punishment drives leaving: 23.6% leave unpunished, 51% at 4-8 points
   per round, 64% above 8; small groups and low own-group contributions
   also push people out (exploration 2).
4. The round right before a switch decision weighs about twice the block's
   first round in the switch decision (logit 0.060 vs 0.028 per point;
   exploration 6).
5. A point of punishment raises the punished player's next contribution by
   ~0.02-0.03 and group-mates' by ~0.03 per point of their mean punishment;
   with contributions persistent (~0.96 per round) this roughly breaks even
   only over a long horizon, and only on low contributors; punishing
   contributors above ~10 makes them drop (explorations 3, 7).
6. Human managers punished by shortfall below the group mean; groups with
   light punishment (~1.2 per member-round) had the largest pools (67 vs 55
   for the least punishing and 38 for the most; exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager against this stack is nearly `zero`: it keeps its group big
and attractive, because size decides the score and punishment both costs
pool directly and drives members out. What it adds to `zero` is a light,
targeted discipline: it punishes only clear free riders, by how far they
fall below what their group-mates give, with a hard cap; it does this early
in the game, when a lifted contribution norm has many rounds to pay back,
and it stops once the remaining horizon is too short; and it eases off in
the round just before a switch decision, which members weigh most when
deciding to leave.

Rule: punishment_i = min(cap, slope * max(0, mean_mates - c_i - tol)),
times pre_switch at rounds 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, and 0 after round t_stop; 0 for
timed-out members and for a member alone in the group.

- `slope` (float, [0.1, 3.0, log]): punishment points per point of shortfall.
  I expect 0.3-1; log so the near-`zero` end (slope*gap < 1 floors to 0) and
  the harsh end both get sampled.
- `tol` (float, [0, 6]): shortfall left unpunished. I expect 1-4, so that
  only clear free riders are hit.
- `cap` (int, [1, 15]): largest punishment per member and round. I expect
  3-8: above ~8 the leaving rate climbs past 50%.
- `t_stop` (int, [0, 23]): last round punished. I expect 10-19; 23 keeps
  the option of punishing throughout, 0 of punishing only in round 0.
- `pre_switch` (float, [0, 1]): factor on punishment in pre-switch rounds.
  I expect 0-0.5.

### Justification

Findings 1 and 3 are why the rule is lenient and capped: every point dealt is
a point off the score and raises the target's chance to leave, and members
are what `zero` wins with; `cap` bounds the per-person exodus risk, `tol`
spares near-average contributors. Findings 2, 5 and 6 are why it punishes at
all: the per-member lift exists, it is largest for low contributors (so
punish by shortfall below the mates' mean, never the above-average ones) and
human groups with light punishment did best; `slope` sets the dose. Finding
5's horizon argument gives `t_stop`, finding 4 gives `pre_switch`. The
hypothesis would be contradicted if the sweep's peak sits at the slope's low
end with no structure in the other parameters (then any punishment loses
against this stack and the rule collapses to `zero`), or if high `t_stop`
and `pre_switch` near 1 win (timing would not matter). I chose not to model
history (repeat offenders, a player's last punishment), punishment of
newcomers differently from stayers, or any reaction to the other group's
state (e.g. punishing less when the other group is bigger), to keep the
sweep at five well-separated dimensions; reacting to rejoiners or to the
other group's pool is a candidate for a later version. The sims here are
open-loop correlations; the causal answer comes from the sweep.
