# pf-20261105-4

## Explorations

1. **What do the reference sims say about punishing at all?** Looked at
   plots/simulation/29_EXP1_reference/aggregates.csv and per_round.parquet
   (quick inline pandas). Under `zero` the clones start at 11.6 and decay
   steadily to 5.5 by round 23; average payoff 589.3 (sum over 24 rounds of
   valid-player mean). Under `humanlike` contributions climb to ~13.2 and stay
   there, but the manager punishes ~2-3 points per player per round and the
   score is only 550.5. Timeouts ~1.9% in both. So the human style buys
   ~7.7 points of contribution late in the game (worth 0.6*7.7 = 4.6 per
   player-round) but pays ~2*2.3 = 4.6 per player-round in punishment, and
   early it pays before the gain exists. A good rule must get the
   contribution lift for much less punishment. Next: how individual humans
   respond to punishment, by their own contribution level.

2. **How do humans respond next round to punishment, by own contribution?**
   Looked at the pilots (contribution, punishment, player_no_input) with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-4/human_response.py. Mean change in
   contribution next round (dc), by own contribution and punishment bin:
   low contributors (c<=5) go +0.7 unpunished, +1.4 with p 2-5, +3.6 with
   p 5-10, +6.2 with p>10 (n=293); c 6-10: -0.6 / +1.2 / +1.5 / +2.7.
   Contributors at 11-19 barely react (around 0, and -5 for heavy p); full
   contributors (c=20) who get punished collapse by -4 to -12 (small n, but
   it is a stark effect). Unpunished mid/high contributors drift down ~1 per
   round, which is the decay `zero` shows. OLS: dc = -0.39 p + 0.55 p*(c<20)
   + ..., i.e. punishment of a non-full contributor raises next contribution
   by ~0.17 per point, while punishing a full contributor backfires. Human
   managers punish c<=5 at 8.2 on average (69% of the time), but also hit
   c 11-19 at 1.5-2.2 about half the time, and full contributors 0.3. The
   mid-range punishments look like waste. So: punish only clear
   under-contributors, and roughly in proportion to the shortfall; never
   punish 20s. Next: is the response relative to the group (deviation) or
   absolute, and does it persist beyond one round?

3. **Do the clones respond like the humans, and does it last?** Looked at
   both reference runs, per player-round, with
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-4/clone_response.py (dc over 1 and 3
   rounds, by own contribution and punishment). The clones copy the human
   pattern: under humanlike, c<=5 players go +0.57 (1 round) / +0.81 (3
   rounds) unpunished, +2.66 / +3.72 with p 5-10, +4.59 / +6.05 with p>10;
   c 6-10: -0.15/-0.09 unpunished vs +1.56/+2.67 at p 5-10. The effect grows
   over 3 rounds, so it persists. Players at 11-15 gain ~+1 over 3 rounds
   from p 2-10; at 16-20 punishment only lowers them (c=20: -1.2 unpunished,
   -1.7 to -2.7 if punished). By deviation from the others' mean the lift
   is concentrated at dev < -5. Under `zero` half of all player-rounds sit
   at c<=5 (46k of 94k), and unpunished mid/high contributors fall 1.6/round:
   decay is the default. Gain per punishment point is diminishing (p~3.5:
   +1.5 over 3 rounds, p~7.5: +2.9, p~15: +5.2). Accounting: group payoff
   = 80 + 0.6*sum c - 2*sum p per round, so a punishment point costs 2 and a
   contribution point earns 0.6 per round it lasts: p=7.5 buying ~+3 lasting
   ~10 rounds roughly breaks even on its own; the extra must come from the
   others not decaying (conditional cooperation) and from deterrence (fewer
   punishments needed later). Next: spillover onto the other players, and
   the shape of the dose response.

4. **Spillover and dose shape.** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-4/spillover.py
   on the humanlike run. Conditional cooperation is the strongest force:
   unpunished players at c>=10 drop -3.7/round when the others average <=5,
   -1.7 at 5-10, -0.9 at 10-15, -0.4 at 15-20. Punishing others has no
   visible positive spillover (dc -0.6 with no punishment dealt to others,
   -1.1 with >20, but confounded by those groups being low). Dose response:
   c<=5 rises roughly steadily with p (p 1-2: +0.97, 4-6: +1.83, 8-10: +2.95,
   14-20: +4.4, 20-30: +5.1 vs +0.57 unpunished); c 6-10 saturates fast
   (p 1-2 already +0.93 vs -0.15, p 8-10 +1.8, more adds little); c 11-15
   gets +0.7 from any small p and nothing more from larger p. The lift is
   the same across round bands (punished c<=10: +2.4 to +3.3 at every
   stage), so the clones do not tire of it. So the efficient shape is: a
   small "signal" punishment for anyone below a threshold plus a part that
   grows with the shortfall, for the deep free riders. The value of each
   lift comes mostly through the others not decaying, so lifting the lowest
   player early is worth the most. Next: a crude empirical toy simulator to
   check orders of magnitude (break-even, stop round).

5. **Toy simulator: orders of magnitude.** Wrote
   scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-4/toy_sim.py: a linear next-round
   contribution model fitted on both reference runs (own c, others' mean,
   their product, log1p(p) and "any p" terms split by own-c band, resid sd
   3.45, residuals resampled). It reproduces the references roughly: zero
   decays 11.5 -> 6.3 (real 11.6 -> 5.5), score 592 (real 589); a
   humanlike-ish rule (8.2/3.0/2.2/1.5/0.3 by c band) holds ~12 (real 13.2)
   for 528 (real 551), so the toy is, if anything, pessimistic about
   punishment. Grid over p = [c<T]*(a + k*(T-c)) while t < t_end (common
   random numbers): the best is a flat 1-point signal to everyone below
   T=12-14, stopping at round 12-20: 604 vs 591.6 zero. Steep slopes
   (k>=0.5) and big bases lose. That rides on the "any punishment" lift for
   c 6-15 (exploration 4: +1.1 and +0.7 next round from p 1-2), which is
   in the clone tables, not just the fit. The toy has no player types, so
   it cannot say whether heavier punishment of the deep free riders pays
   on the real clones; the sweep must cover k from ~0 up. Next: do repeat
   free riders keep responding (persistent types)?

6. **Do repeat free riders keep responding?** scripts/policy_finder/pf-20261105-4/repeat.py
   on the humanlike run, c<=10 players: next-round lift when punished (p>2)
   vs not is +3.3 vs +0.4 for a first punishment, +2.5 vs +0.4 after 3-5
   earlier ones, +2.1 vs +0.9 after more than 10. So the response fades
   somewhat but never vanishes: no escalation for repeat offenders, and no
   need for a "give up on this player" switch. Under humanlike 17% of
   players still average below 6 (vs 47% under zero), so there is a
   persistently low type whose punished rounds are a sawtooth; heavy
   punishment of them is where cost piles up, which argues for a modest
   slope. Decided against a fifth parameter (cap, escalation, group-
   relative threshold): none had support beyond what the four below cover.

7. **Drafted the rule.** p_i = [c_i < T] * (a + k*(T - c_i)) while t <
   t_end, else 0. T: the contribution below which a player is punished
   (uniform [3, 18]: toy best 12-14, but punishing 16+ hurts in the data,
   and a low T lets the sweep reach near-zero punishment). a: the flat
   signal every under-contributor gets (uniform [0, 6]; toy best ~1,
   values below 1 floor to 0 when k is small, so the zero-like corner is
   reachable). k: points per point of shortfall (uniform [0, 1.5]; toy
   best 0-0.25, but the toy has no types and the clone tables show c<=5
   players keep rising with p up to 20-30, so the range goes well past).
   t_end: first round with no punishment (int [8, 24]; 24 = punish
   throughout; a point of punishment costs 2 and needs a lift lasting
   several rounds at 0.6/round, toy best 12-20).

8. **Validated.** configs/managers/rule_based/pf-20261105-4.yml passes
   validate-rule (exp1, 256 distinct Sobol points). The code multiplies by
   this round's `valid`, so a timed-out player (c read as 0) is never
   counted as a free rider. The manager drops that punishment anyway.

## Key findings

1. Never punishing beats the human style on the clones: 589.3 vs 550.5.
   Humanlike holds contributions at ~13.2 against zero's decay to 5.5, but
   pays about as much in punishment (2*2.3 per player-round) as it gains
   (0.6*7.7) (exploration 1).
2. Group payoff per round = 80 + 0.6*sum c - 2*sum p. A punishment point
   costs 2, and a contribution point earns 0.6 per round it lasts
   (exploration 3).
3. Only under-contributors respond. Clones at c<=5 rise +2.7 next round
   (+3.7 over 3 rounds) with p 5-10, against +0.6 unpunished. Players at
   16-20 only lose from punishment (-1.7 to -2.7 at c=20). Humans show the
   same pattern, and more strongly (-4 to -12 for punished 20s)
   (explorations 2, 3).
4. A small punishment already does much of the work for mid contributors.
   At c 6-10, p 1-2 gives +0.93 vs -0.15, and larger p adds little. At
   c 11-15, any small p gives +0.7 and more p adds nothing. Only the deep
   free riders (c<=5) keep rising with p, up to +5.1 at p 20-30
   (exploration 4).
5. Conditional cooperation drives the decay. Unpunished contributors at
   c>=10 lose 3.7/round when the others average <=5, and only 0.4 when the
   others average 15-20. So lifting the low players early protects
   everyone else (exploration 4).
6. The lift does not wear off over rounds (+2.4 to +3.3 in every round
   band). It fades only mildly with repeated punishment (+3.3 at first,
   +2.1 after more than 10 punishments) (explorations 4, 6).
7. A crude empirical toy model (it reproduces the references within ~1-2
   contribution points) favours a flat ~1-point signal below T=12-14,
   stopping around round 12-20: 604 vs 592 for zero. Steep or large
   punishments lose (exploration 5).

## Hypothesis

A good manager punishes cheaply and narrowly. It hits only players who
contribute clearly less than a decent level. Each of them gets a small,
near-symbolic punishment, plus at most a modest extra that grows with
their shortfall. It never touches high contributors. It stops punishing
once too few rounds remain for a lift to repay the double cost. The point
is to stop the conditional-cooperation decay early, at a cost far below
the human managers' blanket ~2.3 points per player-round.

The rule: `punishment_i = [c_i < T] * (a + k * (T - c_i))` in rounds
`t < t_end`, and 0 afterwards.

- `T`, the contribution threshold below which a player is punished.
  Uniform [3, 18].
- `a`, the flat signal punishment for anyone below T. Uniform [0, 6].
- `k`, the extra points per point of shortfall below T. Uniform [0, 1.5].
- `t_end`, the first round with no punishment (24 means never stop).
  Integer [8, 24].

### Justification

- **Only under-contributors are punished, never the top.** Findings 3
  and 4 show that only under-contributors respond and that punishing the
  top backfires. T's range stops at 18, and I expect T around 10-15.
- **A flat signal term.** Finding 4 shows the "any punishment" lift for
  c 6-15, and finding 7 shows it carries the toy optimum. I expect a
  around 1-2. The range runs to 6 in case the real clones need a firmer
  signal. Below 1 it floors to 0, so the zero-like corner stays
  reachable. That corner matters because zero is a strong baseline
  (finding 1).
- **A slope term.** Finding 4 shows c<=5 players keep responding up to
  p 20-30. The toy has no player types, so it may undervalue this. I
  expect k between 0 and 0.5. The range goes to 1.5 (about 20 points for
  a zero contributor at T=12-14) so the sweep can test it.
- **A stop round.** By finding 2, punishment must buy a lift that lasts
  several rounds. The clones do not anticipate the end (they were trained
  on 8/16-round games). I expect t_end around 14-20. The range is 8-24,
  and 24 lets the sweep reject stopping altogether.
- **What would contradict this.** A sweep optimum with a=0 and large k
  would mean the signal effect in findings 4 and 7 is a confound of who
  the human managers chose to punish. An optimum at T near 3 or k=a=0
  would mean any punishment is a loss on these clones.
- **Not modelled: group-relative thresholds.** The dose tables by
  absolute c were clearer than those by deviation, and an absolute
  threshold also pushes up uniformly low groups.
- **Not modelled: escalation or giving up on repeat free riders.**
  Finding 6 shows the response fades only mildly, so neither is needed.
- **Not modelled: reacting to timeouts.** They are rare (~2%) and cannot
  be punished.
