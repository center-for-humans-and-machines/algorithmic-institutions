The herding copula: groups that drift together

correlated-sampling installed by PR #165 · contribution bay of the gnn machine

Spoon-feeding detected

A setting inside this method was chosen by the analyst rather than measured from the data, and the value chosen is the one that makes the result look best. Nothing here broke a rule and no result is wrong — every choice below is written down openly in the experiment's own log. It is flagged because the same choice was made three times across both stacks, always in the same direction.

The copula needs one number: how long a group's mood lasts. The scale runs from 0 (a brand-new mood every round) to 1 (one mood for the entire game). The estimate came back at 1.16 — off the end of the scale, which is not a possible answer.

The experiment rounded it down to 1, the most persistent setting the scale allows. On its own that is a fair call: when an estimate lands outside the legal range, the nearest legal value is the honest fallback. It only becomes a pattern once you see what happened the next two times the same number came up.

Why the number is so hard to measure at all is a separate story: see “Why the dial cannot be learned”. Read it →

The problem

Real groups talk themselves into generosity or into collapse: over an episode, the two groups' average contributions drift apart. With every member sampled independently, simulated groups stayed glued to the grand average. The group-spread row (CG) was the stack's worst score at 9.81 -- almost ten times the human-vs-human noise ceiling.

The change

The punisher's shared-dice trick, moved to the contribution draw and given memory: the shared group number follows an AR(1) chain with full persistence (phi = 1.0), so it is effectively drawn once and carried through the whole episode. A group that starts lucky stays lucky -- which is what herding looks like from outside. Member histograms are again preserved; the mixing weight was re-estimated for the newer trunk (rho = 0.044) after PR #166 showed calibrations do not transfer between stacks.

The maths, in plain English

  1. Same shared-dice trick as the punisher's severity copula, applied where the contribution (0 to 20) is drawn: each member's roll is a blend of one shared group number and their own private number, and each member's own predicted probabilities stay exactly as the network computed them.
  2. The new ingredient is memory. Instead of a fresh group number every round, next round's number is phi x (last round's number) + sqrt(1 - phi^2) x (a fresh draw). With phi between 0 and 1 the group's mood fades gradually; at the fitted phi = 1.0 it never fades -- one mood per group, drawn at the start of the episode and held for all 24 rounds. That is what makes a lucky group stay lucky.
  3. Fitting uses the real games, replayed with the model watching (the model is always shown the true history, never its own outputs). At every decision, note where the member's actual contribution landed inside the model's predicted distribution for them -- 'you gave more than 80% of what I expected of you' -- a percentile.
  4. Convert each percentile to a bell-curve number z (the standard uniform-to-bell-curve conversion). If members were truly independent, groupmates' z values would be uncorrelated. How strongly they correlate within the same round fixes rho; how much of that correlation survives from one round to the next fixes phi. Both by maximum likelihood on the training half of the data only.
  5. Where it plugs in: between the network's probability output and the dice. The trained network file just carries two extra numbers (rho, phi); a file without them samples independently, exactly as before. When the trunk changed in #179 the dial was re-fitted (rho = 0.044), because #166 showed a dial fitted on one model does not transfer to another.

What it bought

CG 9.81 -> 4.16, plus band upgrades on RCD (how a switcher's contribution converges to the new group) and RCA (round-to-round contribution change); the stack mean fell 1.69 -> 1.29, the largest single mean drop of the campaign.
